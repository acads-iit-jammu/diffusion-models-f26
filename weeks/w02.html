Notes 0: Probability 101 — a refresher

This section is a self-contained refresher on the probability prerequisites of the course: sample spaces, random variables, joint and marginal distributions, conditioning, and expectation. Readers comfortable with these notions may proceed directly to Notes I.

Probability theory is built on two distinct maps: the probability measure, which assigns numbers to events, and the random variable, which assigns numbers to outcomes. Distinguishing the two carefully at the outset prevents most of the common confusions, and this refresher is organized accordingly.

The sample space, events, and the probability measure

A random experiment is modeled by three objects.

  • The sample space \(\Omega\) is the set of all possible outcomes of the experiment, with one element \(\omega \in \Omega\) per complete outcome. For a single die, \(\Omega = \{1, \dots, 6\}\). For two coin tosses, \(\Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}\); note that an outcome specifies the result of the entire experiment, i.e. both tosses. For a point drawn from the interval \([0,1]\), \(\Omega = [0,1]\), an uncountable set.

  • An event is a subset \(A \subseteq \Omega\); events are the objects to which probabilities are assigned. “The die shows an even number” is the event \(\{2, 4, 6\}\); “at least one head” is the event \(\{HH, HT, TH\}\). An event is a collection of outcomes, not an outcome.

  • The probability measure is the first of the two maps:

    \[ P : \text{events} \longrightarrow [0, 1], \]

    required to satisfy Kolmogorov’s axioms: (i) \(P(A) \ge 0\) for every event \(A\);

    1. \(P(\Omega) = 1\); (iii) for mutually exclusive events \(A_1, A_2, \dots\), \(P\big(\bigcup_i A_i\big) = \sum_i P(A_i)\) (countable additivity).

All further rules are consequences of the axioms; two used constantly are \(P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)\) and \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\).

A remark on the continuous case. For \(\Omega = [0,1]\) with the uniform measure, every singleton event \(\{\omega\}\) has probability zero, although some outcome certainly occurs. Probability zero therefore does not mean impossibility; it means a vanishing fraction of the possibilities. This is also the reason \(P\) is defined on events rather than on individual outcomes: in the continuous case, individual outcomes are too small to carry probability.

Random variables

The second map is the random variable. A random variable is a function

\[ X : \Omega \longrightarrow \mathbb{R} \]

that assigns a numerical value to each outcome. For the two-coin experiment with \(X\) the number of heads: \(X(HH) = 2\), \(X(HT) = X(TH) = 1\), \(X(TT) = 0\). Despite the name, \(X\) is a fixed, deterministic function; the randomness resides entirely in which outcome \(\omega\) the experiment produces.

The two maps combine as follows. A statement such as \(\mathbb{P}(X = 1)\) is evaluated by first identifying the event determined by the condition and then applying \(P\) to it:

\[ \mathbb{P}(X = 1) \;=\; P\big(\{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) = 1\}\big) \;=\; P(\{HT, TH\}) = \tfrac{2}{4}. \]

Carrying this out for every value (or set of values) of \(X\) yields the distribution of \(X\), a probability assignment on the real line. Once the distribution is available, the underlying sample space is usually suppressed and one works with the distribution directly; this is standard practice, and the remainder of the course follows it. The present section records what that convention leaves implicit.

A discrete random variable is described by its probability mass function (pmf) \(p(x) = \mathbb{P}(X = x)\), with \(\sum_x p(x) = 1\). A continuous random variable is described by its probability density function (pdf) \(f(x) \ge 0\), with \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\, dx = 1\); probabilities are obtained as areas under the density,

\[ \mathbb{P}(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\, dx . \]

Three properties of densities deserve emphasis.

  • A density value is not a probability and may exceed 1; the Uniform distribution on \([0, \tfrac12]\) has \(f \equiv 2\). Only areas under the density are probabilities.
  • \(\mathbb{P}(X = x) = 0\) for every individual \(x\), by the singleton remark above. The informal reading of a density is \(f(x)\,dx \approx\) the probability of an outcome within \(dx\) of \(x\); densities compare the relative plausibility of neighborhoods.
  • The cumulative distribution function (CDF) \(F(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \le x)\) applies to discrete and continuous variables alike, and its derivative, where it exists, is the pdf. The CDF appeared in Notes II as the sampling map \(F^{-1}\).

Joint distributions

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be two random variables defined on the same sample space — two numerical features of the same outcome. Their joint distribution specifies \(p(x, y) = \mathbb{P}(X = x \text{ and } Y = y)\) in the discrete case, or a joint density \(f(x, y)\) whose volumes are probabilities in the continuous case. The joint distribution is a complete description of the pair: every probabilistic statement about \((X, Y)\) can be computed from it.

The following discrete joint distribution serves as the running example (the state of a morning sky and the occurrence of rain):

\(p(x,y)\) \(Y{=}\) rain \(Y{=}\) no rain row sum
\(X{=}\) cloudy 0.20 0.25 0.45
\(X{=}\) clear 0.05 0.50 0.55
column sum 0.25 0.75 1

Marginal distributions

The marginal distribution of \(X\) describes \(X\) alone, with \(Y\) disregarded. It is obtained by summing (or, for densities, integrating) the joint distribution over the other variable:

\[ p(x) = \sum_y p(x, y), \qquad\text{or}\qquad f(x) = \int f(x, y)\, dy . \]

In the table these are the row and column sums — the margins of the table, which is the origin of the term: \(\mathbb{P}(\text{cloudy}) = 0.45\) and \(\mathbb{P}(\text{rain}) = 0.25\). The operation is justified directly by the additivity axiom: the event “cloudy” is the disjoint union of “cloudy and rain” and “cloudy and no rain”. Marginalization recurs throughout the course; in the mixture model of Notes II, the observed density \(p(x) = \sum_z p(z)\, p(x \mid z)\) is precisely the joint distribution of \((x, z)\) with the latent variable summed out.

Conditional distributions and Bayes’ rule

The conditional distribution of \(Y\) given \(X = x\) is defined, whenever \(p(x) > 0\), by

\[ p(y \mid x) = \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)} . \]

The definition restricts attention to the outcomes consistent with \(X = x\) and renormalizes their probabilities to total 1. In the running example, \(\mathbb{P}(\text{rain} \mid \text{cloudy}) = 0.20 / 0.45 \approx 0.44\), compared with the unconditional \(\mathbb{P}(\text{rain}) = 0.25\).

Applying the definition in both directions and eliminating the joint yields Bayes’ rule,

\[ \boxed{\;p(x \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid x)\, p(x)}{p(y)}\;} \]

which converts a conditional in one direction into the conditional in the other. The tanh denoiser of W01 is an application of this identity, with \(x\) the clean data point and \(y\) the noisy observation.

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if \(p(x, y) = p(x)\, p(y)\) for all \(x, y\) — equivalently, if \(p(y \mid x) = p(y)\), so that conditioning on \(X\) conveys no information about \(Y\). In the table, \(0.20 \ne 0.45 \times 0.25\), so the two variables are dependent. The i.i.d. assumption underlying the likelihoods of Notes I is an independence statement applied to repeated observations.

Expectation

The expectation of a random variable is the probability-weighted average of its values,

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\, p(x) \qquad\text{or}\qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\, f(x)\, dx , \]

the center of mass of the distribution. The expectation need not be an attainable value; a fair die has \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 3.5\). More generally, for a function \(g\),

\[ \mathbb{E}[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\, p(x) \qquad\text{or}\qquad \int g(x)\, f(x)\, dx , \]

so that the distribution of \(g(X)\) itself need never be computed (a fact sometimes called the law of the unconscious statistician).

Two properties carry most of the weight in this course.

  • Linearity. \(\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]\) holds for all random variables on a common sample space, with no independence assumption. (Independence is required for the separate statement \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]\).)
  • Variance. \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - (\mathbb{E}X)^2\) measures spread about the mean, and for independent random variables variances add. The identity \(\operatorname{Var}(x_0 + \sigma z) = 1 + \sigma^2\) of W01 is an instance.

Finally, the conditional expectation \(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y = y]\) is the mean of the conditional distribution of \(X\) given \(Y = y\), regarded as a function of \(y\). It is the minimum-mean-squared-error prediction of \(X\) from the observation \(Y = y\) (proved in the setting of Notes I), and the denoiser \(\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_\sigma]\) of W01 is precisely a conditional expectation.

Exercises

  2. For the sky-and-rain table, compute \(\mathbb{P}(\text{cloudy} \mid \text{rain})\) by Bayes’ rule from \(\mathbb{P}(\text{rain} \mid \text{cloudy})\) and the marginals, and confirm the result by direct computation from the table.
  3. Exhibit (a) a pdf exceeding 3 at some point; (b) a random variable whose expectation is not an attainable value; (c) two dependent random variables for which \(\mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]\) nevertheless holds, illustrating that linearity requires no independence.
  4. Let \(U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\) and \(X = U^2\). Compute \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) twice: directly via \(\mathbb{E}[g(U)]\), and by first deriving the CDF and pdf of \(X\). Compare the effort required.

Notes I: Distributions and Maximum Likelihood

Companion note to W02, lecture 1. Interactive versions: the Distribution Playground and the Likelihood Lens.

This note walks one loop twice: distribution → samples → fitted distribution → new samples. First visually (the playground), then analytically (maximum likelihood for the Gaussian, derived in full). It doubles as a compact reference for the distribution families used throughout the course.

The cast of distributions

Family pdf \(f(x)\) Support Mean Variance
Uniform\((a,b)\) \(\frac{1}{b-a}\) \([a,b]\) \(\frac{a+b}{2}\) \(\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}\)
Normal\((\mu,\sigma^2)\) \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}\) \(\mathbb{R}\) \(\mu\) \(\sigma^2\)
Gamma\((k,\theta)\) \(\frac{x^{k-1} e^{-x/\theta}}{\Gamma(k)\,\theta^{k}}\) \((0,\infty)\) \(k\theta\) \(k\theta^2\)
Beta\((\alpha,\beta)\) \(\frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\) \((0,1)\) \(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\) \(\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}\)
Mixture \(\sum_i \pi_i\, f_i(x)\) union \(\sum_i \pi_i \mu_i\) see Exercise 5

A sentence of habitat for each: the Uniform encodes total ignorance on an interval; the Normal is what sums of many small independent effects look like (W01’s warm-up demo is this statement); the Gamma models positive, right-skewed quantities such as waiting times; the Beta lives on \((0,1)\) and models proportions and probabilities; a mixture models data with subpopulations — clusters — and is the recurring character of this course.

How sampling actually works

Every sampler in the playground reduces to uniform random numbers:

  • Inverse CDF. If \(F\) is a CDF and \(U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\), then \(X = F^{-1}(U)\) has CDF \(F\) (proved in the companion note Generative AI, in Its Simplest Form (Notes II, below)). This handles Uniform (\(x = a + (b-a)u\)) and anything with a computable \(F^{-1}\).
  • Box–Muller for the Normal: with \(U_1, U_2\) independent uniforms, \(\sqrt{-2\log U_1}\,\cos(2\pi U_2)\) is exactly \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). This is the sampler inside every demo of this course.
  • Pick-then-sample for mixtures: draw the component index with probabilities \(\pi_i\), then sample that component — ancestral sampling, the subject of the companion note.
  • Gamma and Beta use clever rejection samplers (the playground uses Marsaglia–Tsang); we treat them as black boxes.

Likelihood

Fix a family \(f(x;\theta)\) and observe an i.i.d. dataset \(x_1, \dots, x_n\). The likelihood and log-likelihood are

\[ L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i; \theta), \qquad \ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(x_i; \theta), \]

and the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) is \(\hat\theta = \arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta)\). The log changes nothing about the argmax (monotonicity) and everything about the algebra and the numerics: a product of 3000 numbers of size \(10^{-2}\) underflows; a sum of their logs is a perfectly pleasant \(\approx -13{,}800\).

PDF vs likelihood: the same function, two readings

The chronic confusion, settled: \(f(x;\theta)\) is one formula with two arguments.

Fix Vary You get Area under the curve
parameters \(\theta\) data \(x\) the pdf — a distribution over data \(= 1\), always
observed data \(x\) parameters \(\theta\) the likelihood — a score for fitting meaningless

The Likelihood Lens shows both as slices of the same surface \(f(x;\mu) = \mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \sigma^2)\): the horizontal slice (fix \(\mu\)) is a density in \(x\); the vertical slice (fix \(x\)) is a likelihood in \(\mu\). For the Gaussian the two slices happen to have the same bell shape, because \(f(x;\mu) = f(\mu;x)\) — a symmetry of this family, not a general fact (repeat the exercise mentally with a Gamma in \(\theta\)). What is general: the likelihood is not a probability distribution over \(\theta\) — it need not integrate to 1, and only its argmax and local shape carry meaning.

The Gaussian MLE, in full

\[ \ell(\mu, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2 . \]

Maximize over \(\mu\). Only the second term involves \(\mu\):

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \mu} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu) = \frac{n}{\sigma^2}(\bar{x} - \mu) \;\stackrel{!}{=}\; 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ \hat\mu = \bar{x}\ } \]

for every \(\sigma\) — which is why, in the Lens, changing \(\sigma\) sharpens the log-likelihood parabola but never moves its peak.

Maximize over \(\sigma^2\) (treat \(v = \sigma^2\) as the variable):

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial v} = -\frac{n}{2v} + \frac{1}{2v^2}\sum_i (x_i - \hat\mu)^2 \;\stackrel{!}{=}\; 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2\ } \]

(One should check this is a maximum, not merely critical: \(\ell \to -\infty\) as \(v \to 0^+\) and as \(v \to \infty\), and this is the only critical point.) Note the divisor \(n\): the MLE of the variance is biased — \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2\) — which is where the familiar \(n-1\) estimator comes from. For this course the distinction is a footnote; the MLE is what the theory produces.

Fitting the other families — honestly

  • Uniform. The likelihood is \((b-a)^{-n}\) if all points lie in \([a,b]\) and \(0\) otherwise: to maximize, squeeze the interval as tight as the data allows — \(\hat a = \min_i x_i\), \(\hat b = \max_i x_i\). (No calculus; the maximum is on the boundary of the constraint. That is Exercise 2.)

  • Gamma and Beta have no closed-form MLE. Setting the derivatives to zero produces equations involving the digamma function, solvable only numerically. The playground therefore uses the method of moments (MoM): equate the family’s mean and variance to the sample’s \(\bar x\) and \(s^2\) and solve —

    \[ \text{Gamma: } \hat k = \frac{\bar{x}^2}{s^2},\ \ \hat\theta = \frac{s^2}{\bar{x}}; \qquad \text{Beta: } c = \frac{\bar{x}(1-\bar{x})}{s^2} - 1,\ \ \hat\alpha = \bar{x}\,c,\ \ \hat\beta = (1-\bar{x})\,c . \]

    MoM is a respectable estimator in its own right (consistent, and a standard warm start for numerical MLE); the honest label matters because “just maximize the likelihood” quietly assumes machinery we haven’t built.

  • Mixtures. Even MoM gets awkward; the right tool is expectation–maximization (EM), which belongs to a later conversation. Today the mixture plays a different role — see below.

The trap: right method, wrong family

In the playground: generate 3000 samples from the two-bump mixture, then fit a single Normal by maximum likelihood. The fit is optimal within its family — and its peak sits exactly in the empty valley between the clusters, because the best single Gaussian matches the overall mean and variance, not the shape.

Two lessons. First, MLE optimizes within the family you chose; the choice of family is a modeling decision the likelihood cannot rescue. Second, we currently have no number that says how wrong this fit is. Constructing that number — the KL divergence, and its identity with maximum likelihood — is the next lecture.

Exercises

  1. Re-derive \(\hat\sigma^2\) for the Gaussian, including the argument that the critical point is a maximum.
  2. Show that the Uniform MLE is \((\hat a, \hat b) = (\min_i x_i, \max_i x_i)\), and explain why calculus alone would miss it.
  3. Derive the Gamma method-of-moments estimators from \(\mathbb{E}X = k\theta\) and \(\operatorname{Var} X = k\theta^2\).
  4. For a single observation \(x\) from \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma\) known, sketch the likelihood in \(\mu\). Now hold \(\mu\) known and sketch the likelihood in \(\sigma\) — is it bell-shaped? Symmetric? (This kills the “likelihood looks like a pdf” illusion outside the Gaussian-mean case.)
  5. For a mixture \(\sum_i \pi_i f_i\) with component means \(\mu_i\) and variances \(\sigma_i^2\), show \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \sum_i \pi_i \sigma_i^2 + \sum_i \pi_i (\mu_i - \bar\mu)^2\) with \(\bar\mu = \sum_i \pi_i \mu_i\) — within-component plus between-component spread. Use it to predict what \(\hat\sigma\) the single-Gaussian fit in the trap will report.

Notes II: Generative AI, in Its Simplest Form

Companion note to W02, lecture 2. Interactive version: Sampling as a Map.

This note presents the same tiny generator twice — first as a hierarchy you sample conditionally (a Bayesian model), then as a deterministic map applied to uniform randomness — and then makes the claim that carries the rest of the course: every modern generative model, diffusion included, is the second picture with a learned map.

View 1: conditional generation (a hierarchical model)

The simplest latent-variable model:

\[ z \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\pi), \qquad x \mid z \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\mu_z,\, \sigma^2), \qquad z \in \{1, 2\}. \]

Ancestral sampling generates by following the arrows: draw the latent coin \(z\), then draw \(x\) from the selected component. The observed marginal is obtained by summing out the latent:

\[ p(x) \;=\; \sum_{z} p(z)\, p(x \mid z) \;=\; \pi\, \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_1, \sigma^2) + (1-\pi)\, \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_2, \sigma^2) \]

— exactly the two-component mixture from the playground. A mixture is not just a formula; it is a generative story: population \(\to\) subpopulation \(\to\) individual.

Inference is the same hierarchy, run backwards. Given an observed \(x\), Bayes’ rule gives the posterior over the hidden coin:

\[ \mathbb{P}(z = 1 \mid x) = \frac{\pi\, \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_1, \sigma^2)} {\pi\, \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_1, \sigma^2) + (1-\pi)\, \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_2, \sigma^2)} . \]

For \(\mu_{1,2} = \mp 1\) and \(\pi = \tfrac12\) this is the sigmoid from W01, and the posterior mean of \(\mu_z\) is the tanh denoiser: W01’s denoising step was latent-variable inference all along. Generation and inference are the two directions of one arrow.

View 2: the coin disappears into a map

Theorem (inverse-CDF / probability integral transform). Let \(F\) be a CDF with generalized inverse \(F^{-1}(u) = \inf\{x : F(x) \ge u\}\), and let \(U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\). Then \(X = F^{-1}(U)\) has CDF \(F\).

Proof. \(\mathbb{P}(F^{-1}(U) \le x) = \mathbb{P}(U \le F(x)) = F(x)\), since \(F^{-1}(u) \le x \iff u \le F(x)\) by monotonicity. \(\square\)

For our mixture, \(F(x) = \pi\,\Phi\!\big(\tfrac{x-\mu_1}{\sigma}\big) + (1-\pi)\,\Phi\!\big(\tfrac{x-\mu_2}{\sigma}\big)\) is continuous and strictly increasing, so \(F^{-1}\) is an honest function, and

\[ u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1), \qquad x = F^{-1}(u) \]

generates the mixture with no coin anywhere. Where did the coin go? Into the uniform: since \(F(x) \le \pi\) precisely on the lower component’s territory, the event \(u < \pi\) is the event \(z = 1\). One uniform number carries both the component choice and the position within the component — randomness in, structure out, everything in between deterministic.

The two-point limit

Send \(\sigma \to 0\) with \(\mu_{1,2} = \mp 1\). The CDF collapses to a staircase: a jump of height \(\pi\) at \(-1\), a flat plateau at height \(\pi\) across the empty middle, a jump to \(1\) at \(+1\). The map becomes

\[ x = \begin{cases} -1, & u < \pi \\ +1, & u > \pi \end{cases} \qquad\text{i.e.}\quad x = \operatorname{sign}(u - \pi), \]

W01’s dataset, generated by thresholding a uniform. The plateau is the geometric image of the empty region: no value of \(u\) lands there. (In the demo, slide \(\sigma\) down and watch the staircase form.)

Maps are not unique

\(F^{-1}\) is the monotone map pushing \(\text{Uniform}(0,1)\) to \(p\), but not the only map. The “packed” two-stage construction

\[ x = \begin{cases} \mu_1 + \sigma\, \Phi^{-1}\!\big(u/\pi\big), & u < \pi,\\[2pt] \mu_2 + \sigma\, \Phi^{-1}\!\big(\tfrac{u-\pi}{1-\pi}\big), & u \ge \pi, \end{cases} \]

also pushes uniform to the mixture (each branch rescales its segment of \([0,1]\) back to a full uniform — Exercise 3), and it is literally ancestral sampling written as one formula. Different map, same distribution: what is canonical is the distribution, not the transport. (In higher dimensions, choosing among the many maps is its own subject — optimal transport — which we will brush against later.)

The claim that organizes everything

A generative model is a deterministic map applied to simple randomness.

Model Simple noise The map How obtained
This note \(u \sim \text{U}(0,1)\) \(F^{-1}\), analytic the density is known
GAN \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) neural network adversarial training
VAE \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) neural network variational inference
Normalizing flow \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) invertible network exact likelihood
Diffusion \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) reverse ODE/SDE learned denoiser

In one dimension with a known density, the map is a formula. For images, neither the density nor the CDF exists in usable form — so deep generative models learn the map. And you have already run one: W01’s probability-flow ODE deterministically transported \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1+\sigma_{\max}^2)\) to the two-point data — an \(F^{-1}\) built not from the CDF but from the score, which is exactly the object a neural network can learn in high dimension. That construction, done properly, is Modules 4–6.
