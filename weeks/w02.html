Notes — Bayes’ rule, and why inference is the inverse problem

Generation runs forward; inference runs backward

Almost every model in this course is written in the forward direction. You posit some unobserved state, and you write down a mechanism that turns it into data:

\[ \underbrace{z}_{\text{latent, what we care about}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{x}_{\text{observed, what we get}} , \qquad \text{specified by } p(x \mid z). \]

Writing \(p(x \mid z)\) is usually the easy half. It is a mechanism — a simulator, a physical story, a noise model — and you can often just state it.

But that is rarely what you want. You do not observe \(z\); you observe \(x\), and you want to reason back to the cause. That is the inverse problem:

\[ \underbrace{x}_{\text{observed}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{z}_{\text{inferred}}, \qquad \text{wanted: } p(z \mid x). \]

Forward Inverse
Direction cause \(\to\) effect effect \(\to\) cause
Object \(p(x \mid z)\) \(p(z \mid x)\)
Typical status given, by modelling wanted, must be computed
Names generative model, sampling distribution, simulator inference, inversion, the posterior

You cannot simply invert the arrow, because the forward map is generally many-to-one: different latent states can produce the very same observation. Knowing \(x\) therefore does not determine \(z\) — it only makes some values of \(z\) more plausible than others. Something extra is needed to break the tie, and that something is what you believed about \(z\) before looking. Bayes’ rule is exactly the bookkeeping that combines the two:

\[ \underbrace{p(z \mid x)}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{p(x \mid z)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{p(z)}^{\text{prior}}} {\underbrace{p(x)}_{\text{evidence}}}, \qquad p(x) = \sum_z p(x \mid z)\,p(z). \]

This is the single most useful thing in the course’s toolkit: it is the bridge from the direction you can write down to the direction you want.

A toy example: did the student understand “or”?

A student is set ten short questions on the logical connective or. We would like to know whether they have actually grasped it — but “understanding” is not something we can measure. We can only mark the answers.

  • Latent \(z \in \{0, 1\}\): the student understands the concept, or does not. This is the inferential goal.
  • Observable \(x \in \{0, 1, \dots, 10\}\): the number of questions correct. This is all we ever see.

Now the forward model — the sampling distribution that says how marks are produced given the latent state. A student who understands still slips occasionally; one who does not can still get items right by guessing or by luck in which items came up. Take each item as independent with

\[ x \mid z = 1 \;\sim\; \text{Binomial}(10,\ 0.9), \qquad x \mid z = 0 \;\sim\; \text{Binomial}(10,\ 0.4). \]

The marks scale does not matter

Nothing above depends on marks running \(0\) to \(10\). The same structure works for a single right/wrong item (\(x \in \{0,1\}\), a Bernoulli), or a percentage score \(0\)–\(100\), or a graded rubric. All that changes is the count distribution you put on the observable. What stays fixed is the shape of the problem: a latent you want, an observable you get, and a sampling distribution connecting them.

Suppose the student scores \(x = 7\). Take a neutral prior \(p(z=1) = 0.5\). Then

\[ \frac{p(z=1 \mid x=7)}{p(z=0 \mid x=7)} = \underbrace{\frac{\binom{10}{7} 0.9^{7} 0.1^{3}}{\binom{10}{7} 0.4^{7} 0.6^{3}}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}} \times \underbrace{\frac{0.5}{0.5}}_{\text{prior odds}} = \Big(\tfrac{0.9}{0.4}\Big)^{7}\Big(\tfrac{0.1}{0.6}\Big)^{3} = 1.352 , \]

so \(p(z = 1 \mid x = 7) = 1.352 / 2.352 = \mathbf{0.575}\).

Two things are worth noticing. The binomial coefficient \(\binom{10}{7}\) appears in both the numerator and the denominator and cancels — it describes how many ways the marks could have been arranged, which is the same under either hypothesis, so it carries no evidence. And \(7/10\), which sounds like a decent score, is barely informative here: it moves us from \(50\%\) to \(58\%\).

Across the range of possible scores:

Score \(x\) 3 5 7 9 10
\(p(z = 1 \mid x)\) 0.000 0.007 0.575 0.996 1.000

The posterior is not a smooth reflection of the mark — it is nearly flat at the extremes and swings sharply in the middle, where the two hypotheses actually disagree.

And the prior genuinely matters. At the same score \(x = 7\):

Prior \(p(z=1)\) 0.2 0.5 0.8
Posterior \(p(z=1 \mid x=7)\) 0.253 0.575 0.844

Same evidence, three different conclusions. This is not a defect. It is the honest statement that seven out of ten is weak evidence, and weak evidence does not overturn what you already knew.

The sampling distribution is the likelihood

The expression \(p(x \mid z)\) has been doing two jobs above, and it is worth separating them, because this is the distinction Distributions and Maximum Likelihood makes in the parametric setting.

Hold fixed Vary You get Sums to 1?
the latent \(z\) the data \(x\) the sampling distribution — how marks come out yes, over \(x\)
the observed data \(x\) the latent \(z\) the likelihood — how well each \(z\) explains what we saw no

It is one formula read in two directions. Forward, it generates. Backward, it scores. Bayes’ rule is what converts the backward reading into an actual probability distribution over \(z\), by weighting with the prior and renormalising.

Bayesian Knowledge Tracing

The toy example freezes the student in time. Real learners learn, so the latent state changes as they work — and the natural extension is exactly the model used in intelligent tutoring systems: Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (Corbett and Anderson, 1995, User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction).

BKT keeps the same two-layer structure and lets the latent evolve:

  • a hidden binary skill state \(z_t \in \{\text{not known}, \text{known}\}\);
  • an observed response \(x_t \in \{\text{incorrect}, \text{correct}\}\) at each opportunity \(t\).

Four parameters specify it, and each is a conditional probability:

Parameter Meaning
\(P(L_0)\) probability the skill is known before any practice — the prior
\(P(T)\) probability of learning it at an opportunity, if not yet known — the transition
\(P(G)\) probability of answering correctly without knowing it — the guess
\(P(S)\) probability of answering incorrectly while knowing it — the slip

So the emission model is \(P(\text{correct} \mid \text{known}) = 1 - P(S)\) and \(P(\text{correct} \mid \text{not known}) = P(G)\). Standard BKT assumes no forgetting.

Tracing a student is then a two-step recursion, repeated per question — and the first step is precisely Bayes’ rule:

  1. Update on the evidence. With \(L\) the current probability the skill is known, a correct answer gives \[ L^{+} \;=\; \frac{L\,(1 - P(S))}{L\,(1 - P(S)) + (1-L)\,P(G)} , \] and an incorrect answer gives \(L^{-} = L\,P(S) \,/\, [\,L\,P(S) + (1-L)(1 - P(G))\,]\).
  2. Predict forward through learning. \(L \leftarrow L^{\pm} + (1 - L^{\pm})\,P(T)\).

For instance with \(L = 0.4\), \(P(S) = 0.10\), \(P(G) = 0.25\), \(P(T) = 0.20\), a correct answer raises the belief to \(0.36 / 0.51 = 0.706\), and the learning step then carries it to \(0.706 + 0.294 \times 0.20 = 0.765\) before the next question. An incorrect answer instead drops it to \(0.04/0.49 = 0.082\).

The shape to remember

A latent state you care about, an observable you actually get, a forward model connecting them, and Bayes running the inference backwards — repeated over a sequence. That is BKT, and it is also a hidden Markov model, a Kalman filter, and, as the next few weeks will show, a diffusion model.

Why this is the shape of the whole course

Diffusion models are an inverse problem, and it is worth being blunt about the terminology, because it inverts what you might expect.

  • The forward process of a diffusion model is the corruption: \(x_0 \to x_\sigma = x_0 + \sigma z\). We write it down ourselves, in closed form, and it is trivial to sample. This is the easy direction.
  • Generating an image means going the other way — recovering a plausible clean \(x_0\) from a noisy \(x_\sigma\). The clean signal is the latent; the noisy observation is the data.

So in a diffusion model, generation is the inference problem. That is why the central object of the course is a posterior, and why the optimal denoiser is a posterior mean:

\[ \mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_\sigma] = \tanh\!\Big(\frac{x_\sigma}{\sigma^2}\Big) \quad\text{for the two-point dataset of } \] W01 — which, as Joint, Marginal, Conditional shows, is nothing but Bayes’ rule applied to two Gaussian shelves and then averaged. Swap “understands the concept” for “was a \(+1\)”, and “ten marks” for “one noisy measurement”, and it is the same calculation you did above.
