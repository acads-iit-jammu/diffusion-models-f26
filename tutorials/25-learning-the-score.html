Learning the Score: VP Diffusion from First Principles

NoteTutorial 1 of 4 · Learning

Question: How can adding noise give us training targets for an unknown density?

Prerequisites: derivatives, second-order Taylor expansion, elementary ODEs, Gaussian distributions, independence, and Bayes’ rule. No SDE or stochastic calculus background is assumed. Conditional expectation is introduced below.

Core route: random steps → VP corruption → conditional averaging → score learning → denoising. The stochastic-integral subsection is optional.

Next: Probability-Flow Sampling. The series guide lists the full reading sequence.

What are we trying to learn?

The score is a derivative in data space

A density \(p(x)\) describes a population. Its score is

\[s(x)=\nabla_x\log p(x).\]

In one dimension this is an ordinary derivative. In several dimensions, \(\nabla_x\) collects one partial derivative for each coordinate, so the score is a vector. It points in the direction of fastest increase of log density. For \(p=\mathcal N(\mu,a^2)\),

\[\log p(x)=C-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2a^2},\qquad s(x)=-\frac{x-\mu}{a^2}.\]

We want a network \(s_\theta(x,t)\) that approximates the score \(s_t(x)\) of a noisy-data density \(p_t\). The derivative is with respect to the data coordinate \(x\). It is different from the training gradient \(\nabla_\theta\mathcal L\) and from a particle’s velocity \(dx/dt\).

A convention for random quantities

\(X_t\) denotes a random variable: the possible outcomes at time \(t\). A lowercase \(x\) is a particular value. One run gives a trajectory \(x_t\); many independent runs give a cloud whose histogram approximates \(p_t\).

Forward time \(t\) runs from data to noise. We use \(\epsilon\) for the noise in a single corruption observation, and fresh \(\xi_k\) for successive simulation steps. Every step size \(h\) is positive; the direction of time is stated separately.

From Gaussian random steps to an SDE

Why the random step has size square root of h

Draw independent \(\xi_k\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and form

\[W^{(h)}_{kh}=\sqrt h\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\xi_j.\]

Since independent variances add, at \(t=kh\) this random walk has variance \(kh=t\). Halving the step size doubles the number of steps and halves each increment’s variance, preserving the total spread. If we used \(h\xi_j\) instead, the total variance would be \(kh^2=th\), which vanishes as \(h\to0\).

Brownian motion \(W_t\) is the continuous-time random-motion model obtained in the appropriate limit. It starts at zero, has continuous paths, and has independent Gaussian increments:

\[W_{t+h}-W_t\sim\mathcal N(0,h).\]

In \(d\) dimensions, use independent copies in each coordinate; the increment covariance is \(hI\). Brownian paths are not differentiable like ODE trajectories. We describe their increments rather than assign them an ordinary velocity.

Euler–Maruyama gives an SDE computational meaning

An ODE \(\dot x=f(x,t)\) can be approximated by Euler: \(x_{k+1}=x_k+h f(x_k,t_k)\). Add a random step to obtain

\[\boxed{\widehat X_{k+1}=\widehat X_k+h f(\widehat X_k,t_k) +g(t_k)\sqrt h\,\xi_k.}\]

This is Euler–Maruyama. The hat distinguishes the numerical approximation from the continuous-time process. Draw a new independent \(\xi_k\) at every step. The corresponding stochastic differential equation (SDE) is written

\[\boxed{dX_t=f(X_t,t)\,dt+g(t)\,dW_t.}\]

Under suitable regularity, smaller numerical steps approximate that process. The SDE notation records deterministic drift \(f\) and random spreading with amplitude \(g\). It is not an instruction to differentiate \(W_t\) ordinarily. We use scalar \(g(t)\) depending only on time throughout this series.

One step you can calculate. From \(x=2\), let \(f(x)=-x/2\), \(g=1\), and \(h=0.01\). Euler–Maruyama gives \(1.99+0.1\xi\), a Gaussian with mean \(1.99\) and variance \(0.01\). The drift is fixed given the current position; the noise changes from run to run. A finite Euler–Maruyama step is generally not the exact SDE transition.

Derivation: the expected Taylor step

We can calculate how averages change using ordinary Taylor expansion. In one dimension, condition on \(X_t=x\) and use the small-step increment \(\Delta X=hf(x,t)+g(t)\sqrt h\,\xi\). For a smooth function \(\varphi\),

\[\varphi(x+\Delta X)=\varphi(x)+\varphi'(x)\Delta X +\tfrac12\varphi''(x)(\Delta X)^2+\text{higher-order terms}.\]

Because \(\mathbb E\xi=0\) and \(\mathbb E\xi^2=1\),

\[\mathbb E[\Delta X\mid X_t=x]=hf, \qquad\mathbb E[(\Delta X)^2\mid X_t=x]=g^2h+f^2h^2.\]

Divide the expected change by \(h\) and let \(h\to0\). Under smoothness and moment conditions that justify the limit and averaging, this gives

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\varphi(X_t)] =\mathbb E\left[f(X_t,t)\varphi'(X_t) +\tfrac12g(t)^2\varphi''(X_t)\right].}\]

The last term is the Itô correction. A deterministic increment is of size \(h\), whose square is negligible after dividing by \(h\). A random increment is of size \(\sqrt h\), whose square contributes at order \(h\). That is why ordinary first-order chain-rule reasoning misses the change in variance.

Check: if \(f=0\), \(g=1\), and \(\varphi(x)=x^2\), the formula gives \(d\mathbb E[X_t^2]/dt=1\). Starting from zero, \(\mathbb E[X_t^2]=t\), exactly as in the Gaussian random walk.

Construct the variance-preserving corruption law

Choose a balanced drift and noise rate

Choose a nonnegative schedule \(\beta(t)\) and set

\[f(x,t)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)x,\qquad g(t)=\sqrt{\beta(t)}.\]

This defines variance-preserving (VP) diffusion:

\[dX_t=-\tfrac12\beta(t)X_t\,dt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dW_t.\]

The drift shrinks the cloud; the random steps spread it. We will show that this particular balance preserves unit variance. It is a design choice, not a rule that every diffusion must follow. Assume \(X_0\) has finite second moment and is independent of the Brownian increments.

Derivation: the mean determines the signal coefficient

Set \(\varphi(x)=x\) in the expected Taylor formula. With \(m_t=\mathbb E[X_t]\),

\[\dot m_t=-\tfrac12\beta(t)m_t,\qquad m_t=\alpha_t m_0,\qquad \boxed{\alpha_t=\exp\left[-\tfrac12\int_0^t\beta(r)\,dr\right].}\]

Thus \(\dot\alpha_t/\alpha_t=-\beta(t)/2\). The same contraction factor applies to a fixed initial point before accounting for the added noise.

For constant \(\beta\), \(\alpha_t=e^{-\beta t/2}\). For a linear schedule on \(0\le t\le1\),

\[\beta(t)=\beta_{\min}+t(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min}),\qquad \log\alpha_t=-\tfrac12\beta_{\min}t -\tfrac14(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})t^2.\]

The logarithm of the signal coefficient is quadratic in time. For example, \(\beta_{\min}=0.1\), \(\beta_{\max}=20\) gives \(\alpha_1=e^{-5.025}\approx0.00657\): only about \(4.32\times10^{-5}\) of the original signal variance coefficient remains.

Derivation: second moment and variance are different

Let \(M_2(t)=\mathbb E[X_t^2]\). Set \(\varphi(x)=x^2\) in the expected Taylor formula:

\[\dot M_2(t)=\mathbb E[-\beta(t)X_t^2+\beta(t)] =\beta(t)[1-M_2(t)].\]

Variance is \(V_t=M_2(t)-m_t^2\). Differentiating and substituting \(\dot m_t\) gives

\[\dot V_t=\beta(t)(1-V_t),\qquad \boxed{V_t=\alpha_t^2V_0+(1-\alpha_t^2).}\]

One way to solve is to set \(R_t=V_t-1\); then \(\dot R_t=-\beta(t)R_t\). Unit initial variance stays one, even when the mean is nonzero. Other initial variances move toward one as the signal coefficient decays.

Worked check: if \(X_0\sim\mathcal N(3,1)\), then

\[\mathbb E[X_t]=3\alpha_t,\qquad V_t=1, \qquad\mathbb E[X_t^2]=1+9\alpha_t^2.\]

Only the variance is preserved; neither the mean nor the second moment is constant.

Derivation: why the conditional distribution is Gaussian

Fix \(X_0=x_0\). The VP numerical update is linear:

\[\widehat X_{k+1}=a_k\widehat X_k+b_k\xi_k, \qquad a_k=1-\tfrac12\beta(t_k)h,\quad b_k=\sqrt{\beta(t_k)h}.\]

After two steps,

\[\widehat X_2=a_1a_0x_0+a_1b_0\xi_0+b_1\xi_1.\]

After any number of steps it remains a fixed multiple of \(x_0\) plus a weighted sum of independent Gaussians. The weights are deterministic because \(\beta\) is prescribed. It is therefore Gaussian at every grid time. As \(h\to0\), the mean tends to \(\alpha_t x_0\); the variance tends to the solution above with \(V_0=0\), since we fixed the initial value. Define

\[\boxed{\sigma_t^2=1-\alpha_t^2.}\]

The limiting Gaussian has that mean and variance. Coordinatewise in \(d\) dimensions,

\[\boxed{X_t\mid X_0=x_0\sim\mathcal N(\alpha_t x_0,\sigma_t^2I).}\]

We can therefore sample a noisy observation directly, without simulating a path:

\[\boxed{X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\epsilon, \qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\quad\epsilon\perp X_0.}\]

This identity describes a one-time conditional law. It does not say that one fixed \(\epsilon\) generates a Brownian trajectory across all times. For \(s<t\), the actual transition instead has the representation

\[X_t=\frac{\alpha_t}{\alpha_s}X_s+ \sqrt{1-\frac{\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_s^2}}\,\epsilon', \qquad\epsilon'\perp X_s.\]

There is new randomness between times \(s\) and \(t\).

Conditional variance versus marginal variance

The conditional variance, with the starting point fixed, is \(\sigma_t^2\). When the starting point is random, independence gives

\[\operatorname{Var}(X_t)= \underbrace{\alpha_t^2\operatorname{Var}(X_0)}_{\text{remaining signal variance}} +\underbrace{\sigma_t^2}_{\text{added noise variance}}.\]

In several dimensions the same statement is \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_t)=\alpha_t^2\operatorname{Cov}(X_0)+\sigma_t^2I\). Preserving covariance \(I\) does not make a non-Gaussian distribution Gaussian at every time. The shape changes as the signal is suppressed.

For example, if \(X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,c)\) then \(p_T=\mathcal N(0,1+\alpha_T^2(c-1))\). A finite terminal time usually gives a near-Gaussian law, not exactly \(\mathcal N(0,1)\).

Optional derivation: stochastic-integral notation

Skip this subsection on a first reading; the Gaussian kernel is already established.

For deterministic \(c(s)\), the notation \(\int_0^t c(s)\,dW_s\) denotes the mean-square limit of weighted increment sums \(\sum_k c(t_k)(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k})\). Independence gives

\[\mathbb E\left[\left(\int_0^t c(s)\,dW_s\right)^2\right] =\int_0^t c(s)^2\,ds.\]

This rule is the deterministic-integrand case of Itô isometry; it is the continuous version of adding squared weights to compute a Gaussian sum’s variance.

Multiplying the VP equation by the integrating factor \(1/\alpha_t\) cancels its drift, since \(d(1/\alpha_t)/dt=\beta(t)/(2\alpha_t)\). The factor is deterministic, so the ordinary product rule with this factor gives

\[d(X_t/\alpha_t)=\frac{\sqrt{\beta(t)}}{\alpha_t}\,dW_t, \qquad X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\alpha_t\int_0^t \frac{\sqrt{\beta(s)}}{\alpha_s}\,dW_s.\]

The added term has variance

\[\alpha_t^2\int_0^t\frac{\beta(s)}{\alpha_s^2}\,ds =\alpha_t^2[\alpha_t^{-2}-1]=1-\alpha_t^2,\]

because \(d(\alpha_s^{-2})/ds=\beta(s)\alpha_s^{-2}\). This confirms the elementary Gaussian-step derivation.

Learn an unknown score from known targets

Conditional and marginal densities

The conditional density \(p(x\mid x_0)\) describes noise around one fixed clean point. The marginal density averages these conditional densities over all possible clean origins:

\[p_t(x)=\int p(x\mid x_0)p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)\,dx_0.\]

For a discrete distribution replace the integral by a weighted sum. For the empirical distribution on \(N\) training points, it is \(N^{-1}\sum_i p(x\mid x_0^{(i)})\). Gaussian corruption gives a smooth positive marginal at \(\sigma_t>0\) even when the clean distribution is discrete.

The conditional Gaussian score is known:

\[\nabla_x\log p(x\mid x_0)=-\frac{x-\alpha_t x_0}{\sigma_t^2}.\]

Evaluated at a random training pair, this target is

\[\left.\nabla_x\log p(x\mid X_0)\right|_{x=X_t} =-\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma_t}.\]

We generated \(\epsilon\), so we know the target. But the sampler needs the marginal score \(s_t(x)=\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\), which need not equal this single pair’s conditional score.

Worked example: conditional expectation as a posterior average

Let \(X_0\) be \(+1\) or \(-1\), each with probability one half. Observing \(X_t=x\) does not reveal its clean origin. Bayes’ rule assigns relative weights using

\[\frac{p(x\mid X_0=+1)}{p(x\mid X_0=-1)} =\exp\left[\frac{(x+\alpha_t)^2-(x-\alpha_t)^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\right] =e^{2\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2}.\]

Thus, with \(w(x)=P(X_0=+1\mid X_t=x)\),

\[w(x)=\frac1{1+e^{-2\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2}},\qquad \mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x]=w(x)-(1-w(x)) =\tanh\left(\frac{\alpha_t x}{\sigma_t^2}\right).\]

The notation \(\mathbb E[A\mid X_t=x]\) means the average of \(A\) under the posterior distribution associated with the observed value \(x\). For a continuous observation, we compute that posterior using densities, rather than divide by \(P(X_t=x)\), which is zero. A useful identity is the tower property: averaging these conditional averages over the observations gives the original average, \(\mathbb E[\mathbb E[A\mid X_t]]=\mathbb E[A]\).

At \(x=0\) both origins have weight one half. Their conditional-score targets are \(+\alpha_t/\sigma_t^2\) and \(-\alpha_t/\sigma_t^2\). The appropriate average is zero.

Derivation: averaging conditional scores gives the marginal score

Differentiate the mixture, using Gaussian smoothness to justify the operation:

\[\begin{aligned} \nabla_xp_t(x) &=\int p(x\mid x_0)\nabla_x\log p(x\mid x_0) p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)\,dx_0,\\ \nabla_x\log p_t(x) &=\int p(x_0\mid x)\nabla_x\log p(x\mid x_0)\,dx_0. \end{aligned}\]

The second line follows by dividing by \(p_t(x)>0\) and applying Bayes’ rule. The same algebra holds with sums for a discrete prior. Consequently,

\[\boxed{s_t(x)=\mathbb E[-\epsilon/\sigma_t\mid X_t=x].}\]

For the two-point example, averaging the two targets gives

\[s_t(x)=\frac{\alpha_t\tanh(\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2)-x}{\sigma_t^2}.\]

Derivation: why squared-error regression learns this average

Let \(Y\) be a square-integrable target, \(Z\) an input, and \(m(Z)=\mathbb E[Y\mid Z]\). For a predictor \(a(Z)\), write

\[a(Z)-Y=[a(Z)-m(Z)]+[m(Z)-Y].\]

On squaring and averaging, the cross term is zero: conditional on \(Z\), \(\mathbb E[m(Z)-Y\mid Z]=0\). The tower property therefore gives

\[\boxed{\mathbb E\|a(Z)-Y\|^2 =\mathbb E\|a(Z)-m(Z)\|^2+\mathbb E\|Y-m(Z)\|^2.}\]

Only the first term depends on the predictor, and it is minimized by \(a=m\). At fixed \(t>0\) with \(\sigma_t>0\), use \(Z=X_t\), \(Y=-\epsilon/\sigma_t\):

\[\mathcal L_t(\theta)=\mathbb E\|s_\theta(X_t,t)+\epsilon/\sigma_t\|^2, \qquad s^*(x,t)=s_t(x).\]

This is denoising score matching (DSM). Its minimum is generally positive: a single noisy observation does not determine its training target. At \(x=0\) in the two-point example, even the ideal output zero leaves conditional loss \(\alpha_t^2/\sigma_t^4\).

Train across noise levels without a singular target

A concrete training loop is:

  1. Draw \(X_0\) from the data and \(t\) from \([t_{\min},T]\), where \(t_{\min}>0\).
  2. Draw independent \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\).
  3. Construct \(X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\epsilon\) directly.
  4. Minimize \(\lambda(t)\|s_\theta(X_t,t)+\epsilon/\sigma_t\|^2\).

Choose an interval where \(\sigma_t\) is bounded away from zero. This avoids the singular endpoint target. For a smooth schedule with \(\beta(0)>0\), \(\sigma_t^2\approx\beta(0)t\), so \(\mathbb E\|\epsilon/\sigma_t\|^2=d/\sigma_t^2\) grows like \(1/t\). With smooth clean densities, even the irreducible unweighted DSM loss can have a divergent time integral at zero. Endpoint behavior depends on the data and schedule; positive-time training is the simple prescription used here.

Noise prediction and time weighting

Define \(\epsilon_\theta(x,t)=-\sigma_t s_\theta(x,t)\). Then pointwise,

\[\boxed{\sigma_t^2\|s_\theta(X_t,t)+\epsilon/\sigma_t\|^2 =\|\epsilon_\theta(X_t,t)-\epsilon\|^2.}\]

Thus choosing \(\lambda(t)=\sigma_t^2\) gives the noise-prediction loss. This removes the explicit inverse-noise scaling; finite expected loss still requires finite second moments of the predictions. At the population optimum, \(\epsilon^*(x,t)=\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid X_t=x]=-\sigma_t s_t(x)\). The targets contain equivalent information, but different time weights and finite-capacity parameterizations can produce different learned fits.

Interpret the score as a denoiser

Derivation: Tweedie’s formula

Average the conditional Gaussian score over the clean posterior:

\[s_t(x)=\frac{\alpha_t\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x]-x}{\sigma_t^2}.\]

For \(\alpha_t>0\) this rearranges to

\[\boxed{\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x] =\frac{x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x)}{\alpha_t}.}\]

The MSE lemma above, now with target \(X_0\), proves that this posterior mean is the Bayes-optimal squared-error denoiser. For unscaled additive noise \(Y=X+\sigma\epsilon\), set \(\alpha=1\) to get \(\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y]=y+\sigma^2\nabla_y\log p_Y(y)\). A denoised posterior mean need not itself be a data sample: for the two-point prior and observation zero it equals zero, although the clean values are \(\pm1\).

What comes next?

We chose a VP corruption law, derived its Gaussian conditional distribution, and proved why regression on known noisy targets recovers the marginal score. Tutorial 2 will derive the sampling velocity

\[v(x,t)=f(x,t)-\tfrac12g(t)^2s_t(x).\]

For now this is a preview: a spatial score must be combined with drift and noise scaling before it becomes a temporal velocity. Learning the score and using it to transport a population are separate steps.

  3. At \(X_t=0\) for the two-point prior, why are the individual training targets nonzero while the ideal score prediction is zero?
  4. Does one fixed \(\epsilon\) in the corruption formula specify a Brownian path?
