The Random Walk: Forward, and Backward

A standalone tutorial, and the most economical object in the course. Toss a coin, add \(+1\) for heads and \(-1\) for tails, and keep a running total. That is the forward process of a diffusion model with the noise made as simple as noise can possibly be — and because it is that simple, every single thing we will later have to learn or approximate can here be done exactly, by counting.

The companion sheet Practice 1 built the machinery. This one spends it. The full derivations, with the small cases written out and the induction in full, are in Practice 2; the Walk Lab below is the same object to play with.

1. The object

Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) be independent with \(\mathbb{P}(X_i = +1) = \mathbb{P}(X_i = -1) = \tfrac12\), and set

\[S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i .\]

This is the simple symmetric random walk. Put its update rule beside the one from note 01 and the resemblance is the entire point:

\[\underbrace{S_n = S_{n-1} + X_n}_{\text{here}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_t}_{\text{a diffusion model}}\]

Both are discrete-time Markov chains in which noise accumulates and nothing removes it; both run forward with no training required; and both end somewhere structureless. The difference is one multiplicative factor. The walk has no shrinkage, so its variance grows without bound — variance exploding; the DDPM update shrinks the old state just enough to hold the total variance at 1 — variance preserving. Both are used in practice, and the choice is a design decision rather than a mathematical necessity.

2. Variances add, so the walk spreads like \(\sqrt{n}\)

Each increment has mean \(0\), and since \(X_i^2 = 1\) always, variance \(1\). The increments are independent, so variances add:

\[\mathbb{E}[S_n] = 0, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n, \qquad \operatorname{sd}(S_n) = \sqrt{n}.\]

This is the \(\sqrt{t}\) envelope

The W01 demo Progressively Adding Noise draws a cloud of walkers spreading with a \(\sqrt{t}\) curve over it. That curve is the line above. A walk does not travel a distance proportional to the number of steps — it travels proportional to the square root of it, because the steps cancel in a way that lets variances rather than distances accumulate.

Everything in this course that spreads like \(\sqrt{t}\) spreads for this reason.

3. The exact law, and where the binomial comes from

Here it matters what the underlying sample space actually is, because the answer is a counting argument and counting needs something to count.

An outcome is a whole path, not a position:

\[\Omega_n = \{-1,+1\}^n, \qquad \omega = (\omega_1,\dots,\omega_n), \qquad |\Omega_n| = 2^n,\]

with every path equally likely, \(\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\}) = 2^{-n}\). On this space \(S_n(\omega) = \sum_i \omega_i\) is a random variable in the sense of note 02 — an honest function — and the interesting thing about it is that it is massively many-to-one: it crushes \(2^n\) paths onto at most \(n+1\) positions.

The event \(\{S_n = k\}\) is therefore a set of paths, the preimage \(S_n^{-1}(\{k\})\), and because the measure is uniform,

\[\mathbb{P}(S_n = k) \;=\; \frac{\big|S_n^{-1}(\{k\})\big|}{2^n} \;=\; \frac{\text{paths ending at } k}{\text{all paths}} .\]

Probability has become combinatorics, not by choice but because the coin is fair.

Now count. A path with \(j\) up-steps and \(n-j\) down-steps ends at \(j - (n-j) = 2j-n\), so ending at \(k\) is the same condition as having exactly \(j = (n+k)/2\) up-steps. The paths ending at \(k\) are in bijection with the ways of choosing which \(j\) of the \(n\) steps went up, and there are \(\binom{n}{j}\) of those:

\[\boxed{\;\mathbb{P}(S_n = k) = \binom{n}{\tfrac{n+k}{2}}\,2^{-n}, \qquad k \in \{-n, -n+2, \dots, n-2, n\}.\;}\]

Note the parity constraint: each step changes \(S\) by an odd amount, so after \(n\) steps only positions of the same parity as \(n\) are reachable. \(\mathbb{P}(S_4 = 1)\) is not small — it is exactly zero.

This is the discrete \(q(x_t \mid x_0)\)

Note 05’s closed-form marginal is what lets a diffusion model jump to any noise level in one step rather than simulating the chain. The boxed formula is that same property, with a binomial coefficient where the Gaussian would be. Without it, sampling \(S_{1000}\) would mean tossing a thousand coins.

Interactive — The Walk Lab runs all of this. Act I stacks the counts up column by column and draws the distribution on the same \(k\) axis, so it reads as the column of counts turned on its side. Act II is §7 below — observe an endpoint and watch path space collapse. Act III is §6, the reverse step.

4. The equation the density obeys

Stop watching the walker and watch the distribution. Write \(p_n(k) = \mathbb{P}(S_n = k)\). There are exactly two ways to be at \(k\) at time \(n+1\), so

\[p_{n+1}(k) = \tfrac12\,p_n(k-1) + \tfrac12\,p_n(k+1).\]

Subtract \(p_n(k)\) from both sides and regroup:

\[\underbrace{p_{n+1}(k) - p_n(k)}_{\text{difference in time}} \;=\; \tfrac12 \underbrace{\big[\,p_n(k+1) - 2p_n(k) + p_n(k-1)\,\big]}_{\text{second difference in space}}\]

The left side is a discrete time derivative; the right is the three-point second difference, the discrete \(\partial_x^2\). In the continuum,

\[\boxed{\;\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} = \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial x^2}\;}\]

That is the heat equation

It is the Fokker–Planck equation for Brownian motion — Module 5’s centrepiece — derived from a coin toss with nothing but addition and subtraction.

Read it rather than merely verifying it: where \(p\) is concave (a peak) the second difference is negative and the density falls; where it is convex (a trough) it rises. Peaks flatten, troughs fill, the whole thing spreads. That is what “adding noise” does to a distribution, as opposed to what it does to a sample.

Where \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) comes from. Take lattice spacing \(h\) and time step \(\Delta t\), so after time \(t\) the walker has taken \(n = t/\Delta t\) steps and, by §2, has variance \(n h^2 = t h^2/\Delta t\). Only one choice of \(h\) survives the limit:

choice variance at time \(t\) limit
\(h = \Delta t\) \(t\,\Delta t \to 0\) the walk dies
\(h = \sqrt{\Delta t}\) \(t\) Brownian motion
\(h = (\Delta t)^{1/4}\) \(t/\sqrt{\Delta t} \to \infty\) the walk explodes

This is why an SDE is written \(dX = b\,dt + \sigma\,dW\) with \(dW\) of size \(\sqrt{dt}\) and not \(dt\) — and it is the same fact that puts a square root on the noise coefficient \(\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\), since that coefficient multiplies the variable while variances scale by its square.

5. The Gaussian is a limit, not an assumption

\(S_n\) is a sum of \(n\) i.i.d. terms with mean \(0\) and variance \(1\), so \(S_n/\sqrt{n} \Rightarrow \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) — equivalently, the binomial of §3, once centred and scaled, approaches a Gaussian density.

The increments here are as un-Gaussian as a random variable can be: two atoms, no density at all. After enough steps the marginal is Gaussian anyway. So the Gaussian noise in a diffusion model is not a modelling restriction being smuggled in — after many small steps you would arrive at a Gaussian whatever the increments looked like. Choosing Gaussians only buys closed forms at every \(t\) rather than asymptotically.

Run \(d\) independent walks and a second fact appears: \(\mathbb{E}\lVert S_n\rVert^2 = nd\), and the relative spread of \(\lVert S_n \rVert\) shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{2d}\). In high dimension essentially every sample sits in a thin shell at radius \(\sqrt{nd}\), even though the origin is the single most likely point. That is the Gaussian annulus of note 06, and it is why \(x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_d)\) is a sphere rather than a blob — the reverse process starts on a shell.

6. Backward: the reverse step is exactly solvable

Now the diffusion question, asked of a coin: you are told where the walk ended; what was the last step?

By Bayes. To have \(S_n = k\) with \(X_n = +1\) the walk must have been at \(k-1\) after \(n-1\) steps. With \(j = (n+k)/2\),

\[\mathbb{P}(X_n = +1 \mid S_n = k) = \frac{\mathbb{P}(S_{n-1}=k-1)\cdot\tfrac12}{\mathbb{P}(S_n = k)} = \frac{\binom{n-1}{j-1}}{\binom{n}{j}} = \frac{j}{n} = \frac{n+k}{2n},\]

the powers of two cancelling and the last step being \(\binom{n}{j} = \tfrac{n}{j}\binom{n-1}{j-1}\).

By symmetry, in one line. Given \(S_n = k\), every path with \(j\) up-steps has the same probability — a path’s probability depends on how many up-steps it has, not where they fall. So the increments are conditionally exchangeable, \(\mathbb{E}[X_i \mid S_n = k]\) is the same number \(c\) for every \(i\), and they must sum to \(k\). Hence \(nc = k\) and

\[\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}\big[X_i \mid S_n = k\big] = \frac{k}{n} \quad\text{for every } i.\;}\]

The expected step is the average step. Same theorem; one derivation takes a blackboard and the other takes a sentence.

This is a denoiser

A hidden quantity, a noisy observation, a posterior, and its mean — and note 07’s best-predictor theorem says the conditional mean is exactly what squared-error training converges to. So \(k/n\) is what a network trained to predict the increment from the endpoint would learn. Here we have it in closed form, so we can check the answer a network would otherwise have to discover.

A question worth sitting with: does the answer change if the coin is biased? It does not. Conditioning on the endpoint fixes the number of up-steps, all arrangements stay equally likely whatever \(p\) is, and exchangeability is untouched. The forward process depends on the bias; the reverse step given the endpoint does not.

Reverse sampling. The reverse step is all you need to run the process backwards. From \(S_n = k\): set \(j = (n+k)/2\), declare the last step \(+1\) with probability \(j/n\) (else \(-1\)), update \(k\) and \(n\), repeat. This produces a trajectory distributed exactly as a forward walk conditioned to end at \(k\) — a complete diffusion model, in nine lines of code, with nothing trained. Act III of the lab runs it.

7. What the observation actually did

This is the part the lab is built to make visible, and it is worth stating carefully because it is the idea the whole course rests on.

Before you observe anything, all \(2^n\) paths are equally likely. Ranking paths by probability gives one enormous tie. The distribution of \(S_n\) is peaked in the middle not because those paths are likelier, but because more paths land there.

Observing \(S_n = k\) then reweights nothing. It rules paths out. The \(\binom{n}{j}\) survivors remain exactly as likely as one another — a perfect tie, just a smaller one. And the size of what survives measures how much you learned:

observation paths surviving (\(n = 20\)) bits still unknown
\(S_n = n\) \(1\) \(0\) — the path is pinned
\(S_n = n-2\) \(20\) \(4.3\)
\(S_n = 0\) \(184{,}756\) \(17.5\) of \(20\)

The extreme case is the clearest. \(S_n = n\) can be reached by exactly one path, all \(+1\)s, so the observation determines the entire history and the reverse process is deterministic. At the other end, the most likely endpoint is also the least informative: more paths survive there than anywhere else.

8. Where learning has to enter

Everything above was exact. It is worth being precise about which step stops being exact when the data stops being a coin, because that step is the rest of the course.

The reverse process needs \(\mathbb{P}(x_0 \mid x_t)\). Here we could write it down because the possibilities could be enumerated. Replace the coin with images and three things break at once: there is no formula for \(p(x_0)\) — note 06 is a catalogue of failed attempts to get one; the sum over possible \(x_0\) becomes an intractable integral over \(\mathbb{R}^d\); and by §5 the relevant region is a thin shell in a huge space, so no amount of sampling covers it.

So the posterior mean is not computable. But note 07 §1 says it is exactly what squared-error regression converges to — and a regression target is available, because the forward process is known and we can noise a real image ourselves.

The whole idea, assembled

A forward process that is free to run and has a closed-form marginal (§3); a reverse process that is a posterior over what was added (§6); and a training objective that reaches that posterior without ever writing it down (note 07).

Modules 3 to 6 replace the coin with a Gaussian and the enumeration with a network. Nothing else changes.

Exercises

The full set, with the small cases enumerated and the induction written out, is in Practice 2. A representative few:

  1. Compute \(\mathbb{P}(S_6 = k)\) for every \(k\) and check the values sum to 1. Which \(k\) are unreachable, and why?
  2. Prove the recursion \(N_{n+1}(k) = N_n(k-1) + N_n(k+1)\) by deleting the last step, and show it is Pascal’s rule in disguise.
  3. A walker starts at 0. Estimate how many steps until it is typically 100 away, then 1000 away, and comment on the ratio.
  4. Show \(\mathbb{E}[X_i \mid S_n = k] = k/n\) for every \(i\), not just \(i = n\). Why does this not contradict the increments being unconditionally independent?
  5. Let \(\mathbb{P}(X_i = +1) = p\). Show \(\mathbb{E}[S_n] = n(2p-1)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = 4np(1-p)\), and then that \(\mathbb{P}(X_n = +1 \mid S_n = k)\) is still \((n+k)/2n\), independent of \(p\).
  6. Which choice of \(\alpha_t\) turns the DDPM update of §1 into this walk? What does that say about variance-preserving versus variance-exploding processes?
