Sampling with Scores: Worked Examples of the Probability-Flow ODE

A teaching sequence: generate particles, solve two Gaussian score fields, add noise to obtain a sampler, construct the probability-flow ODE, and finally learn the score through denoising. Derivatives, Gaussian distributions, and conditional expectation are enough to begin.

Place in the teaching sequence. This is the worked-solution track. Read Sections 1–8 of From Local Arrows first if fields and flows are new to you. Here we revisit those ideas through solved Gaussian examples, then build a sampler. The Tweedie tutorial supplies the separate statistical derivation when we reach score estimation. The overlap is intentional: the geometric note explains what the objects mean; this note computes with them.

1. Generation means producing samples

Suppose our target is a distribution \(p_{\text{data}}\). To generate from it means to return a random variable whose distribution is \(p_{\text{data}}\). Returning the most likely point every time would fail: it would give a point mass.

One way to generate is to draw an easy random starting point and move it:

\[\text{random start}\ \longrightarrow\ \text{successive updates} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{output sample}.\]

An ODE supplies deterministic updates. Given a velocity field \(v\), explicit Euler uses

\[\boxed{X\leftarrow X+h\,v(X,t),\qquad t\leftarrow t+h.}\]

An SDE also injects fresh randomness. For \(dX_t=f(X_t,t)dt+g(t)dW_t\), Euler–Maruyama uses a positive forward step \(h\):

\[\boxed{X\leftarrow X+h\,f(X,t)+g(t)\sqrt h\,z, \qquad z\sim\mathcal N(0,I).}\]

Each step draws a new independent \(z\). The \(\sqrt h\) occurs because a Brownian increment over length \(h\) has covariance \(hI\).

These are computational recipes, not yet proofs of correct sampling. An ODE solver transports its input distribution; an SDE solver simulates drift and random motion. We still have to choose dynamics that deliver the desired law. That choice is where the score enters.

Our notation

We follow The Probability-Flow ODE: \(p_t\) is the forward-noised density; \(s_t=\nabla_x\log p_t\) its score; \(f,g\) the forward-SDE coefficients; \(v\) the probability-flow velocity. The flow from \(s\) to \(t\) is \(\Psi_{s\to t}\), and \(\Phi\) is reserved for the standard normal CDF.

For generation we use \(\tau=T-t\) and a descending grid with \(h_k=t_{k-1}-t_k<0\). The positive elapsed-time variable \(u\) below is used only for experiments at a fixed target density. This keeps it distinct from the changing diffusion noise level \(t\).

2. First solved problem: the score of a standard Gaussian

Let the target density be

\[p_{\text{data}}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}.\]

Take the logarithm and differentiate with respect to the sample coordinate:

\[\log p_{\text{data}}(x)=-\tfrac12x^2-\tfrac12\log(2\pi), \qquad \boxed{s(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\log p_{\text{data}}(x)=-x.}\]

At \(x=2\) the score points left; at \(x=-3\) it points right; at zero it vanishes. The score is a contraction field toward the origin. Try using it directly as velocity:

\[\frac{dx}{du}=s(x)=-x,\qquad x(0)=x_0.\]

For \(x_0\ne0\), separate variables:

\[\frac{dx}{x}=-du,\qquad \log|x|=-u+C, \qquad \boxed{x(u)=x_0e^{-u}.}\]

The equilibrium solution \(x\equiv0\) completes the case omitted by division by \(x\). Collecting all initial conditions gives the flow

\[\boxed{\Psi_{0\to u}(x_0)=e^{-u}x_0.}\]

After one unit of time, every distance from zero has been multiplied by \(e^{-1}\). After \(u+w\) units, it has been multiplied by \(e^{-u}e^{-w}\). At every finite time, distinct particles remain distinct; they approach zero asymptotically.

Euler approximates the exact multiplier \(e^{-h}\) by \(1-h\):

\[x_{k+1}=(1-h)x_k,\qquad x_k=(1-h)^kx_0.\]

For \(0<h<1\) this approaches zero without changing sign. For \(1<h<2\) it alternates sides while approaching zero. For \(h>2\) it diverges. The last two behaviors remind us that a solver’s behavior can differ from the exact ODE.

Does this generate standard Gaussian samples? If \(X_{\mathrm{start}}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\), the output after time \(u\) is

\[\Psi_{0\to u}(X_{\mathrm{start}})\sim\mathcal N(0,e^{-2u}).\]

It is no longer standard Gaussian. The field is interpretable and the flow is correctly solved, but the dynamics concentrate the cloud at the mode. This is the score-ascent ODE, not yet the probability-flow ODE of a chosen noising process. Keep this solved example: it will let us check every later construction.

3. Second solved problem: a Gaussian centered at mu

Translate the target to \(p_{\text{data}}=\mathcal N(\mu,1)\). Then

\[\log p_{\text{data}}(x)=C-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^2, \qquad \boxed{s(x)=\mu-x.}\]

The zero of the field has moved from \(0\) to \(\mu\). For the ODE \(dx/du=\mu-x\), set \(z=x-\mu\). Because \(\mu\) is constant,

\[\frac{dz}{du}=-z.\]

We have already solved this equation. Therefore

\[\boxed{x(u)=\mu+(x_0-\mu)e^{-u},\qquad \Psi_{0\to u}(x_0)=\mu+e^{-u}(x_0-\mu).}\]

For example, with \(\mu=2\) and \(x_0=5\), the particle is at \(2+3e^{-1}\approx3.10\) after one unit. It approaches \(2\) without crossing it. The flow first measures displacement from \(\mu\), shrinks that displacement, then adds \(\mu\) back.

Euler gives

\[x_{k+1}=x_k+h(\mu-x_k),\qquad x_k-\mu=(1-h)^k(x_0-\mu).\]

If the starting cloud is \(\mathcal N(m_{\mathrm{start}},V_{\mathrm{start}})\), the exact output law is

\[\mathcal N\!\left(\mu+e^{-u}(m_{\mathrm{start}}-\mu), e^{-2u}V_{\mathrm{start}}\right).\]

Its mean approaches the right location, but its variance approaches zero. Getting the center right is not enough to sample the target distribution.

For the slightly more general target \(\mathcal N(\mu,\varrho^2)\),

\[s(x)=-\frac{x-\mu}{\varrho^2},\qquad \Psi_{0\to u}(x)=\mu+(x-\mu)e^{-u/\varrho^2}.\]

We use \(\varrho\) for the target standard deviation so that \(\sigma_t\) can retain its diffusion meaning: the standard deviation of the corruption noise.

4. Why choose a score field? Geometry and energy

The Gaussian calculation is an instance of a general construction:

\[\boxed{s(x)=\nabla_x\log p(x).}\]

It produces one vector component per data coordinate. For a small displacement \(\delta\),

\[\log\frac{p(x+\delta)}{p(x)}=s(x)^\top\delta+o(\|\delta\|).\]

The score thus measures local relative density change. In Euclidean coordinates its direction is the steepest first-order increase in log density, where the score is nonzero. This gives a local direction rather than a global destination.

An energy interpretation supplies the same field. Write

\[p(x)=Z^{-1}e^{-E(x)}.\]

Then \(\log p=-E-\log Z\), and since \(Z\) does not depend on \(x\),

\[\boxed{s(x)=-\nabla E(x).}\]

The score points downhill in energy; the unknown normalizing constant disappears. For \(\mathcal N(\mu,\varrho^2)\), take \(E(x)=(x-\mu)^2/(2\varrho^2)\). Our Gaussian score ODE is precisely gradient descent in this quadratic energy.

But downhill motion alone settles at an energy minimum. A Gibbs distribution \(e^{-E}/Z\) occupies a range of energies. We need a mechanism that balances this concentration with spread. The energy picture motivates the score; the next calculation justifies its coefficient in a sampler.

5. Add noise: Langevin sampling and Euler–Maruyama

For a fixed target \(p\), consider

\[\boxed{dX_u=s(X_u)\,du+\sqrt2\,dW_u.}\]

The deterministic part pulls uphill in density, and Brownian motion spreads the cloud. Euler–Maruyama makes this directly executable:

\[\boxed{X\leftarrow X+h\,s(X)+\sqrt{2h}\,z_k, \qquad z_k\overset{\mathrm{iid}}\sim\mathcal N(0,I).}\]

Draw many initial particles and run the update for each. With suitable regularity and ergodicity, continuous-time Langevin dynamics approach \(p\) as \(u\to\infty\). A finite-step discretization approximates that dynamics; it has discretization bias, and multimodal targets can mix slowly.

Solve the Gaussian sampler too

For \(p=\mathcal N(\mu,1)\),

\[dX_u=(\mu-X_u)du+\sqrt2\,dW_u.\]

Multiplying \(X_u-\mu\) by the integrating factor \(e^u\) gives

\[X_u=\mu+e^{-u}(X_{\mathrm{start}}-\mu) +\sqrt2\int_0^u e^{-(u-a)}\,dW_a.\]

The stochastic integral is a zero-mean Gaussian independent of the starting point, with variance \(2\int_0^u e^{-2(u-a)}da=1-e^{-2u}\). Thus for a Gaussian start,

\[\boxed{X_u\sim\mathcal N\!\left( \mu+e^{-u}(m_{\mathrm{start}}-\mu), e^{-2u}V_{\mathrm{start}}+1-e^{-2u}\right).}\]

Compare with the score ODE: the mean behaves identically, but the variance now approaches one. If the initial law already is \(\mathcal N(\mu,1)\), it stays that way. This is the balance that makes it a sampler.

Euler–Maruyama uses \(X\leftarrow(1-h)X+h\mu+\sqrt{2h}z_k\). Its stationary variance, for \(0<h<2\), solves

\[V_h=(1-h)^2V_h+2h,\qquad V_h=\frac1{1-h/2}.\]

The finite-step law is too broad. Reducing \(h\) improves the numerical approximation; running more steps at the same \(h\) does not remove this bias.

Pause for an experiment. In Lab 2, compare score ascent and Langevin sampling for \(\mu=0\). Predict their variances before moving the time slider. Translating every particle by \(\mu\) gives the unit-variance, nonzero-mean example just solved.

Why does this work for a general density?

Let \(q_u\) be the density of the current particle cloud. Its evolution is

\[\partial_u q_u=-\nabla\cdot(q_u s)+\Delta q_u.\]

The first term transports mass; the second spreads it. At \(q_u=p\), use \(ps=p\nabla\log p=\nabla p\):

\[-\nabla\cdot(ps)+\Delta p=-\Delta p+\Delta p=0.\]

Thus \(p\) is invariant, with appropriate boundary or tail conditions. This is a specific justification for using the score with noise coefficient \(\sqrt2\). Invariance and convergence are distinct: convergence also requires conditions that rule out trapping or explosion.

6. From one fixed target to a path of noisy densities

For real data, the fixed-target score is unknown and the density may have separated modes or singular support. Diffusion instead constructs a path of increasingly smoothed distributions, then learns their time-indexed scores.

Choose the forward SDE

\[dX_t=f(X_t,t)dt+g(t)dW_t,\qquad X_0\sim p_0=p_{\text{data}}.\]

Here \(g(t)\) is scalar and independent of position. Our common VP choice is

\[f(x,t)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)x,\qquad g(t)=\sqrt{\beta(t)},\]

with

\[\alpha_t=\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12\int_0^t\beta(a)da\right), \qquad\sigma_t^2=1-\alpha_t^2.\]

At each fixed time, the conditional law is generated by

\[\boxed{X_t\overset d=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\varepsilon, \qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\quad\varepsilon\perp X_0.}\]

This is a statement about one time slice, not a prescription to reuse a single \(\varepsilon\) for a Brownian path. The density \(p_t\) depends on both the known corruption kernel and the unknown data law. Its score is \(s_t=\nabla\log p_t\).

Choose \(T\) so that \(\alpha_T\) is small; \(p_T\) is then approximately standard Gaussian for suitably scaled data. Generation aims to move from \(p_T\) back to \(p_0\). It does not wait for equilibrium at every intermediate density.

7. The reverse-SDE sampler, in the common notation

The reverse-time theorem gives, in the increasing clock \(\tau=T-t\),

\[\boxed{dX=[-f(X,t)+g(t)^2s_t(X)]d\tau+g(t)d\bar W_\tau.}\]

Here \(X\) is the current sampler state and \(t=T-\tau\). The theorem needs suitable regularity and nondegeneracy. Its score correction accounts for how probable different predecessors are, conditional on the current noisy state.

Use \(T=t_N>\cdots>t_0=\epsilon\) and \(h_k=t_{k-1}-t_k<0\). The corresponding Euler–Maruyama sampler is

\[\boxed{X\leftarrow X+h_k[f(X,t_k)-g(t_k)^2s_{t_k}(X)] +g(t_k)\sqrt{|h_k|}\,z_k.}\]

The drift is evaluated at the current point. Brownian variance is proportional to the positive reverse elapsed time \(|h_k|\). In VP form this becomes

\[X\leftarrow X+|h_k|\beta(t_k) \left[\tfrac12X+s_{t_k}(X)\right] +\sqrt{\beta(t_k)|h_k|}\,z_k.\]

With the exact terminal law, exact scores, and exact continuous-time dynamics, the sampler traverses the marginals backward. Finite steps approximate this result. Starting instead from \(\mathcal N(0,I)\) introduces terminal-prior error.

8. Derive the probability-flow ODE by conserving probability

Now ask: can deterministic particles follow the same density path?

The forward SDE evolves its density by Fokker–Planck:

\[\partial_t p_t=-\nabla\cdot(f p_t)+\tfrac12g(t)^2\Delta p_t.\]

An ODE \(dX_t/dt=v(X_t,t)\) evolves its density by continuity:

\[\partial_t p_t=-\nabla\cdot(p_t v).\]

In one dimension, \(-\partial_x(p_tv)\) is the net inflow of mass per unit length. The multidimensional divergence expresses the same conservation principle. To match these equations, write the probability current as

\[p_t v=f p_t-\tfrac12g^2\nabla p_t.\]

Divide by the positive density. This division is where the log derivative appears:

\[\boxed{v=f-\tfrac12g^2\frac{\nabla p_t}{p_t} =f-\tfrac12g^2s_t.}\]

Consequently,

\[\boxed{\frac{dX_t}{dt}=v(X_t,t) =f(X_t,t)-\tfrac12g(t)^2s_t(X_t).}\]

This is the probability-flow ODE. Under conditions ensuring well-posed density evolution and flows, it has the same marginals as the forward SDE when initialized with a matching law. It gives a particular transport field; in higher dimensions the density path need not determine a unique field.

The score is therefore more than an attractive direction. It is the exact quantity that converts a diffusive probability current into a velocity field.

For VP noising,

\[\boxed{v(x,t)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)[x+s_t(x)].}\]

Sampling is now ordinary Euler integration on the descending grid:

\[\boxed{X\leftarrow X+h_kv(X,t_k),\qquad h_k<0.}\]

Dynamics toward lower noise Increment to \(X\)
Reverse SDE \(h_k(f-g^2s_t)+g\sqrt{|h_k|}z_k\)
Probability-flow ODE \(h_k(f-\tfrac12g^2s_t)\)

The ODE needs half the score coefficient because it no longer injects spreading noise. Simply removing the random term from the reverse SDE gives the wrong deterministic dynamics. Randomness in an ODE sampler comes from its starting sample; randomness in the SDE sampler also comes from its Brownian increments.

9. Return to the solved Gaussians: now solve their probability flows

We can test the entire construction using the two targets from the beginning.

Standard normal data under VP noising

If \(p_0=\mathcal N(0,1)\), then \(p_t=\mathcal N(0,\alpha_t^2+\sigma_t^2)=\mathcal N(0,1)\). Thus

\[s_t(x)=-x,\qquad \boxed{v(x,t)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)[x-x]=0.}\]

The exact solution is \(X_t=X_s\) and

\[\boxed{\Psi_{s\to t}(x)=x.}\]

The probability-flow sampler leaves each initial standard Gaussian draw in place. This is correct: its source and target distributions already agree. The score contracts, but its contribution cancels the known VP drift in the probability-flow velocity. The score-ascent flow \(e^{-u}x\) solved earlier and this identity flow answer different questions.

Data with mean mu and unit variance

If \(p_0=\mathcal N(\mu,1)\), VP noising gives

\[p_t=\mathcal N(\alpha_t\mu,1),\qquad s_t(x)=\alpha_t\mu-x.\]

Substitute into the probability-flow velocity:

\[v(x,t)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)\alpha_t\mu=\alpha_t'\mu.\]

The dependence on \(x\) cancels. Integrating from \(s\) to \(t\),

\[\boxed{\Psi_{s\to t}(x)=x+(\alpha_t-\alpha_s)\mu.}\]

This is a translation, preserving all distances and the variance. Forward time moves the mean toward zero; backward generation restores it. Since \(\alpha_0=1\),

\[X_T\sim\mathcal N(\alpha_T\mu,1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Psi_{T\to0}(X_T)=X_T+(1-\alpha_T)\mu\sim\mathcal N(\mu,1).\]

For \(\beta\equiv1\), \(\alpha_t=e^{-t/2}\). With \(\mu=2\), \(T=2\), the exact starting mean is \(2e^{-1}\approx0.736\) and generation adds approximately \(1.264\) to every particle. Its mean becomes \(2\), while its variance stays one.

Starting from a standard Gaussian at this finite \(T\) instead would give mean \((1-\alpha_T)\mu\), not \(\mu\). This explicitly exhibits terminal-prior mismatch, even with a perfect score and exact solver.

Extension: a different target variance

For \(p_0=\mathcal N(\mu,\varrho^2)\) define its noisy marginal variance

\[q_t=\alpha_t^2\varrho^2+\sigma_t^2.\]

Then \(p_t=\mathcal N(\alpha_t\mu,q_t)\), and

\[s_t(x)=-\frac{x-\alpha_t\mu}{q_t},\qquad v(x,t)=\alpha_t'\mu+\frac{q_t'}{2q_t}(x-\alpha_t\mu).\]

Here \(q_t'=\beta(t)(1-q_t)\). Differentiate the standardized coordinate \(z_t=(X_t-\alpha_t\mu)/\sqrt{q_t}\) along this ODE; its derivative is zero. Consequently,

\[\boxed{\Psi_{s\to t}(x)=\alpha_t\mu+ \sqrt{\frac{q_t}{q_s}}(x-\alpha_s\mu).}\]

The map translates the center and rescales its spread. This is an exact, fully interpretable generative flow, and reduces to the preceding translation when \(\varrho=1\).

Check in the lab. Lab 3 uses a two-Gaussian mixture instead of one Gaussian. The score and both samplers are still explicit, but the transport can now separate a cloud into two modes. Compare histograms rather than expecting SDE and ODE particle paths to match. The lab displays the positive step magnitude \(h=|h_k|\); this note uses signed backward steps \(h_k<0\). The numerical updates are the same.

10. We can sample if we know the score. How do we learn it?

For the Gaussian examples, differentiating an explicit density was easy. Real data supply examples \(x_0\), not an evaluable marginal density \(p_t\). Directly asking a network to match \(\nabla\log p_t\) would require an unavailable target.

But we can manufacture noisy/clean training pairs by drawing \(x_0\) and \(\varepsilon\) independently:

\[x_0\sim p_{\text{data}},\qquad x_t=\alpha_t x_0+\sigma_t\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I).\]

A familiar regression problem is now available: predict \(x_0\) from \((x_t,t)\) under squared error. Its population-optimal prediction is

\[\widehat X_0(x,t):=\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x].\]

To see why, write \(X_0-D=(X_0-\widehat X_0)+(\widehat X_0-D)\). Conditional on \(X_t=x\), the first bracket has mean zero, so the cross term vanishes and

\[\mathbb E[\|X_0-D\|^2\mid X_t=x] =\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(X_0\mid X_t=x) +\|\widehat X_0(x,t)-D\|^2.\]

The second term is minimized at \(D=\widehat X_0\). We can therefore learn a posterior-mean denoiser. Does a denoiser give the score needed by the sampler? This is the question that motivates Tweedie’s formula.

Derive the bridge

At a fixed positive noise level,

\[p_t(x)=\int\mathcal N(x;\alpha_t x_0,\sigma_t^2I)\,p_0(dx_0).\]

Differentiate the Gaussian kernel with respect to its observed argument \(x\):

\[\nabla_xp_t(x\mid x_0) =\frac{\alpha_t x_0-x}{\sigma_t^2}p_t(x\mid x_0).\]

Differentiating under the integral, dividing by \(p_t(x)\), and recognizing Bayes’ posterior gives

\[ \begin{aligned} s_t(x) &=\int\frac{\alpha_t x_0-x}{\sigma_t^2} \frac{p_t(x\mid x_0)p_0(dx_0)}{p_t(x)}\\ &=\boxed{\frac{\alpha_t\widehat X_0(x,t)-x}{\sigma_t^2}}. \end{aligned} \]

For \(\alpha_t>0\), equivalently,

\[\boxed{\widehat X_0(x,t)=\frac{x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x)}{\alpha_t}.}\]

This is VP Tweedie. It says that a posterior-mean denoiser and a score carry the same information at a fixed nondegenerate noise level. For the separate additive observation model \(Y=X_0+\sigma\varepsilon\), with freely chosen \(\sigma>0\), the displacement \(\sigma^2\nabla_y\log p_Y(y)\) points directly toward the posterior mean. This additive model does not impose the VP constraint \(\alpha_t^2+\sigma_t^2=1\): setting \(\alpha_t=1\) within that constraint would force zero noise. In VP coordinates, \(\sigma_t^2s_t(x)\) points toward the attenuated mean \(\alpha_t\widehat X_0(x,t)\).

The standalone Tweedie tutorial supplies the regularity conditions, additive-noise derivation, and two-point posterior example.

Direct score training and noise prediction

The conditional corruption score is the available random target

\[\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t\mid x_0)=-\varepsilon/\sigma_t.\]

Squared-error regression against that target fits its conditional mean:

\[s^\star(x,t)=\mathbb E[-\varepsilon/\sigma_t\mid X_t=x]=s_t(x).\]

Thus we can train \(s_\theta\) with

\[\mathcal L(\theta)=\mathbb E\left[\lambda(t) \left\|s_\theta(x_t,t)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_t}\right\|^2\right],\]

using positive-noise times and suitable weighting or a lower cutoff to keep the objective finite. Alternatively, a squared-error noise predictor estimates \(\varepsilon^\star(x,t)=\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_t=x]\), with

\[s_t(x)=-\varepsilon^\star(x,t)/\sigma_t.\]

The estimated function is a conditional mean, not the particular unseen noise realization. Loss weights must be adjusted when comparing parameterizations; finite networks and optimization need not give identical results.

11. The probability-flow sampler we can now implement

After training, choose the grid \(T=t_N>\cdots>t_0=\epsilon\) and follow

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} &X\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\quad\text{(approximation to }p_T\text{)};\\ &\text{for }k=N,N-1,\ldots,1:\\ &\qquad h_k\leftarrow t_{k-1}-t_k;\\ &\qquad s\leftarrow s_\theta(X,t_k);\\ &\qquad v\leftarrow-\tfrac12\beta(t_k)(X+s);\\ &\qquad X\leftarrow X+h_kv;\\ &\text{return }X. \end{aligned}} \]

Every step reevaluates the score because both the state and noise level change. The algorithm uses the learned velocity

\[v_\theta(x,t)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)[x+s_\theta(x,t)].\]

Write its flow as \(\Psi^\theta_{T\to\epsilon}\). Under the usual convergence conditions, refining the Euler mesh converges to this learned flow. The exact probability-flow map \(\Psi_{T\to\epsilon}\) instead uses \(s_t\). They coincide when the learned and exact velocity fields agree; reducing the numerical step size alone does not eliminate score-estimation error.

There are four separate approximations: replacing \(p_T\) by a Gaussian, estimating the score, integrating with finite steps, and stopping at \(\epsilon>0\) instead of zero. With a smooth target the endpoint may be regular at zero; atomic data typically require a limiting argument. The exact SDE/ODE marginal equivalence does not automatically survive an imperfect learned score.

A posterior mean from one noisy observation is also not the same object as this integrated flow. Denoising estimates a hidden clean value under squared loss; sampling must reproduce the population’s distribution.

Problems for discussion

  1. For \(p=\mathcal N(3,4)\), compute the score-ascent flow and its Euler stability range. Check: \(3+(x-3)e^{-u/4}\); \(0<h<8\).
  2. For that same target, show that Langevin variance satisfies \(V'=-V/2+2\). What is its limiting value? Check: \(4\).
  3. Under VP noising, take \(p_0=\mathcal N(3,1)\) and \(\beta\equiv1\). Write the exact map from \(T\) to zero. Check: \(x+3(1-e^{-T/2})\).
  4. Why is \(s_t(x)=-x\) compatible with zero probability-flow velocity? Check: its correction cancels the VP forward drift.
  5. If an exact denoiser is available, how do we obtain a score for the sampler? Check: \((\alpha_t\widehat X_0-x)/\sigma_t^2\), at positive noise.
