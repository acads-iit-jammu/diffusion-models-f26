Transport You Can Compute: Histograms, Flows, and the Price of Moving Mass

A standalone tutorial. The transport tutorials so far have been about the continuum — the intuitive story and the rigorous account. This one puts transport on a finite ground set, where it becomes a linear program you can solve exactly, and where the one modelling decision that drives everything — what does it cost to move mass? — is out in the open. Earth Mover’s Distance, Word Mover’s Distance, and the Wasserstein distances in generative modelling are all this one problem. The closing section answers the question this setting always provokes: when the bins dissolve into a continuous density and the Jacobian shows up, what plays the role of the cost, and what plays the role of the transporter?

1. Two histograms, one question

Fix four bins \(A, B, C, D\) — they can be greyscale levels, vocabulary items, mesh cells, cluster centroids, anything. Put two probability mass functions on them:

\[ p = (p_A, p_B, p_C, p_D), \qquad q = (q_A, q_B, q_C, q_D), \qquad \textstyle\sum_i p_i = \sum_j q_j = 1 . \]

Now ask how to turn \(p\) into \(q\) by moving mass around. A transport plan is a table \(\gamma_{ij} \ge 0\) recording how much mass travels from bin \(i\) to bin \(j\), and it is a legal plan exactly when nothing is created or destroyed:

\[ \underbrace{\sum_j \gamma_{ij} = p_i}_{\text{all of bin } i \text{ leaves}}, \qquad \underbrace{\sum_i \gamma_{ij} = q_j}_{\text{bin } j \text{ receives exactly } q_j} . \]

You have already met this object

Those two conditions say the non-negative table \(\gamma\) has row sums \(p\) and column sums \(q\) — that is, \(\gamma\) is a joint distribution whose two marginals are \(p\) and \(q\). A transport plan is a coupling, in the exact sense of Joint, Marginal, Conditional: the mountain whose two shadows are \(p\) and \(q\).

So everything from that tutorial applies verbatim, including the part that matters most here: there are many couplings. The shadows do not determine the mountain, so the constraints alone do not determine the plan. Something else has to choose, and that something is the cost.

The set of legal plans is never empty — the independent coupling \(\gamma_{ij} = p_i q_j\) always qualifies — and it is a convex polytope, the transportation polytope. Optimal transport is the problem of picking the cheapest point in it.

2. The linear program, and why it is a flow

Let \(C_{ij} \ge 0\) be the price of moving one unit of mass from bin \(i\) to bin \(j\). Then the total bill for a plan is \(\langle \gamma, C\rangle = \sum_{ij} \gamma_{ij} C_{ij}\), and the Kantorovich problem is

\[ \operatorname{OT}_C(p,q) \;=\; \min_{\gamma \ge 0}\; \sum_{i,j} \gamma_{ij} C_{ij} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_j \gamma_{ij} = p_i, \;\; \sum_i \gamma_{ij} = q_j . \]

Linear objective, linear constraints, non-negative variables: a linear program. But it is a linear program of a very particular kind. Draw the bins twice — once as sources, once as sinks — and join every source to every sink. Then \(\gamma\) is a flow on that bipartite graph: \(p_i\) is supply at source \(i\), \(q_j\) is demand at sink \(j\), \(C_{ij}\) is the per-unit cost on edge \(i \to j\), and we want the cheapest flow meeting all demands. This is the classical transportation problem, and it is solved by min-cost-flow machinery — network simplex, the Hungarian algorithm in the assignment special case, auction algorithms — rather than by a general-purpose LP solver.

Two structural facts are worth carrying around.

Sparsity. Two separate facts, often run together. The constraint matrix is totally unimodular, which gives integral vertices when the marginals are integral. Separately, the constraint matrix has rank \(m + n - 1\), so a vertex (basic feasible solution) has at most \(m + n - 1\) non-zero entries — its support is a forest. For our \(4 \times 4\) problem that is 7 of the 16 routes.

Note what this does and does not say. There always exists an optimal plan that is this sparse, and that is what a simplex-type method returns. It is not true that every optimal plan is sparse: if \(C \equiv 0\) then every coupling is optimal, including the dense independent one. The demo shows the count live for the plan it displays.

Duality, as prices. The dual of the program above is

\[ \max_{f, g} \;\; \sum_i f_i\, p_i + \sum_j g_j\, q_j \qquad \text{subject to} \qquad f_i + g_j \le C_{ij} \;\; \text{for all } i,j, \]

which has an irresistible reading. Imagine a shipping contractor who offers to collect a unit at bin \(i\) for a fee \(f_i\) and deliver it at bin \(j\) for \(g_j\). The constraint says the contractor can never charge more than it would cost you to move the mass yourself. Duality says that, at the optimum, the contractor’s best total take equals your cheapest shipping bill exactly. When \(C\) is a metric this specialises to the Kantorovich–Rubinstein form, \(\operatorname{OT}_C = \sup_{\text{1-Lipschitz } f} \sum_i f_i (p_i - q_i)\) — the form a Wasserstein GAN critic approximates.

3. The cost function is the modelling decision

Here is the point of the whole tutorial. The bins, the histograms, and the constraints are fixed. Change only the price list \(C\) and you get a different optimal plan, a different total, and — this is the part that surprises people — a different kind of geometry on the space of histograms.

Four price lists, all built from the bin index distance \(d = |i-j|\):

\(C_{ij}\) Name Optimal total is Plan
\(\lvert i - j\rvert\) distance on a line \(W_1\), the Earth Mover’s Distance monotone (ties possible)
\((i-j)^2\) squared distance \(W_2^2\) (its square root is \(W_2\)) monotone, uniquely
\(\mathbf{1}[i \ne j]\) all-or-nothing the total variation distance order need not be kept
\(\sqrt{\lvert i-j \rvert}\) concave in distance no standard name common mass stays; residue may cross

Three of these deserve a proof sketch, because each is a small surprise.

All-or-nothing gives total variation. If every move costs the same regardless of distance, the only thing worth optimising is how much you move at all. You can leave \(\min(p_i, q_i)\) in place in each bin, and everything else must move, so

\[ \operatorname{OT}_{\mathbf{1}[i \ne j]}(p,q) = 1 - \sum_i \min(p_i, q_i) = \tfrac12 \sum_i |p_i - q_i| = \mathrm{TV}(p,q). \]

Total variation is optimal transport with a cost that has no sense of distance — which is exactly why TV is so brutal a distance between distributions with disjoint supports, and why generative modelling reaches for Wasserstein instead.

Distance-on-a-line gives the area between the CDFs. With bins ordered on a line, the optimum is the monotone (quantile) coupling, and

\[ W_1(p,q) \;=\; \sum_{k} \big| F_p(k) - F_q(k) \big| , \]

the total gap between the two cumulative distributions. This is the discrete image of the inverse-CDF transport that The Map, Proved builds in the continuum — the finite analogue of the same quantile coupling. Not of the same map: the discrete monotone coupling generally has to split a source bin across two targets, which §4 turns into a theorem.

Convex costs need not cross; concave costs may have to. State this carefully, because the folklore version is false. Convexity of \(c\) alone is not enough: \(c(r) = (r-1)^2\) is convex, and with \(p = q = (\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\) on two adjacent bins the order-preserving plan costs \(1\) while swapping costs \(0\). The condition that works is convexity of the displacement cost \(z \mapsto c(|z|)\) — for which it suffices that \(c\) be nondecreasing and convex with \(c(0) = 0\), as all four price lists above are. Then an order-preserving optimum always exists; if \(c\) is strictly convex, crossings are strictly suboptimal and the monotone coupling is the unique optimum on distinct ordered locations. For \(c(r) = r\), crossing plans can tie.

For concave \(c\) the picture changes: an increasing concave \(c\) with \(c(0)=0\) makes \(c(|i-j|)\) itself a metric, so some optimum leaves all common mass on the diagonal — but that much follows from the triangle inequality, not from concavity. What concavity adds is that after the common mass is cancelled, one long jump can beat two short ones, so a crossing plan can be strictly cheaper. There is no universal rule for where the residue goes; it depends on \(p\) and \(q\). The demo reports which case you are in, by cost rather than by inspection.

When is the answer a distance?

Not always. If \(C\) is itself a metric on the ground set (\(C_{ij}=0\) exactly when \(i=j\), symmetric, triangle inequality), then \(\operatorname{OT}_C\) is a metric on the simplex of histograms. Definiteness matters: drop it and \(C \equiv 0\) passes the other two conditions while making \(\operatorname{OT}_C\) identically zero — a pseudometric, not a metric — this is the \(W_1\)/EMD case, and it is what earns EMD the word “distance.” Drop any of those conditions and you may keep a useful number while losing the guarantees: \((i-j)^2\) is not a metric on bins, and correspondingly \(W_2^2\) does not satisfy the triangle inequality — only its square root \(W_2\) does. A cost with \(C_{ii} \neq 0\) will not even vanish when \(p = q\).

The demo reports totals under all four price lists at once. Their magnitudes are not comparable — different units, and scaling \(C\) scales the answer. What is comparable is the plan each induces and the ordering each imposes on pairs of histograms.

4. Plans versus maps: Kantorovich versus Monge

Monge asked the question first, in 1781, and asked a harder version of it. He wanted a map: a rule \(T\) sending each source location to a single destination, with total cost \(\sum_i p_i\, C_{i, T(i)}\). Kantorovich’s 1942 relaxation allows mass at one location to split across several destinations — that is what the table \(\gamma\) permits and a function \(T\) does not.

The relaxation is not a technicality; without it the problem can have no solution at all. Take \(p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0, 0)\) and \(q = (\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13, 0)\). No map works: bin \(A\) holds mass \(\tfrac12\), every target bin wants at most \(\tfrac13\), so \(A\)’s mass must be split. A plan handles this without comment; a map cannot exist.

This is the discrete shadow of the atomic obstruction in Generative Models as Transport: a deterministic map cannot split an atom. Generation from an atomless prior into an atomic target is fine; the obstruction is having to split an indivisible source atom, which is what the example above does. Kantorovich’s plans are the repair, and they are why the modern theory is stated in terms of couplings rather than maps.

5. From bins to densities: what replaces the cost, and the transporter

Now let the bins dissolve. Replace the finite ground set by \(\mathbb{R}^d\), the histograms by densities \(p\) and \(q\), and ask the same question. A natural first thought is that the change-of-variables formula is what defines transport here: if \(T\) is a smooth invertible map pushing \(p\) onto \(q\), then

\[ p(x) \;=\; q\big(T(x)\big)\, \big|\det \nabla T(x)\big| . \]

That formula is indeed central — but it is worth being precise about which role it plays, because this is the most common confusion in the subject.

The Jacobian is the constraint, not the cost

Look back at §1. The discrete problem had two separate ingredients:

  1. Constraints — row sums \(p\), column sums \(q\): “\(\gamma\) is a coupling.”
  2. Objective — \(\sum \gamma_{ij} C_{ij}\): “and it is the cheapest one.”

In the continuum the Jacobian equation above is ingredient 1, and nothing else. It is precisely the statement \(T_\# p = q\) — this map is a valid transport — written in densities. It is the continuous form of “the row sums and column sums come out right.”

It says nothing whatever about cost. Just as many couplings satisfy the marginal constraints, the Jacobian equation is typically satisfied by many maps — always by many measurable transports, and in \(d \ge 2\) usually by many smooth ones, since composing with any \(p\)-preserving diffeomorphism gives another. (In \(d=1\) it is more rigid: a diffeomorphism must be monotone, so for positive continuous densities there is one increasing solution and one decreasing one.) Wherever several exist, the Jacobian equation expresses no preference among them. You still need to name a price.

One caution before the table. The general continuous object is a coupling \(\gamma\), exactly as in the discrete case, and its constraints are that its two marginals are \(p\) and \(q\). The Jacobian equation is the constraint only for the Monge special case, where \(\gamma = (\mathrm{id}, T)_\#\,p\,dx\) is concentrated on the graph of a map — and even then the pointwise density form requires \(T\) to be a smooth enough diffeomorphism. Read the middle column below as “the Kantorovich constraint, with the Monge form in parentheses.”

So the dictionary is:

Discrete (this tutorial) Continuous, static Continuous, dynamic
bins \(i, j\) points \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}^d\) a path of densities \(p_t\)
\(p_i\), \(q_j\) densities \(p\), \(q\) endpoints \(p_0 = p\), \(p_1 = q\)
plan \(\gamma_{ij} \ge 0\) coupling measure \(\gamma(dx,dy)\) density path \(p_t\) with velocity \(v_t\)
row/column sums \((\pi_1)_\#\gamma = p\,dx\), \((\pi_2)_\#\gamma = q\,dy\) continuity equation \(\partial_t p_t + \nabla\!\cdot(p_t v_t) = 0\), with \(p_0 = p\), \(p_1 = q\)
cost matrix \(C_{ij}\) cost function \(c(x,y)\) kinetic energy density \(\lVert v_t(x)\rVert^2\)
\(\sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij}C_{ij}\) \(\iint c(x,y)\, d\gamma\) \(\int_0^1\!\!\int \lVert v_t\rVert^2 p_t \,dx\,dt\)
min-cost flow Monge–Kantorovich problem Benamou–Brenier problem

Three consequences, all of them load-bearing for this course.

The transporter is a velocity field. In the dynamic (Benamou–Brenier) form,

\[ W_2^2(p,q) \;=\; \min_{(p_t, v_t)} \int_0^1\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \lVert v_t(x)\rVert^2 \, p_t(x)\,dx\,dt \quad\text{subject to}\quad \begin{cases} \partial_t p_t + \nabla\!\cdot(p_t v_t) = 0,\\[2pt] p_0 = p, \quad p_1 = q, \end{cases} \]

for \(p, q \in \mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)\), with the continuity equation read distributionally. The endpoint conditions are not decoration: without them the minimum is \(0\), achieved by standing still. The unknown is a density path together with its velocity field — not a matrix, not a map — and the map is recovered as the flow of that field when the field is regular enough to have one.

The continuity equation is the infinitesimal Jacobian equation. For a spatially Lipschitz \(v_t\) with a well-defined flow \(\dot\Phi_t = v_t(\Phi_t)\),

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\log\det\nabla\Phi_t = (\nabla\!\cdot v_t)\circ\Phi_t, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad p_t\big(\Phi_t(x)\big)\,\det\nabla\Phi_t(x) = p_0(x), \]

the second form being the Jacobian equation of §5 with a clock attached. This is the kind of object that flow matching regresses onto and that the probability-flow ODE integrates — though note that neither solves this minimum-action problem: they produce a velocity field consistent with a prescribed density path, which as tutorial 10 insists is a weaker thing than selecting the Benamou–Brenier geodesic. Learning a velocity field is learning a transporter; making it the cheapest transporter is a further demand.

Quadratic cost has a canonical answer. For \(c(x,y) = \tfrac12\lVert x-y\rVert^2\) with \(p\,dx, q\,dy \in \mathcal{P}_2(\mathbb{R}^d)\) and the source absolutely continuous, Brenier’s theorem (1991) says the optimal map is the gradient of a convex function, \(T = \nabla\varphi\), uniquely \(p\)-almost everywhere. Under further regularity making \(\varphi\) twice differentiable and \(\nabla\varphi\) suitably invertible — Brenier alone does not supply this, and in general the equation is read in a weak (Aleksandrov) sense — substituting into the Jacobian equation gives the Monge–Ampère equation

\[ p(x) \;=\; q\big(\nabla\varphi(x)\big)\, \det\big(\nabla^2\varphi(x)\big), \]

the constraint and the cost finally in one place. Note what each contributed: the Jacobian equation supplied the equation, and the cost supplied the restriction to gradients of convex functions — the higher-dimensional replacement for “monotone,” and the reason \(W_2\) generalises the quantile map.

Maximum likelihood is not a transport cost. A normalizing flow trains on

\[ \log p_{\text{model}}(x) = \log q\big(T(x)\big) + \log\big|\det \nabla T(x)\big| , \]

and the Jacobian term sits squarely in the objective — which invites the reading that flows are doing optimal transport. They are not. That term is change-of-variables volume bookkeeping, the price of keeping the model density normalized; it does not price displacement. Two encoders \(T\) and \(T \circ S\), where \(S\) is a \(p\)-preserving diffeomorphism, induce the same model density and so are worth exactly the same likelihood, while having wildly different transport costs. Maximum likelihood identifies the density; it does not select a map. To get the optimal map you have to add a transport cost on purpose — which is what OT-regularised flows and minibatch-OT couplings in flow matching do, and why they straighten trajectories that likelihood training leaves tangled.

6. Computing it, at realistic sizes

The \(4 \times 4\) lab is solved exactly by min-cost flow. Real problems are bigger, and three routes matter.

  • Network simplex / Hungarian. Exact, and the right answer for moderate \(n\). Roughly \(O(n^3 \log n)\) for the transportation problem; the Hungarian algorithm is the \(O(n^3)\) special case when both histograms are uniform with equal atoms, where an optimum can be chosen to be a permutation (ties admit mixtures too).
  • Entropic regularisation (Sinkhorn). Add \(\varepsilon \sum_{ij}\gamma_{ij} (\log \gamma_{ij} - 1)\) to the objective. The problem becomes strictly convex, and its solution has the form \(\gamma = \operatorname{diag}(u)\, K \operatorname{diag}(v)\) with \(K = e^{-C/\varepsilon}\), found by alternately rescaling rows and columns. Each iteration is a matrix–vector product: \(O(n^2)\), GPU-friendly, and differentiable, which is why it dominates in machine learning (Cuturi, 2013). The regulariser interpolates between the two couplings we have already met: as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) it recovers the sharp optimal plan, and as \(\varepsilon \to \infty\) it relaxes to the independent coupling \(p q^{\top}\) — the “shipping blind” baseline reported in the demo.
  • Sliced approximations. Project to one dimension, where §3’s sorted-order formula applies, and average the resulting 1-D costs over many projections. Cheap and differentiable — but note that sliced Wasserstein is a different metric, not full Wasserstein recovered in the limit of enough projections. (Low-rank OT is a separate family of methods, factorising the plan rather than the space.) One dimension is special here not because it is the only solvable case — finite OT is exactly solvable in any dimension by LP — but because it is the one with a closed-form order-statistic answer.

7. Where this shows up

  • Earth Mover’s Distance. Rubner, Tomasi and Guibas introduced EMD for image retrieval: represent an image by a signature of colour clusters, let \(C\) be distance in colour space, and compare images by transport cost. Robust to the binning artefacts that wreck bin-by-bin distances like \(\chi^2\) — because EMD knows that adjacent bins are nearly the same, and TV does not.
  • Word Mover’s Distance. Kusner and coauthors applied the same idea to text: a document is a histogram over its vocabulary, and \(C_{ij}\) is the Euclidean distance between word embeddings \(i\) and \(j\). Document distance is then the cheapest way to transport one bag of words onto another. This is the “fixed vocabulary on both sides” setting exactly — and it shows what the cost matrix buys you: semantics enters only through \(C\).
  • Generative modelling — but at three genuinely different levels, and it is worth not blurring them.
    • Comparing endpoint laws. Wasserstein GANs approximate a Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual objective with a Lipschitz-constrained neural critic (Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017). FID scores samples with the closed-form \(W_2^2\) — the squared Fréchet distance — between Gaussians fitted to Inception features (Heusel et al., 2017). Neither of these chooses the generator’s noise-to-data coupling; they measure how far apart two endpoint distributions are.
    • Choosing a coupling. Minibatch-OT flow matching genuinely does select which noise sample is paired with which data sample, and reports simpler, straighter learned flows as a result (Pooladian et al., 2023; Tong et al., 2024). It does not thereby certify the population OT map.
    • The course’s thesis. “Generative modelling is transport” is a statement about the third level, and tutorial 10 is careful about how much it buys: a transport representation is available for almost anything, so exhibiting one explains less than it appears to. Using OT to score a model and using OT to build one are different activities.
Key takeaway

Transport has two ingredients and they do different jobs. The constraints say which plans are legal: row and column sums on a finite ground set, the Jacobian equation for a map between densities, the continuity equation for a flow. They never single out an answer, because couplings are plentiful — the lesson of many mountains, same shadows. The cost is what selects, and it is a modelling choice, not a mathematical given. Choose \(\mathbf{1}[i \ne j]\) and you have total variation; choose \(|i-j|\) and you have Earth Mover; choose embedding distance and you have Word Mover; choose \(\lVert v\rVert^2\) along a path and you have the velocity field this course spends the rest of its time learning.

Exercises

  1. Verify the total-variation identity directly: show \(1 - \sum_i \min(p_i,q_i) = \tfrac12\sum_i |p_i - q_i|\) for any two PMFs, and explain why the plan “leave \(\min(p_i,q_i)\) in place” is optimal for the all-or-nothing cost. Confirm against the demo’s readout.
  2. Take \(p = (0.4, 0.1, 0.1, 0.4)\) and \(q = (0.1, 0.4, 0.4, 0.1)\). Compute the optimal plan and cost by hand for \(C_{ij} = |i-j|\), using the CDF formula. Then predict, before clicking, which routes the demo will use.
  3. Show that the independent coupling \(\gamma_{ij} = p_i q_j\) is always feasible. Then show that if \(C_{ij} = a_i + b_j\) for any vectors \(a, b\) — an additively separable price list — then every coupling has the same cost \(\sum_i a_i p_i + \sum_j b_j q_j\), so the independent one is optimal along with all the others. (\(C \equiv 0\) is the special case \(a = b = 0\).)
  4. Prove that a vertex of the \(m \times n\) transportation polytope has at most \(m + n - 1\) non-zero entries. (Hint: a cycle among the used routes lets you push mass around it in either direction at no change to the marginals.)
  5. A triangle-inequality violation needs three histograms. On three bins with \(C_{ij} = (i-j)^2\), take \(\mu = \delta_A\), \(\nu = \delta_B\), \(\rho = \delta_C\) and show \(\operatorname{OT}_C(\mu,\rho) = 4 > 1 + 1 = \operatorname{OT}_C(\mu,\nu) + \operatorname{OT}_C(\nu,\rho)\). Then explain why \(W_2 = \sqrt{\operatorname{OT}_C}\) is a metric.
  6. (Concave costs.) Take \(C_{ij} = \sqrt{|i-j|}\) with \(p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0, 0)\) and \(q = (0, \tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0)\). Show the order-preserving plan costs \(1\), while leaving \(B\) in place and sending \(A \to C\) costs \(\sqrt2/2 \approx 0.707\). Describe in words what the cheaper plan prefers. Then explain why a convex displacement cost permits the uncrossing exchange that rules this out, and why strict convexity makes crossings strictly worse.
  7. (The Jacobian is not the cost.) Let \(p = q = \mathcal{N}(0, I_2)\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Show that both \(T = \mathrm{id}\) and \(T = R_\theta\), any rotation, satisfy the change-of-variables equation and push \(p\) to \(q\). Using the convention \(c(x,y) = \tfrac12\lVert x - y\rVert^2\) of §5, show the cost of \(R_\theta\) is \(2(1 - \cos\theta) = 4\sin^2(\theta/2)\), minimised uniquely at \(\theta = 0\). Which does population maximum likelihood prefer, and which does quadratic optimal transport prefer?
  8. (Dynamic form.) For \(p = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)\) in one dimension, verify that the pair \(p_t = \mathcal{N}(t\mu, 1)\) with the constant velocity field \(v_t(x) = \mu\) satisfies the continuity equation and both endpoint conditions. Compute its action \(\int_0^1\!\int \mu^2 p_t = \mu^2\) and check it against \(W_2^2(p,q) = \mu^2\). Why is it plausible that no other admissible pair does better?
