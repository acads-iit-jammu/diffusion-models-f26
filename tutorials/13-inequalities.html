Inequalities Worth Knowing

A standalone tutorial, and a short one. Five inequalities that between them justify most of the “and therefore it concentrates” steps in this course. Each is stated, proved in a few lines, and given the job it actually does. The last two sections are the payoff: how a bound that knows only the mean and variance already forces data to cluster, and where that bound is far too pessimistic — a gap the Advanced tutorial then turns into an argument for the Gaussian.

1. Markov: the one everything else comes from

For a non-negative random variable \(X \ge 0\) and any \(a > 0\),

\[ \boxed{\;\mathbb{P}(X \ge a) \;\le\; \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}.\;} \]

Proof. Since \(X \ge 0\), we have \(X \ge a\,\mathbf{1}\{X \ge a\}\) pointwise — check the two cases: if \(X \ge a\) the right side is \(a \le X\); otherwise it is \(0 \le X\). Take expectations and use monotonicity: \(\mathbb{E}[X] \ge a\,\mathbb{P}(X \ge a)\). \(\square\)

That is the entire proof, and it is worth noticing how little it uses: only non-negativity and the mean. In exchange, the bound is usually weak. Its value is that it needs almost nothing, and that everything below is a corollary of it applied to a cleverly chosen non-negative quantity.

2. Chebyshev: mean and variance force concentration

Apply Markov to \((X - \mu)^2\), which is non-negative, with threshold \(k^2\sigma^2\):

\[ \mathbb{P}\big(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma\big) = \mathbb{P}\big((X-\mu)^2 \ge k^2\sigma^2\big) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]}{k^2\sigma^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2\sigma^2}, \]

so for any random variable with finite variance and any \(k > 0\),

\[ \boxed{\;\mathbb{P}\big(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma\big) \;\le\; \frac{1}{k^{2}}.\;} \]

Read what this says. It assumes nothing about the shape of the distribution — not symmetry, not unimodality, not a density. Knowing only the mean and the variance, at most \(1/4\) of the mass can sit two standard deviations or more from the mean, at most \(1/9\) beyond three. Variance is not merely a summary of spread; it is a budget that limits how much mass can be far out.

\(k\) Chebyshev bound \(1/k^2\) Actual, if Gaussian
1 1.000 0.317
2 0.250 0.0455
3 0.111 0.0027
4 0.0625 0.0000633

The right column is the point of the Advanced tutorial. Chebyshev is tight — there are distributions attaining it — but for the Gaussian it is wildly pessimistic, off by a factor of nearly \(1000\) at \(k=4\). Something much stronger is true for the shapes we actually meet, and asking why leads straight to the normal distribution.

3. Jensen: which way convexity bends an average

For a convex \(\varphi\) and integrable \(X\),

\[ \boxed{\;\varphi\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big) \;\le\; \mathbb{E}\big[\varphi(X)\big].\;} \]

For concave \(\varphi\) the inequality reverses.

Proof. Convexity means the graph lies above each of its tangent lines. Take the tangent at \(m = \mathbb{E}[X]\): there is a slope \(c\) with \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(m) + c(x - m)\) for all \(x\). Take expectations; the linear term has expectation zero. \(\square\)

Three uses you will meet:

  • \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] \ge (\mathbb{E}X)^2\), i.e. variance is non-negative — Jensen with \(\varphi(x)=x^2\).
  • \(\log \mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log X]\) — \(\log\) is concave. This single line is what makes the ELBO a lower bound on the log-evidence.
  • \(\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) \ge 0\), with equality only when \(p = q\) — Jensen applied to \(-\log\). Every “this divergence is non-negative, so minimising it makes sense” argument is this.

4. Cauchy–Schwarz, and correlation being bounded

\[ \boxed{\;\big|\mathbb{E}[XY]\big| \;\le\; \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X^2]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^2]}.\;} \]

Proof. For every \(t\), \(0 \le \mathbb{E}[(X - tY)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2t\,\mathbb{E}[XY] + t^2\mathbb{E}[Y^2]\). A quadratic in \(t\) that is never negative has non-positive discriminant: \(4(\mathbb{E}[XY])^2 - 4\mathbb{E}[X^2]\mathbb{E}[Y^2] \le 0\). \(\square\)

Applied to centred variables this says exactly that the correlation coefficient lies in \([-1,1]\), with \(|\rho| = 1\) precisely when \(Y\) is an affine function of \(X\). That is the fact underlying the constraint \(|\rho| < 1\) everywhere in the multivariate Gaussian reference — at \(|\rho| = 1\) the covariance matrix is singular and the ellipse collapses to a line.

5. The union bound

For any events \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) — no independence required —

\[ \boxed{\;\mathbb{P}\Big(\bigcup_i A_i\Big) \;\le\; \sum_i \mathbb{P}(A_i).\;} \]

Proof. Countable subadditivity, which follows from countable additivity by disjointifying: replace \(A_i\) by \(A_i \setminus \bigcup_{j<i}A_j\), which is contained in \(A_i\) and hence no more probable. \(\square\)

Crude, and indispensable. It is how you go from “each of \(d\) coordinates is unlikely to be extreme” to “no coordinate is extreme”, at a cost of a factor \(d\). In high dimension that factor is the whole difficulty: with \(d = 784\), an event of probability \(10^{-3}\) per coordinate gives no useful bound at all across coordinates, which is one honest way to see why high-dimensional density estimation is hard.

6. Putting Markov to work: the weak law

The inequalities are not decoration. Here is a complete theorem in three lines.

Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\), and let \(\bar X_n\) be their average. Then \(\mathbb{E}[\bar X_n] = \mu\) and, because variances of independent variables add, \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2/n\). Chebyshev gives, for any \(\varepsilon > 0\),

\[ \mathbb{P}\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon\big) \;\le\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\; 0 . \]

That is the weak law of large numbers, proved entirely from Markov. Note what it does and does not give: it says the average concentrates, but it says nothing about the shape of the fluctuation around \(\mu\). Rescaling by \(\sqrt{n}\) instead of \(n\) and asking what shape survives is the central limit theorem — and the answer being Gaussian, always, is the subject of the next tutorial.

Key takeaway

Markov is the whole toolkit: it needs only non-negativity, and Chebyshev, the weak law and most concentration results are Markov applied to a well-chosen non-negative quantity. What you buy with more assumptions is sharpness — Chebyshev’s \(1/k^2\) decay becomes the Gaussian’s \(e^{-k^2/2}\) once you know more than two moments, and closing that gap is exactly the question the Advanced track takes up.

Exercises

  1. Show Markov is tight: find a non-negative \(X\) and an \(a\) for which \(\mathbb{P}(X \ge a) = \mathbb{E}[X]/a\) exactly. (Two atoms suffice.)
  2. Show Chebyshev is tight at \(k = 2\): construct a distribution with mean \(0\), variance \(1\), and exactly \(1/4\) of its mass at distance \(\ge 2\).
  3. Why does Markov require \(X \ge 0\)? Give a random variable with \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\) for which the conclusion fails without it.
  4. Use Jensen to show \(\mathbb{E}[1/X] \ge 1/\mathbb{E}[X]\) for a positive random variable, and give an example where the inequality is strict.
  5. Use Cauchy–Schwarz to prove \(|\rho| \le 1\), and characterise exactly when \(|\rho| = 1\).
  6. A vector has \(d\) coordinates, each of which exceeds \(3\sigma\) with probability at most \(0.0027\). Bound the probability that some coordinate does, for \(d = 10\) and \(d = 784\). At what \(d\) does the union bound stop saying anything?
  7. Compare the Chebyshev bound at \(k=3\) with the true Gaussian tail. By what factor is Chebyshev too pessimistic? Now do the same at \(k=5\) and comment on how the gap grows.
