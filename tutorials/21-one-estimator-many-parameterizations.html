One Estimator, Many Parameterizations

ImportantDraft

This note isolates one claim that is easily blurred in standard introductions to diffusion models: a network trained to “predict noise” does not predict an unrevealed realization of randomness. Under squared loss it estimates a conditional mean, expressed in a particular coordinate system.

Standalone companion to The Probability-Flow ODE. That note uses the score to construct a sampler; this one studies what the different training heads estimate. The two arguments are logically separate.

The claim

Fix a noise level \(t\) and begin with the minimal linear observation model

\[ \boxed{\;X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\varepsilon,\qquad \alpha_t,\sigma_t\ \text{known}.\;} \]

Assume the conditional means below exist and \(\sigma_t>0\); require also \(\alpha_t>0\) when inverting the relation. The standard diffusion instance adds \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) independent of \(X_0\), but the first identity does not need either Gaussianity or independence.

The phrase predict the injected noise suggests that a network recovers the particular random draw \(\varepsilon\). It does not. If the network is trained by squared error, its unrestricted population optimum is

\[ \varepsilon_t^\star(x) =\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_t=x,t]. \]

This is a deterministic function of the network input. More strongly, it is an affine transformation of the familiar posterior-mean denoiser

\[ m_t(x)=\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x,t]. \]

Taking the conditional expectation of the corruption equation gives

\[ x=\alpha_t m_t(x)+\sigma_t\varepsilon_t^\star(x), \]

and hence, whenever \(\alpha_t,\sigma_t>0\),

\[ \boxed{ \varepsilon_t^\star(x) =\frac{x-\alpha_t m_t(x)}{\sigma_t}, \qquad m_t(x)=\frac{x-\sigma_t\varepsilon_t^\star(x)}{\alpha_t}. } \]

The two estimators contain exactly the same information. “Noise prediction” and posterior-mean denoising are not rival explanations of what the population regressor learns; they are two coordinate systems for the same conditional expectation.

Scope of the identity

The affine identity above uses only:

  1. the linear observation equation;
  2. known coefficients \(\alpha_t\) and \(\sigma_t\);
  3. existence of the relevant conditional expectations.

Squared-error training is a separate assumption: it is what makes these conditional means the population targets of trained regression heads, not what makes the affine identity true.

It does not require a density for \(X_0\), Gaussian data, a two-point support, a closed-form posterior, or even independence of \(X_0\) and \(\varepsilon\). It applies in any dimension and to discrete, continuous, singular, or mixed data laws. The squared-loss decomposition below additionally assumes finite second moments. Gaussianity and independence of the corruption noise become important when connecting the estimator to the marginal score; neither is needed for the affine equivalence between the two conditional means.

The identity is therefore not special to the two-point example. That example is special only because the posterior mean is explicit. For independent Gaussian corruption and \(X_0\) uniform on \(\{-1,+1\}\), For \(X_0\) uniform on \(\{-1,+1\}\),

\[ m_t(x)=\tanh\!\left(\frac{\alpha_t x}{\sigma_t^2}\right), \]

so

\[ \varepsilon_t^\star(x) =\frac{x-\alpha_t\tanh(\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2)}{\sigma_t}. \]

The closed form makes the general fact visible: the fitted output is a field, not a fresh noise draw.

Why squared loss learns a mean

Let \(h(X_t,t)\) be any predictor. Conditional expectation gives the orthogonal decomposition

\[ \varepsilon =\underbrace{\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_t,t]}_{\varepsilon_t^\star(X_t)} +\underbrace{\left(\varepsilon- \mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_t,t]\right)}_{R_t}, \qquad \mathbb E[R_t\mid X_t,t]=0. \]

Consequently,

\[ \boxed{ \mathbb E\|h(X_t,t)-\varepsilon\|^2 =\mathbb E\|h(X_t,t)-\varepsilon_t^\star(X_t)\|^2 +\mathbb E\|R_t\|^2. } \]

Only the first term can be reduced by learning. The second is irreducible. Thus the random sample \(\varepsilon\) is the training label, while \(\varepsilon_t^\star\) is the function being estimated — the population optimum that a fitted \(\varepsilon_\theta\) approximates. Calling all three “the noise” is the source of the confusion.

The residual uncertainty is also an affine image of uncertainty about the clean sample. Rearranging the observation equation gives the pathwise identity

\[ \varepsilon-\varepsilon_t^\star(X_t) =-\frac{\alpha_t}{\sigma_t}\bigl(X_0-m_t(X_t)\bigr), \]

and therefore, after conditioning on \((X_t,t)\),

\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon\mid X_t,t) =\frac{\alpha_t^2}{\sigma_t^2} \operatorname{Cov}(X_0\mid X_t,t). } \]

What remains unpredictable about the noise is precisely what remains uncertain about the clean sample, expressed in different units. Neither this covariance identity nor the affine mean identity needs Gaussianity or independence.

Gaussian corruption: the score is the same field again

For Gaussian corruption,

\[ p_t(x\mid x_0)= \mathcal N(x;\alpha_t x_0,\sigma_t^2I), \qquad \nabla_x\log p_t(x\mid x_0) =-\frac{x-\alpha_t x_0}{\sigma_t^2}. \]

Averaging the conditional score over the posterior of \(X_0\) gives the marginal score — the denoising-score identity derived in the companion note1:

\[ s_t(x)=\nabla_x\log p_t(x) =\frac{\alpha_t m_t(x)-x}{\sigma_t^2} =-\frac{\varepsilon_t^\star(x)}{\sigma_t}. \]

Thus the common population estimands are connected by invertible affine maps:

Name of head Population-optimal output
clean-sample prediction \(m_t(x)=\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x,t]\)
noise prediction \(\varepsilon_t^\star(x)=(x-\alpha_t m_t(x))/\sigma_t\)
score prediction \(s_t(x)=(\alpha_t m_t(x)-x)/\sigma_t^2\)

The score identity is specifically Gaussian. The mean-to-noise identity is not.

The \(v\) parameterization

For any linear corruption, define the \(v\) target introduced for diffusion-model parameterization by Salimans and Ho2:

\[ v_t=\alpha_t\varepsilon-\sigma_tX_0. \]

Then

\[ \begin{bmatrix}X_t\\v_t\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_t&\sigma_t\\ -\sigma_t&\alpha_t \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}X_0\\\varepsilon\end{bmatrix}. \]

When \(\alpha_t^2+\sigma_t^2=1\), as in a variance-preserving schedule, the matrix is a rotation. Its population-optimal regression target is

\[ v_t^\star(x) =\mathbb E[v_t\mid X_t=x,t] =\alpha_t\varepsilon_t^\star(x)-\sigma_t m_t(x). \]

So \(v\)-prediction is a fourth representation of the same conditional-mean information. Under the VP normalization the inverse rotation gives

\[ \boxed{ m_t(x)=\alpha_t x-\sigma_t v_t^\star(x), \qquad \varepsilon_t^\star(x)=\sigma_t x+\alpha_t v_t^\star(x). } \]

If \(\alpha_t=\cos\phi_t\) and \(\sigma_t=\sin\phi_t\), \(v_t\) is also the derivative of the interpolation with respect to the angle \(\phi_t\). The companion note uses \(v\) for the probability-flow ODE velocity; here \(v\) is reserved for the prediction target, so call the ODE velocity \(u_t\). From the ODE derived there, for the VP SDE,

\[ u_t(x)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)\bigl(x+s_t(x)\bigr) =\dot\phi_t\,v_t^\star(x), \qquad \dot\phi_t=\frac{\beta(t)\alpha_t}{2\sigma_t}. \]

Thus, at interior times with \(\sigma_t>0\), the conditional mean of the \(v\) target is the probability-flow velocity after the deterministic time change \(t\mapsto\phi_t\). The derivative \(\dot\phi_t\) generally diverges as \(\sigma_t\downarrow0\), so this is not a regular reparameterization at the clean endpoint.

Equivalent estimands, different training problems

The affine equivalence is a population statement. It does not imply that all parameterizations train equally well with a finite network, finite data, and a particular optimizer.

Coordinate conversion makes the endpoint conditioning visible. An error \(\delta_\varepsilon\) in a noise head produces

\[ \delta_{x_0}=-\frac{\sigma_t}{\alpha_t}\delta_\varepsilon, \]

which is amplified near the high-noise endpoint. In the other direction,

\[ \delta_\varepsilon=-\frac{\alpha_t}{\sigma_t}\delta_{x_0}, \]

which is amplified near the clean endpoint.

These factors alone do not prove that one head is easier to learn: the same affine map rescales signal, approximation error, and the irreducible conditional variance together. A comparison becomes meaningful only after specifying the error consumed downstream — score, denoised sample, reverse mean, or velocity — and the approximation error achieved by a fixed finite model in each coordinate system. Parameterization matters because architectures, loss weights, numerical precision, and optimizers are not invariant to these time-dependent rescalings, not because the Bayes estimands contain different information.

Why did \(\varepsilon\)-prediction become the default?

If the heads contain the same population information, a preference among them can come only from approximation, optimization, loss weighting, downstream numerics, or historical convention — not from one head containing additional population information. The reasons below mix documented design history with a retrospective conditioning analysis; they should not be mistaken for a single causal story.

A normalized supervised target

At every noise level the sampled label has the same known marginal law,

\[ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I). \]

Its coordinate scale does not inherit image brightness, dataset covariance, or the changing signal-to-noise ratio. Direct \(x_0\) targets retain the structure and scale of the data, while conditional-score targets \(-\varepsilon/\sigma_t\) diverge in magnitude as \(\sigma_t\downarrow0\). Predicting \(\varepsilon\) avoids that small-noise target explosion. Earlier noise-conditional score models used an explicitly noise-scaled output for the same normalization reason.3

This statement concerns the sampled labels, not the fitted function. The population-optimal output remains \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_t=x,t]\), whose distribution and difficulty do vary with \(t\) and with the data law.

A useful implicit weighting over noise levels

Because \(\varepsilon_\theta=-\sigma_t s_\theta\) and \(\varepsilon^\star=-\sigma_t s_t\),

\[ \|\varepsilon_\theta-\varepsilon^\star\|^2 =\sigma_t^2\|s_\theta-s_t\|^2. \]

An unweighted \(\varepsilon\) objective is therefore a score objective weighted by \(\sigma_t^2\). Relative to an unweighted score objective, it gives less weight to the small-\(\sigma_t\) region in which conditional score labels have scale \(1/\sigma_t\). Equivalently, a score loss with weight \(\lambda(t)\) becomes a noise loss with weight \(\lambda(t)/\sigma_t^2\). The familiar “simple” DDPM objective is consequently not just a relabeling: it chooses a consequential weighting across noise levels. This is best read as an analysis of the objective, not by itself as a historical account of why it was adopted.

Direct use in the DDPM reverse mean

For a discrete DDPM, write the one-step retention as \(a_k\), its cumulative product as \(\bar a_k=\prod_{j=1}^k a_j\), and \(X_k=\sqrt{\bar a_k}X_0+\sqrt{1-\bar a_k}\,\varepsilon\). The exact posterior \(q(x_{k-1}\mid x_k,x_0)\) has a mean affine in \(x_0\). Averaging over \(X_0\mid X_k=x\) therefore makes the exact conditional reverse mean a fifth coordinate of the same estimator:

\[ \mathbb E[X_{k-1}\mid X_k=x] =\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_k}} \left(x-\frac{1-a_k}{\sqrt{1-\bar a_k}}\, \varepsilon_k^\star(x)\right). \]

The full reverse conditional need not be Gaussian, but this conditional-mean identity is exact. The DDPM Gaussian model uses the same expression with \(\varepsilon_k^\star\) replaced by \(\varepsilon_\theta\).4 This made the training target and reverse update compact: generate a known standard-normal label, fit it by MSE, and insert the output into the reverse mean. Unlike the probability-flow velocity \(u_t\), this is a discrete-time conditional expectation of the previous state, not a continuous-time derivative.

Empirical success became convention

The original DDPM work arrived at \(\varepsilon\)-prediction by reparameterizing the variational reverse mean and reported its effectiveness empirically; its ablations also tie the result to the simplified objective rather than to output coordinates alone.5 Subsequent architectures, schedulers, software interfaces, and explanations standardized around it. Part of its status as the “preferred” parameterization is therefore path dependence: a successful baseline became the default language of the field. That history is evidence for usefulness, not proof of universal optimality.

It is not uniformly well-conditioned

The endpoint factors do identify where a coordinate conversion is ill-conditioned, provided we say which downstream quantity must be accurate. Near the high-noise endpoint, an apparently small \(\varepsilon\) error can become a large \(x_0\) error after multiplication by \(\sigma_t/\alpha_t\); the observation is nearly the noise, and the small difference that carries clean-signal information is difficult to resolve. Near the clean endpoint, an \(x_0\) error becomes a large \(\varepsilon\) or score error after multiplication by \(\alpha_t/\sigma_t\). Accordingly, \(\varepsilon\) coordinates resolve the small residual naturally near clean data, while \(x_0\) coordinates avoid the singular recovery of clean signal near zero SNR. The VP rotation motivates \(v\)-prediction as a more balanced coordinate system.6 EDM-style formulations go further by explicitly preconditioning the input, output, and loss rather than treating any raw head as intrinsically correct.7

The preference can therefore be summarized precisely:

\(\varepsilon\)-prediction became standard not because noise is the fundamental object being learned, but because noise coordinates give normalized sampled labels, a useful implicit timestep weighting, and a convenient reverse-step formula. These properties, empirical results, and subsequent convention made it a practical default, not a uniquely privileged statistical target.

The defensible conclusion is:

The standard prediction heads are information-equivalent at the unrestricted population optimum, but they are not necessarily optimization-equivalent in practice.

What the phrase gets wrong

“Noise prediction” is defensible as shorthand for the target used in a regression problem. It becomes misleading when read as a description of the fitted function.

  • The network does not reproduce a hidden random draw exactly; an irreducible conditional variance generally remains.
  • Its output is a deterministic function of \((x,t)\), not newly generated static.
  • The predictable component is the posterior-mean displacement from the noisy observation to the attenuated clean signal.
  • Under Gaussian corruption, that component is precisely the score in different units.

A more faithful phrase would be conditional-noise mean estimation. Better still, present \(x_0\)-, \(\varepsilon\)-, score-, and \(v\)-prediction together as alternative parameterizations of one learned conditional-mean field.
