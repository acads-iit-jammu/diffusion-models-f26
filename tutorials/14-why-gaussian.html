Why the Gaussian? Four Arguments for One Density

An Advanced tutorial. Optional: nothing in the lectures depends on it. The question it answers is the one that nags after you have used the normal distribution for a while — where does that formula actually come from? Writing \(e^{-x^2/2}\) and calling it natural is not an explanation. Four independent arguments arrive at it, each starting from a different demand, and the fact that they converge is the real reason the Gaussian is not just convenient but inevitable.

Prerequisites and where this sits

Builds on Inequalities Worth Knowing — particularly Chebyshev, which is where §1 starts — and on The Multivariate Gaussian for the \(d\)-dimensional statements in §2. The working versions of everything here are in the Foundations track; read this when you want to know why the working versions look the way they do.

1. The gap Chebyshev leaves open

Start where the Foundations tutorial stopped. Chebyshev says that for any law with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\),

\[ \mathbb{P}\big(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma\big) \le \frac{1}{k^{2}} . \]

It is a genuine theorem and it is tight — some distribution attains it for each \(k\). But look at what it costs to be that general:

\(k\) Chebyshev \(1/k^2\) Gaussian \(\mathbb{P}(|Z|\ge k)\) ratio
1 1.000 0.3173 3.2
2 0.250 0.04550 5.5
3 0.1111 0.002700 41
4 0.0625 0.0000633 987

Chebyshev’s tail decays polynomially, like \(k^{-2}\). The Gaussian’s decays like \(e^{-k^2/2}\) — not merely faster, but in a different regime altogether. By \(k=4\) the bound is a thousand times too pessimistic.

So the honest question is not “why is the Gaussian’s tail small” but: what extra assumption, beyond a finite variance, buys you \(e^{-k^2/2}\) instead of \(k^{-2}\)? Chebyshev is what you can say knowing two moments and nothing else. Each of the arguments below adds one structural demand — symmetry, or additivity, or ignorance — and each time the answer that comes back is \(e^{-x^2/2}\).

2. Symmetry plus independence, and the density falls out

This is the argument that lets you see the density rather than be handed it. It is due to Herschel (1850) and Maxwell (1860), who each wanted the distribution of errors in two dimensions.

Impose exactly two demands on a density \(f\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\):

  1. The coordinates are independent — \(f(x,y) = g(x)\,g(y)\) for some one-dimensional \(g\), the same in each coordinate.
  2. The density is rotationally symmetric — \(f(x,y)\) depends on \((x,y)\) only through the radius \(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\). Errors have no preferred direction.

Both are natural for a measurement error: the horizontal and vertical components of a miss should not influence each other, and no direction on the target should be special. Now watch what they force together.

Write the second demand as \(f(x,y) = h(x^2+y^2)\). Combining with the first,

\[ g(x)\,g(y) = h(x^2 + y^2). \]

Set \(y = 0\): \(g(x)g(0) = h(x^2)\), so \(h(u) = g(\sqrt{u})\,g(0)\). Substituting back,

\[ g(x)\,g(y) \;=\; g\big(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\big)\,g(0). \]

Define \(\varphi(u) = g(\sqrt{u})/g(0)\) for \(u \ge 0\). The relation becomes

\[ \varphi(u)\,\varphi(v) \;=\; \varphi(u+v), \]

which is Cauchy’s exponential functional equation. Its only measurable solutions are \(\varphi(u) = e^{cu}\). Therefore

\[ g(t) \;=\; g(0)\,e^{c t^{2}}, \]

and for \(g\) to be integrable we need \(c < 0\). Write \(c = -1/(2\sigma^2)\) and normalise:

\[ \boxed{\;g(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\,e^{-t^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}.\;} \]

The Gaussian, with no probabilistic input at all — only “independent coordinates” and “no preferred direction”, plus the requirement that the answer be a density.

What that argument really shows

The exponent is quadratic because the only rotation-invariant quantity built from independent coordinates is the sum of squares, and the only way to turn a sum into a product is to exponentiate. That is the whole mechanism: \(e^{-\frac12\sum x_i^2} = \prod e^{-x_i^2/2}\). Read it in reverse and it is exactly §2 of the multivariate reference: \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) factorises into independent coordinates because its exponent is a sum of squares.

It also explains the shape of the level sets. Rotational symmetry gives circles; apply a linear map and circles become ellipses, which is why every multivariate Gaussian contour is an ellipsoid and no other shape ever appears.

The Gaussian is the only density with independent coordinates and rotational symmetry. Try \(e^{-|t|}\): the product \(e^{-|x|}e^{-|y|} = e^{-(|x|+|y|)}\) has diamond-shaped level sets, not circles. It fails, and so does everything else.

3. Maximum entropy: the least committed choice

A second demand, of a completely different kind. Suppose all you are willing to assert about a quantity is its mean and its variance. Among all densities with those two moments, which should you pick?

The principled answer is: the one that assumes the least — that is, the one of maximum entropy, \(h(p) = -\int p \log p\). Committing to anything with lower entropy means smuggling in structure you have no evidence for.

The answer is the Gaussian. Let \(q = \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\) and let \(p\) be any density with the same mean and variance. Then

\[ 0 \;\le\; \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) \;=\; \int p \log \frac{p}{q} \;=\; -h(p) - \int p \log q , \]

using \(\mathrm{KL} \ge 0\), which is Jensen from the inequalities tutorial. Now the second term: because \(\log q\) is a quadratic in \(x\), the integral \(\int p \log q\) depends on \(p\) only through its mean and variance — which are the same as \(q\)’s by assumption. So \(\int p\log q = \int q \log q = -h(q)\), and the display rearranges to

\[ h(p) \;\le\; h(q), \]

with equality exactly when \(p = q\). \(\square\)

The step that makes it work is worth naming: \(\log\) of a Gaussian is a quadratic, and a quadratic only sees two moments. Fix a different pair of constraints and you get a different maximum-entropy family — fix the mean alone on \([0,\infty)\) and you get the exponential; fix nothing but a bounded range and you get the uniform. The Gaussian is the maximum-entropy answer to precisely the question “I know the mean and the variance”.

Numerically, at variance \(1\): Gaussian \(h = 1.4189\) nats, Laplace \(1.3466\), uniform \(1.2425\) — the Gaussian wins, as it must.

4. The central limit theorem: additivity

The third demand is additivity. Let \(X_1, X_2, \dots\) be i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\) — no other assumption about their distribution. Then

\[ \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \mu) \;\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{d}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1). \]

The weak law of §6 of the inequalities tutorial said the average concentrates on \(\mu\). The CLT says what the residual fluctuation looks like once you magnify it by the right amount: divide by \(n\) and everything collapses to a point; divide by \(\sqrt{n}\) and a fixed shape survives — and that shape is the Gaussian, whatever you started from.

Act III of the Gaussian Lab makes the convergence visible, and also makes visible that the rate depends heavily on the source: a symmetric one like the uniform is close by \(n=3\), while a lopsided exponential or a two-valued coin flip takes far longer. The limit is identical; only the approach differs.

Why this is an argument for the Gaussian and not merely a fact about sums. Measurement error is typically the accumulation of many small independent perturbations. So is thermal noise, and so is the pixel noise in a sensor. If a quantity is a sum of many small independent contributions and you know nothing about the individual terms, the CLT says you already know the shape of the total. That is why Gaussian noise models are so often right without anyone having checked.

And it explains stability. The Gaussian is a fixed point of adding: sum two independent Gaussians and you get a Gaussian, with variances adding. It is the only distribution with finite variance that is stable in this sense, which is why it is the limit — anything else would be changed by the very operation the limit is built from.

5. Four demands, one density

Demand Argument What forces the exponent
Only two moments known Chebyshev (§1) nothing — this is the weak baseline the others improve on
Independent coordinates, no preferred direction Herschel–Maxwell (§2) sum of squares is the only rotation-invariant form
Assume as little as possible given mean and variance maximum entropy (§3) \(\log\)-Gaussian is quadratic, so it only sees two moments
Sum of many small independent effects central limit theorem (§4) stability under addition

Four different starting points — a symmetry, an epistemic principle, a limit theorem — and one density at the end of each. That convergence is what earns the Gaussian its place. It is not that it is convenient, though it is; it is that several quite different demands each single it out uniquely.

Why this course adds Gaussian noise

Every argument above is also a reason the forward process of a diffusion model uses \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I)\) rather than anything else. It is isotropic, so no direction of image space is privileged (§2). It is the least committed choice given a noise scale (§3). It is stable under composition, so many small steps equal one big step with variances added (§4) — which is what makes \(p(x_t \mid x_0)\) available in closed form at every \(t\) instead of only step-by-step. And it is closed under the linear maps the model applies, per the multivariate reference.

Choose any other noise and all four properties fail at once.

Exercises

  1. Complete the Herschel–Maxwell derivation carefully. Where exactly is measurability (or continuity) of \(\varphi\) used, and what goes wrong without it? (Cauchy’s equation has pathological non-measurable solutions.)
  2. Show that \(e^{-|x|}e^{-|y|}\) is not rotationally symmetric by finding two points at the same radius with different densities. Sketch its level sets.
  3. Carry the Herschel–Maxwell argument into \(d\) dimensions and show it yields \(\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2 I_d)\). Which of the two demands does a non-isotropic Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\) violate?
  4. Verify the maximum-entropy proof’s key step: show explicitly that \(\int p \log q\) depends on \(p\) only through \(\int x\,p\) and \(\int x^2 p\) when \(q\) is Gaussian.
  5. Find the maximum-entropy density on \([0,\infty)\) subject to a fixed mean. (Answer: exponential. The same Lagrangian argument, with a different constraint.)
  6. Compute the Chebyshev bound and the true Gaussian tail at \(k = 6\). How large is the discrepancy, and which of §§2–4 explains why the true tail is so much thinner?
  7. (Stability.) Show directly, using the MGF from the multivariate reference, that a sum of two independent Gaussians is Gaussian with variances adding. Then explain why this makes the Gaussian a natural candidate for the CLT limit.
