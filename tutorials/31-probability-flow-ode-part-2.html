02 · Higher-dimensional transport

NoteDraft — Part 2: scope

These lecture notes are in development and may be revised.

Part 1 developed the sampling problem and the probability-flow construction in one dimension. Here we extend the notation and state the main results for data in \(\mathbb R^d\), without repeating the derivations. Time remains normalized to \(0\le t\le1\): data at zero, terminal prior at one, and generation from one to zero.

1. Sampling in more than one dimension

A data sample now has \(d\) coordinates, collected in a vector \(\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)^\mathsf T\in\mathbb R^d\). For example, an image can be represented by a vector containing all its pixel values. One sample is one particle in this \(d\)-dimensional space; its coordinates are not separate data particles.

Our goal is still to generate samples from \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\). We can draw from the prior \(\mathcal N(\mathbf0,I_d)\) and numerically integrate an ODE backward. The construction problem is to find a vector field whose induced densities satisfy the desired endpoints:

\[\boxed{p_0=p_{\mathrm{data}},\qquad p_1=p_{\mathrm{prior}}=\mathcal N(\mathbf0,I_d).}\]

These specify population distributions, not an endpoint for each individual particle. The finite-rate diffusion construction below generally achieves an approximate Gaussian terminal law. Exact distributional recovery assumes the actual terminal law, exact dynamics, and exact integration.

Multivariate CDFs exist, but a single cumulative probability does not uniquely identify a position in \(\mathbb R^d\). Applying a separate CDF transform to each coordinate does not generally remove dependence between coordinates. The scalar percentile construction in Part 1 therefore has no direct coordinatewise extension. The continuity equation and score-based probability-flow construction do extend to \(d\) dimensions.

2. Notation and differential operators

Bold symbols denote vectors. A subscript \(t\) denotes time; a subscript \(i\) on a vector component denotes a coordinate.

Symbol Meaning
\(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^d\) A position in data space
\(\mathbf X_t\in\mathbb R^d\) A random particle position at time \(t\)
\(p_t(\mathbf x)\) Scalar probability density with respect to \(d\)-dimensional volume
\(\mathbf v(\mathbf x,t)\in\mathbb R^d\) Deterministic velocity field
\(\mathbf f(\mathbf x,t)\in\mathbb R^d\) Drift of the forward SDE
\(g(t)\ge0\) Scalar noise coefficient, independent of position
\(\mathbf W_t\in\mathbb R^d\) Brownian motion with independent standard Brownian coordinates
\(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf0,I_d)\) A fresh Gaussian noise vector; use \(\varepsilon\) in one dimension
\(I_d\) The \(d\times d\) identity matrix

For a scalar function \(u(\mathbf x)\), its gradient is a vector:

\[\nabla_{\mathbf x}u= \begin{bmatrix}\partial_{x_1}u&\cdots&\partial_{x_d}u\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}.\]

For a vector field \(\mathbf a=(a_1,\ldots,a_d)^\mathsf T\), its divergence is a scalar:

\[\nabla_{\mathbf x}\!\cdot\mathbf a =\sum_{i=1}^d\partial_{x_i}a_i.\]

Divergence measures local net outward flux per unit volume. The Laplacian of a scalar function is

\[\Delta_{\mathbf x}u=\sum_{i=1}^d\partial_{x_i}^2u.\]

These reduce to the first derivative, first derivative, and second derivative, respectively, when \(d=1\).

3. ODE trajectories, flows, and the continuity equation

A vector field assigns all \(d\) velocity components at each position and time:

\[\boxed{\frac{d\mathbf x(t)}{dt}=\mathbf v(\mathbf x(t),t).}\]

Given an initial position, its solution defines a trajectory. Collecting the solutions defines the flow map \(\Psi_{0\to t}\), with \(\mathbf X_t=\Psi_{0\to t}(\mathbf X_0)\). Assume sufficient regularity for unique trajectories in both time directions over the interval of interest. The resulting flow is invertible.

The transported density obeys the continuity equation:

\[\boxed{\partial_t p_t(\mathbf x) =-\nabla_{\mathbf x}\!\cdot \left[p_t(\mathbf x)\mathbf v(\mathbf x,t)\right].}\]

The vector \(p_t\mathbf v\) is the probability flux. In any fixed region, probability changes through net flux across its boundary. With suitable vanishing boundary flux, total probability stays one.

In higher dimensions a prescribed density path need not determine a unique velocity. Adding a field \(\mathbf u\) satisfying \(\nabla\cdot(p_t\mathbf u)=0\) leaves the continuity equation unchanged. The probability-flow construction below supplies one compatible field.

4. Forward diffusion defines the density path

Consider the Itô SDE

\[\boxed{d\mathbf X_t=\mathbf f(\mathbf X_t,t)\,dt +g(t)\,d\mathbf W_t,\qquad \mathbf X_0\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}.}\]

Brownian increments satisfy

\[\mathbf W_{t+h}-\mathbf W_t\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf0,hI_d),\]

independently over disjoint time intervals. We restrict the noise coefficient to a scalar function of time. Matrix-valued or position-dependent noise requires a more general formula than the one stated here.

The density satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation:

\[\boxed{\partial_t p_t =-\nabla_{\mathbf x}\!\cdot(\mathbf f p_t) +\frac12g(t)^2\Delta_{\mathbf x}p_t.}\]

The first term is drift transport; the second is diffusion due to noise. Together with the initial distribution, this equation specifies the density path under the usual well-posedness assumptions.

Variance-preserving diffusion

The constant-rate example from Part 1 becomes

\[\boxed{d\mathbf X_t=-\frac\beta2\mathbf X_t\,dt +\sqrt\beta\,d\mathbf W_t,\qquad \beta>0.}\]

Its cumulative conditional law is

\[\boxed{\mathbf X_t\mid\mathbf X_0=\mathbf x_0 \sim\mathcal N\!\left(\alpha_t\mathbf x_0, (1-\alpha_t^2)I_d\right),\qquad \alpha_t=e^{-\beta t/2}.}\]

Thus a draw at a chosen time is obtained by

\[\mathbf x_t=\alpha_t\mathbf x_0+ \sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon, \qquad\boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf0,I_d).\]

The noise is independent of the clean sample. This describes a conditional Gaussian; the marginal \(p_t\) is generally not Gaussian because the clean sample follows the data distribution. At \(t=1\), the law approaches standard normal as \(\alpha_1\) becomes small. Positive finite \(\beta\) generally gives an approximate terminal match.

A time-dependent nonnegative rate \(\beta(t)\) uses the same formulas with

\[\alpha_t=\exp\left[-\frac12\int_0^t\beta(u)\,du\right].\]

Replace the constant \(\beta\) in the drift, noise coefficient, and sampling updates by its value at the current time. This schedule generalization is used in the later training and image-model notes. Equivalently, \(\beta(t)=-2\,d(\log\alpha_t)/dt\). The training corruption, score conversion, and solver must all use this same schedule; see the worked schedule in Part 3.

5. The score and the probability-flow vector field

For a smooth positive density, define its score by

\[\boxed{\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x)=\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_t(\mathbf x).}\]

The score has \(d\) components, one derivative for each coordinate. It is the gradient of the joint log density, so each component can depend on all coordinates. Learning it does not assume independent data coordinates.

The probability-flow field associated with the forward SDE is

\[\boxed{\mathbf v(\mathbf x,t) =\mathbf f(\mathbf x,t)-\frac12g(t)^2\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x).}\]

With the exact score, the same initial law, and suitable regularity and uniqueness assumptions, its ODE reproduces the forward SDE’s marginal density \(p_t\) at every time. It generally pairs initial and final particles differently from the SDE. Reversing its ODE does not undo a particular stochastic trajectory. This is the probability-flow result of Song et al..

For VP diffusion, the field simplifies to

\[\boxed{\mathbf v(\mathbf x,t) =-\frac\beta2\left[\mathbf x+\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x)\right].}\]

As a check, if the data are already standard normal, then \(\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x)=-\mathbf x\) and the probability-flow velocity is zero. The ODE particles stay fixed while the forward SDE particles continue to move; both populations remain standard normal.

6. Sampling with the ODE

Draw \(\mathbf x\) from the terminal law at \(t=1\) and integrate toward zero. With a trained score network, use

\[\mathbf v_\theta(\mathbf x,t) =\mathbf f(\mathbf x,t)-\frac12g(t)^2\mathbf s_\theta(\mathbf x,t).\]

For a positive step magnitude \(h\), explicit Euler integration backward in time gives

\[\boxed{\mathbf x(t-h)\approx \mathbf x(t)-h\,\mathbf v_\theta(\mathbf x(t),t).}\]

For VP diffusion this is

\[\mathbf x(t-h)\approx\mathbf x(t) +\frac{\beta h}{2}\left[\mathbf x(t)+ \mathbf s_\theta(\mathbf x(t),t)\right].\]

Each update acts on the whole data vector. There is no additional noise injection in this ODE sampler. With the exact terminal law and exact flow, the output has density \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\). Gaussian prior substitution, score estimation, and numerical integration introduce the same three approximations described in Part 1.

7. The reverse SDE is a different sampler

For comparison, the same forward process admits a stochastic reverse sampler. Use an increasing generation clock \(\tau=1-t\) and define \(\mathbf Y_\tau\) to have density \(p_{1-\tau}\). Its reverse SDE is

\[\boxed{d\mathbf Y_\tau= \left[-\mathbf f(\mathbf Y_\tau,1-\tau) +g(1-\tau)^2\mathbf s_{1-\tau}(\mathbf Y_\tau)\right]d\tau +g(1-\tau)\,d\overline{\mathbf W}_\tau.}\]

Here \(\overline{\mathbf W}_\tau\) is Brownian motion in the generation clock, and \(\mathbf Y_0\sim p_1\). This is a statement of the reverse-time result; its derivation is not included here. See Song’s discussion of reverse SDEs.

In terms of a positive step magnitude \(h\) and decreasing original time \(t\), the Euler–Maruyama update is

\[\boxed{\mathbf x_{t-h}\approx\mathbf x_t+ h\left[-\mathbf f(\mathbf x_t,t)+g(t)^2\mathbf s_\theta(\mathbf x_t,t)\right] +g(t)\sqrt h\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon,\qquad \boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf0,I_d).}\]

Each step uses a fresh independent Gaussian draw. This uses the derived reverse drift; it is not obtained by applying a negative time step to the forward SDE. Compared with the ODE sampler, the score coefficient is doubled and stochastic noise remains. With exact scores and initialization, both samplers have the same time marginals, but different trajectories.

8. What the neural network must estimate

The network is a vector-valued function

\[\mathbf s_\theta:\mathbb R^d\times(0,1]\longrightarrow\mathbb R^d.\]

Training aims to estimate \(\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x)\) at noisy data positions and different times. Gaussian corruption supplies the conditional-score target

\[\nabla_{\mathbf x_t}\log p(\mathbf x_t\mid\mathbf x_0) =-\frac{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}}.\]

Its conditional expectation given \((\mathbf X_t,t)\) is the marginal score; squared-error regression targets that conditional mean. This states the training principle without deriving its loss or the associated denoising identities. Inference fixes the learned network and uses one of the sampling updates above.

At positive noise levels the density is smooth and positive. Reaching a regular invertible flow at time zero requires appropriate assumptions on the data density; a discrete or lower-dimensional data law can give a singular endpoint. The main results above apply on intervals where the required regularity holds.

Part 3 develops score estimation, noise prediction, and Tweedie’s formula.

9. A correlated Gaussian example

Let \(\mathbf X_0\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma_0)\) with positive-definite covariance. The VP marginals and their scores are

\[\boldsymbol m_t=\alpha_t\boldsymbol\mu,\qquad \Sigma_t=\alpha_t^2\Sigma_0+(1-\alpha_t^2)I_d,\]

\[\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x)=-\Sigma_t^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol m_t).\]

A probability-flow trajectory for this family is

\[\mathbf x(t)=\boldsymbol m_t+ \Sigma_t^{1/2}\Sigma_0^{-1/2}(\mathbf x_0-\boldsymbol\mu).\]

The covariance matrices here have common eigenvectors, which makes the displayed symmetric-square-root formula valid. In those eigenvector coordinates, each centered component follows the one-dimensional Gaussian scaling from Part 1. In the original coordinates the score generally couples coordinates through \(\Sigma_t^{-1}\).

For \(\Sigma_0=I_d\) and \(\boldsymbol\mu=\mathbf0\), the velocity is zero. A nonzero mean with \(\Sigma_0=I_d\) instead gives a translation of the mean toward zero; it does not collapse the population variance.

10. Connection to neural ODEs and normalizing flows

A neural ODE parameterizes a velocity with a network. When its flow transports a density and we track its change of volume, it defines a continuous normalizing flow. Along a trajectory,

\[\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(\mathbf x(t)) =-\nabla\cdot\mathbf v(\mathbf x(t),t).\]

This is the continuous counterpart of subtracting a log absolute Jacobian determinant in a discrete invertible normalizing-flow layer. For a smooth invertible flow it implies

\[\log p_0(\mathbf x_0) =\log p_1(\mathbf x_1)+\int_0^1\nabla\cdot\mathbf v(\mathbf x(t),t)\,dt.\]

The probability-flow ODE uses this same mathematics. Its distinguishing construction is to specify a stochastic corruption path, estimate the path’s scores, and obtain the velocity from those scores. A generic continuous normalizing flow may instead parameterize and learn its velocity directly. A finite Euler step is a numerical approximation and need not itself be invertible for an arbitrary step size.

With an approximate learned score, the ODE still transports its own model density under suitable regularity. That density need not equal the true corruption marginal \(p_t\). The likelihood formula applies to the transported model density, with additional numerical error in practical integration. Neither exact data likelihood nor exact marginal agreement follows merely from using a neural vector field.

These relationships are discussed in Song et al.’s probability-flow construction. Detailed divergence and continuity derivations are kept in Part 1’s appendix.
