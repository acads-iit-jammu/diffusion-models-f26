NoteDraft — Learning the score

These lecture notes are in development and may be revised. Part 2 specified sampling dynamics given a score. We now construct training targets from clean data and Gaussian noise. We first use scalars to make the conditional expectations explicit, then state the vector formulas.

1. What remains unknown?

Generation requires evaluating a vector field at the current sample and time. For VP diffusion, the probability-flow field in one dimension is

\[v(x,t)=-\frac{\beta(t)}2[x+s_t(x)],\qquad s_t(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\log p_t(x).\]

The schedule is chosen by us; the marginal score is unknown. Our dataset contains observations \(X_0\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}\), not values of \(p_t\) or its derivative. We therefore need a regression problem that can be constructed from those observations and whose population solution is the score.

Throughout this lecture,

\[X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}\,\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\]

with independent \(X_0,\varepsilon\). Data are at \(t=0\) and the terminal prior is at \(t=1\). For a VP rate schedule,

\[\alpha_t=\exp\left(-\frac12\int_0^t\beta(u)\,du\right).\]

A constant rate gives the expression \(e^{-\beta t/2}\) used in Part 1. The training identities depend on \(\alpha_t\), and do not require a constant rate. We work at times with \(0<\alpha_t<1\).

2. Forward corruption supplies supervised examples

For each minibatch, select a clean observation \(x_0\), a time \(t\), and independent standard-normal noise \(\varepsilon\). Construct

\[x_t=\alpha_tx_0+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}\,\varepsilon.\]

The conditional Gaussian density is known, so its score is known:

\[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log p_t(x\mid x_0) =-\frac{x-\alpha_tx_0}{1-\alpha_t^2}.\]

At the generated observation, this becomes

\[U=-\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}}.\]

The network sees \((x_t,t)\) as input and \(U\) as a target. It does not see \(x_0\) as an input. Individual training targets are noisy: several \((x_0,\varepsilon)\) pairs can produce similar \(x_t\).

3. Why conditional targets recover the marginal score

The key identity is

\[\boxed{s_t(x)=\mathbb E\left[ \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log p_t(x\mid X_0) \right|X_t=x\right] =-\frac{\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_t=x]}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}}.}\]

To see why, differentiate the marginal mixture and divide by its density:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{p_t'(x)}{p_t(x)} &=\int \frac{\partial_xp_t(x\mid x_0)}{p_t(x)}p_{\mathrm{data}}(x_0)\,dx_0\\ &=\int \partial_x\log p_t(x\mid x_0)\,p(x_0\mid x)\,dx_0. \end{aligned}\]

The second line uses Bayes’ rule. The integral notation assumes a data density; an expectation with respect to the data distribution gives the same identity for discrete data after positive Gaussian corruption. Differentiation under the integral requires the usual integrability conditions.

Squared-error regression estimates conditional means. Therefore the denoising score-matching loss

\[\boxed{\mathcal J_s(\theta)=\mathbb E\left[ \lambda(t)\left(s_\theta(X_t,t)+\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}}\right)^2\right]}\]

has the desired population solution \(s_\theta(x,t)=s_t(x)\), for positive weights on the times being trained and sufficient model capacity. Finite datasets, restricted networks, and optimization error make practical estimates approximate. This training construction is used in Song et al., Score-Based Generative Modeling through Stochastic Differential Equations.

The loss can also be decomposed as

\[\mathcal J_s(\theta) =\mathbb E\left[\lambda(t)(s_\theta(X_t,t)-s_t(X_t))^2\right] +\mathbb E\left[\lambda(t)\operatorname{Var}(U\mid X_t,t)\right].\]

The second term does not depend on the network. Training against conditional targets minimizes the same parameter-dependent error as training against the unavailable marginal score.

4. Predicting noise instead

A numerically convenient network output is \(\varepsilon_\theta(x,t)\), trained with

\[\mathcal J_\varepsilon(\theta) =\mathbb E[(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta(X_t,t))^2].\]

At the population optimum, \(\varepsilon_*(x,t)=\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_t=x]\). Convert any noise predictor to a score estimate using

\[\boxed{s_\theta(x,t)=-\frac{\varepsilon_\theta(x,t)}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}}.}\]

You can also construct a clean-data predictor:

\[\boxed{\widehat x_{0,\theta}(x,t) =\frac{x-\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x,t)}{\alpha_t}.}\]

These are exact algebraic conversions of predictions. They do not imply that three unweighted training losses emphasize the same times. For predictions related by these conversions, the samplewise errors satisfy

\[\begin{aligned} (\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta)^2 &=(1-\alpha_t^2)(U-s_\theta)^2,\\ (X_0-\widehat x_{0,\theta})^2 &=\frac{1-\alpha_t^2}{\alpha_t^2}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta)^2. \end{aligned}\]

Thus uniform-time noise prediction is a particular time weighting of score matching. Near zero noise, dividing prediction error by \(\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}\) amplifies score error. Near the terminal time, a small \(\alpha_t\) amplifies errors in clean-data prediction.

5. Tweedie’s formula: the posterior mean denoiser

First consider the standalone additive-noise model

\[Y=X+\sigma \varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\quad \sigma>0.\]

Among all functions \(D(y)\), the one minimizing expected squared denoising error is

\[D_*(y)=\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y].\]

Tweedie’s formula expresses this optimal estimator through the noisy density:

\[\boxed{D_*(y)=y+\sigma^2\frac{d}{dy}\log p_Y(y).}\]

Equivalently, the score is the displacement from the observed value toward its posterior mean, divided by the noise variance. It describes the noisy marginal density, not the conditional likelihood for one particular clean observation. The complete Bayes-optimality argument and differentiation are in Appendix A.

Apply this formula to \(Z=\alpha_tX_0\), with noise variance \(1-\alpha_t^2\):

\[\boxed{\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x] =\frac{x+(1-\alpha_t^2)s_t(x)}{\alpha_t}.}\]

This also follows directly by taking conditional expectations in the forward corruption equation and using the noise–score identity. It connects the same three quantities: posterior mean clean data, conditional mean noise, and marginal score.

6. Two solved denoising examples

A Gaussian prior

Let \(X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,V)\) and \(Y=X+\sigma \varepsilon\). Then

\[p_Y=\mathcal N(\mu,V+\sigma^2),\qquad \frac{d}{dy}\log p_Y(y)=-\frac{y-\mu}{V+\sigma^2}.\]

Tweedie’s formula gives

\[\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y] =\mu+\frac{V}{V+\sigma^2}(y-\mu).\]

At small noise, the posterior mean stays close to \(y\). At large noise, it stays close to the prior mean. The posterior variance is \(V\sigma^2/(V+\sigma^2)\); the mean alone does not represent this remaining uncertainty.

Two equally likely clean values

Let \(P(X_0=1)=P(X_0=-1)=1/2\). At a positive diffusion time, Bayes’ rule gives posterior log odds

\[\log\frac{P(X_0=1\mid X_t=x)}{P(X_0=-1\mid X_t=x)} =\frac{2\alpha_tx}{1-\alpha_t^2}.\]

Consequently,

\[\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x] =\tanh\left(\frac{\alpha_tx}{1-\alpha_t^2}\right),\]

\[s_t(x)=\frac{\alpha_t\tanh(\alpha_tx/(1-\alpha_t^2))-x}{1-\alpha_t^2}.\]

At \(x=0\), the posterior mean is zero, although clean observations are only \(-1\) or \(+1\). A posterior sample is one of those two values. This is why a single posterior-mean denoising operation generally does not generate the clean-data distribution.

For each fixed nonzero \(x\), the posterior mean tends to \(\operatorname{sign}(x)\) as noise vanishes. At \(x=0\), it remains zero by symmetry. This discrete clean distribution also illustrates why smooth invertible flow statements are made at positive noise or under appropriate endpoint regularity.

7. The vector formulas and an implementable training loop

For \(\mathbf X_0\in\mathbb R^d\), use independent Gaussian noise \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf0,I_d)\). Replace scalar derivatives by gradients and scalar squared errors by squared Euclidean norms. In particular,

\[\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x) =-\frac{\mathbb E[\boldsymbol\varepsilon\mid\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x]}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}},\qquad \mathbb E[\mathbf X_0\mid\mathbf X_t=\mathbf x] =\frac{\mathbf x+(1-\alpha_t^2)\mathbf s_t(\mathbf x)}{\alpha_t}.\]

A continuous-time training loop can use:

repeat:
    draw a minibatch x0 from the data
    draw t in [t_min, 1], independently for each observation
    draw independent standard-normal noise eps
    xt = alpha(t) * x0 + sqrt(1 - alpha(t)^2) * eps
    predict eps_hat = network(xt, t)
    minimize mean squared error between eps_hat and eps

Time is an input because the density and its score change with noise level. A small positive \(t_{\min}\) avoids evaluating a score conversion at zero noise. Its value is an implementation choice, not a universal constant. The notebook uses \(t_{\min}=0.01\) and reports sampling at that time.

The conditional score target \(\mathbf U=-\boldsymbol\varepsilon/\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}\) has second moment \(d/(1-\alpha_t^2)\) at a fixed time. The noise target \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\) instead has second moment \(d\). It can become large near zero noise, even when the marginal score is well behaved. This is another reason to state the output parameterization, time sampling, and loss weighting explicitly.

8. Using the trained model for generation

Freeze the network. At every solver evaluation, convert its output to a score and evaluate

\[\mathbf v_\theta(\mathbf x,t) =-\frac{\beta(t)}2\left[\mathbf x- \frac{\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf x,t)}{\sqrt{1-\alpha_t^2}}\right].\]

Start with a standard-normal sample at \(t=1\) and integrate toward smaller \(t\). An ODE step uses no fresh randomness. A reverse-SDE step adds the appropriate Gaussian increment, as specified in Part 2.

If sampling stops at \(t_{\min}\), its intended output law is \(p_{t_{\min}}\), not exactly \(p_0\). Applying a final posterior mean changes the output law again; it is not an exact substitute for the missing evolution. Evaluate separately:

  • terminal prior error: standard normal versus the actual \(p_1\);
  • numerical error: solver resolution at a fixed field;
  • estimation error: the learned field versus the exact field;
  • endpoint choice: \(p_{t_{\min}}\) versus clean data.

Part 4 implements these comparisons on a Gaussian mixture where the reference score is known.

Appendix A. Bayes optimality and a standalone derivation of Tweedie’s formula

Assume \(X\) has a finite second moment. Let \(m(y)=\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y]\). For any proposed estimate \(d\),

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[(X-d)^2\mid Y=y] &=\mathbb E[(X-m(y)+m(y)-d)^2\mid Y=y]\\ &=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y=y)+(m(y)-d)^2. \end{aligned}\]

The cross term vanishes because \(\mathbb E[X-m(y)\mid Y=y]=0\). Hence \(d=m(y)\) minimizes conditional risk, and choosing this value at each \(y\) minimizes overall risk. This is Bayes optimality for squared error; other losses can give other estimators.

Now write the Gaussian likelihood and marginal:

\[p(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left[-\frac{(y-x)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right],\qquad p_Y(y)=\int p(y\mid x)p_X(x)\,dx.\]

Differentiate the likelihood:

\[\partial_y p(y\mid x)=\frac{x-y}{\sigma^2}p(y\mid x).\]

Differentiate the marginal and separate terms:

\[p_Y'(y)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2} \left[\int x\,p(y\mid x)p_X(x)\,dx-y\,p_Y(y)\right].\]

Bayes’ rule identifies the first integral as \(p_Y(y)\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y]\). Dividing by the positive noisy marginal gives

\[\partial_y\log p_Y(y)=\frac{\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y]-y}{\sigma^2}.\]

Rearrange to obtain the stated formula. For data without a density, replace \(p_X(x)dx\) by the probability measure of \(X\). For vectors and isotropic Gaussian noise, the same calculation uses a gradient and gives \(\mathbb E[\mathbf X\mid\mathbf Y=\mathbf y]=\mathbf y+\sigma^2\nabla\log p_{\mathbf Y}(\mathbf y)\).

Appendix B. The regression decomposition

For a target \(U\) and input \(A=(X_t,t)\), let \(m(A)=\mathbb E[U\mid A]\). Conditional expansion gives

\[\mathbb E[(h(A)-U)^2\mid A] =(h(A)-m(A))^2+\operatorname{Var}(U\mid A).\]

Multiply by a time-dependent weight and average. Taking \(U\) to be the conditional corruption score makes \(m(A)\) the marginal score, which proves the loss decomposition in Section 3. The irreducible conditional variance is nonnegative; it need not be strictly positive for every possible data distribution.
