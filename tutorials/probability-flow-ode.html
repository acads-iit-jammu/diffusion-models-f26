The Probability-Flow ODE

Standalone tutorial — a forward-looking one: it spans material from Modules 3–6 (training objectives, Fokker–Planck, reverse-time dynamics) and shows where the course is headed. Everything is self-contained, but the pace is brisker than the W01–W02 tutorials it builds on: The World’s Smallest Diffusion Model, Generative AI, in Its Simplest Form, and The Deterministic Map, Proved. Notation: \(\Phi\) is always the standard normal CDF; flow maps are written \(\Psi\).

The problem, and the thesis

Score-based diffusion models are usually introduced as stochastic denoising: add Gaussian noise to data, learn to reverse the corruption, then start from noise and iteratively denoise. That description is correct, but it hides a second, equally exact reading:

A diffusion model learns a time-dependent velocity field that transports the (near-Gaussian) terminal law \(p_T\) of the forward process into the data distribution. Once the field is known, samples can be generated deterministically, by solving an ordinary differential equation.

That deterministic dynamics is the probability-flow ODE, and the whole tutorial is the unpacking of one line:

\[ \boxed{\; \begin{gathered} \text{learn local score information} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{construct a velocity field} \\ \Downarrow \\ \text{integrate a global transport map} \end{gathered} \;} \]

The generative problem itself is the one fixed in Generative AI, in Its Simplest Form: construct a map carrying a simple distribution to \(p_{\text{data}}\). In practice the simple distribution is \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\); the exact statement, kept honest throughout, is that the machinery transports \(p_T\), which the forward process drives close to \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\).

One could try to learn the endpoint map directly (GANs do). Diffusion models instead construct a continuous path of distributions \(p_0 \to p_t \to p_T\) interpolating between data and near-Gaussian, and learn only the local information needed to move along the path. Why the incremental route wins was the subject of the factorization discussion in The Deterministic Map, Proved; here we build the machinery that makes it practical when nothing about \(p_{\text{data}}\) is known in closed form.

Two conventions: a dictionary

A warning before any formulas. The W01–W02 tutorials used the variance-exploding (VE) convention,

\[ x_\sigma = x_0 + \sigma z , \]

in which the clean signal is never rescaled and the variance grows without bound. This tutorial uses the variance-preserving (VP) convention standard in the DDPM literature, in which the signal is simultaneously attenuated:

\[ x_t = \alpha_t\, x_0 + \sigma_t\, \varepsilon, \qquad \alpha_t^2 + \sigma_t^2 = 1 . \]

The two are the same construction in different coordinates. The exact dictionary, valid whenever \(\alpha_t > 0\):

\[ \boxed{\; \begin{aligned} Y_t &= \frac{X_t}{\alpha_t} = X_0 + \tilde\sigma_t\, \varepsilon, \qquad \tilde\sigma_t^2 = \frac{\sigma_t^2}{\alpha_t^2} = \frac{1}{\alpha_t^2} - 1, \\[2pt] s_Y(y, t) &= \alpha_t\, s_X(\alpha_t y,\, t) \end{aligned} \;} \]

i.e. dividing the VP state by \(\alpha_t\) yields exactly a VE process at noise scale \(\tilde\sigma_t = \sigma_t/\alpha_t\) (the noise-to-signal ratio: with the usual \(\mathrm{SNR} = \alpha^2/\sigma^2\), this is \(\mathrm{SNR}^{-1/2}\)), and scores transform by the chain rule. VP is VE followed by a deterministic shrink.

One caution about a tempting shortcut. For static identities — the corruption formula, the posterior, the denoiser, Tweedie — setting \(\alpha_t = 1\) in a VP expression recovers the corresponding VE formula from W01–W02, and we use this check repeatedly below. For dynamics the shortcut fails: \(\alpha \equiv 1\) in VP forces \(\beta \equiv 0\), a frozen process. The dynamical bridge is the coordinate change above, not a parameter setting.

The forward SDE, and what it constructs

A general diffusion process is specified by a stochastic differential equation

\[ \boxed{\ dX_t = f(X_t, t)\, dt + g(t)\, dW_t \ } \]

with four ingredients: the state \(X_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) (the sample at noise level \(t\), with \(X_0 \sim p_{\text{data}}\)); the time \(t \in [0, T]\), which is not physical time but a continuous corruption index; the drift \(f(x,t)\), the deterministic part of the motion (remove the noise term and the dynamics would be \(\dot X = f(X,t)\)); and the diffusion coefficient \(g(t)\) scaling the Brownian increments \(W_{t + dt} - W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, dt\, I)\) — how much fresh noise is injected per unit of forward time.

The VP diffusion is the choice

\[ \boxed{\ dX_t = -\tfrac12 \beta(t)\, X_t\, dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\, dW_t \ } \]

\[ f(x,t) = -\tfrac12\beta(t)\, x, \qquad g(t) = \sqrt{\beta(t)}, \]

where \(\beta(t) > 0\) is the noise schedule — the only coefficient choice within this VP family: it fixes the drift, the diffusion coefficient, and (below) the corruption parameters. Small \(\beta\) means slow corruption; the usual schedules increase \(\beta(t)\) so that early times perturb gently and late times destroy aggressively.

The name variance-preserving records a covariance identity. From the closed form below, \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_t) = \alpha_t^2 \operatorname{Cov}(X_0) + (1 - \alpha_t^2) I\): any data law with unit covariance keeps covariance \(I\) for all \(t\) (and if \(X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), the entire law is stationary). For general data the intermediate laws are not Gaussian, but the variance scale stays controlled, and the law approaches \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) insofar as \(\alpha_t \to 0\).

The VP SDE admits a closed-form conditional description — for each fixed \(t\),

\[ \boxed{\ X_t \overset{d}{=} \alpha_t X_0 + \sigma_t \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \text{ independent of } X_0 \ } \]

\[ \alpha_t = \exp\!\Big(\!-\tfrac12 \int_0^t \beta(s)\, ds\Big), \qquad \sigma_t^2 = 1 - \alpha_t^2 . \]

(This is a distributional identity per time slice; a single fixed \(\varepsilon\) does not describe the joint law of the whole path — for that, the SDE itself is the object.) At \(t = 0\): \(\alpha_0 = 1\), \(\sigma_0 = 0\) — the data itself. As \(t\) grows, \(\alpha_t \to 0\) exactly when \(\int_0^t \beta \to \infty\); at any finite \(T\) with finite \(\int_0^T \beta\), \(\alpha_T\) is small but positive, and \(p_T \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) is an approximation — a good one for standard schedules, and the first entry in the error inventory kept below.

The forward process is a construction, not a destruction

What the forward process constructs is more important than what it destroys: a continuous family of distributions

\[ p_0 = p_{\text{data}} \;\longrightarrow\; p_t \;\longrightarrow\; p_T \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I), \]

\[ p_t(x) = \int \mathcal{N}\big(x;\, \alpha_t x_0,\, \sigma_t^2 I\big)\, p_0(dx_0) \]

— the rescaled data law convolved with \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_t^2 I)\). Every later object of the theory — the score, the velocity field, the transport — is a property of this family, not of any single noisy sample.

(Writing \(p_0(dx_0)\) matters: the two-point example below has an atomic \(p_0\) with no density.) This family is the path along which the model will later transport probability mass; it is the VP version of the family \(\{p_\sigma\}\) whose 1-D portrait fills the W01–W02 demos. Note that the family depends jointly on the schedule and on \(p_{\text{data}}\): the schedule fixes the corruption kernel, the data fixes what gets corrupted.

The score

For each intermediate distribution at \(t > 0\) (where \(p_t\) is a genuine smooth density, whatever \(p_0\) was), define the score:

\[ \boxed{\ s_t(x) = \nabla_x \log p_t(x). \ } \]

Geometrically: read the density as a landscape; the score points uphill, toward locally more probable samples. It is local information only — a direction, not a destination. Two canonical computations:

  • Gaussian: for \(p = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I)\), \(\nabla_x \log p(x) = -(x - \mu)/\sigma^2\), pointing at the mean; for \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\), simply \(-x\).
  • The two-bump mixture of W01, whose score \(\big(\tanh(x/\sigma^2) - x\big)/\sigma^2\) you have already derived and watched drive the reverse flow.

Here is the crux of the learning problem. The conditional corruption law \(p_t(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\alpha_t x_0, \sigma_t^2 I)\) is fully known — the schedule fixed it. But the marginal \(p_t\) mixes the conditional over the unknown \(p_{\text{data}}\), so its score cannot be evaluated. The score is the only learned time-dependent function this sampler requires (the exact terminal law \(p_T\) also depends on the data, but it is approximated by a Gaussian rather than learned).

Learning the score

Three objects must be kept distinct in this section; conflating any two of them is a standard error.

Object 1: the sampled conditional target. The forward process manufactures training data: draw \(x_0 \sim p_{\text{data}}\), a time \(t\) (from a range bounded away from \(0\), or with a weight that keeps the objective finite — see below), and \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\); form \(x_t = \alpha_t x_0 + \sigma_t \varepsilon\). The conditional score is analytically available:

\[ \nabla_{x_t} \log p_t(x_t \mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \alpha_t x_0}{\sigma_t^2} = -\frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_t}. \]

This is a random target, different for every draw.

Object 2: the marginal score — the posterior mean of the target. Denoising score matching trains a network \(s_\theta(x, t)\) against Object 1:

\[ \boxed{\ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[ \lambda(t)\, \Big\| s_\theta(x_t, t) + \frac{\varepsilon}{\sigma_t} \Big\|^2 \right], \ } \]

with \(\lambda(t) > 0\) a weighting choice. The reason this works: for each fixed \((x, t)\) with \(t > 0\), the minimizer of a conditional squared error is the conditional mean, and (differentiating under the integral)

\[ \begin{aligned} s^\star(x, t) &= \mathbb{E}\big[ \nabla_x \log p_t(x \mid x_0) \,\big|\, x_t = x \big] = \frac{\int \nabla_x p_t(x \mid x_0)\, p_0(dx_0)}{p_t(x)} \\[2pt] &= \frac{\nabla_x p_t(x)}{p_t(x)} = \nabla_x \log p_t(x). \end{aligned} \]

The population minimizer over unrestricted functions is the marginal score; a neural network approximates it within its class. (Module 4 does this with full care — Vincent’s identity. Note also that with \(\lambda \equiv 1\) the target variance behaves like \(1/\sigma_t^2\) near \(t = 0\), which is why practical recipes either weight or avoid the smallest times.)

Object 3: the noise-predictor parameterization. Many implementations train \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\) to predict the injected noise. Its population optimum is the posterior mean \(\varepsilon^\star(x, t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t = x]\) — not the per-sample \(\varepsilon\) — and the two optima are linked by

\[ s^\star(x, t) = -\frac{\varepsilon^\star(x, t)}{\sigma_t}, \qquad\text{hence one trains with}\qquad s_\theta = -\frac{\varepsilon_\theta}{\sigma_t}. \]

Score prediction and noise prediction are the same estimator in different units; a score loss with weight \(\lambda(t)\) equals a noise loss with weight \(\lambda(t)/\sigma_t^2\).

“Noise prediction” is conditional-mean estimation

The account above is the standard one and its algebra is correct — but the phrase “the network predicts the injected noise”, read literally, is misleading, and it quietly breeds specific misconceptions (collected at the end of this box, each traced to its root). Nor is the phrase a strawman: it is the research literature’s own language, verbatim. The DDPM paper introduces the network as “a function approximator intended to predict \(\varepsilon\) from \(\mathbf{x}_t\)”1; the DDIM paper writes that “the model \(\varepsilon_\theta^{(t)}(\mathbf{x}_t)\) then attempts to predict \(\varepsilon_t\) from \(\mathbf{x}_t\), without knowledge of \(\mathbf{x}_0\)”2; Nichol and Dhariwal state that “the network could also predict the noise \(\varepsilon\)” and that “predicting \(\varepsilon\) worked best”3. The expository canon carries it further: the widely used Hugging Face implementation guide describes the reparametrization as making “the neural network learn (predict) the added noise” and concludes that “our neural network becomes a noise predictor, rather than a (direct) mean predictor”4 — a phrasing worth savoring, since the population-optimal noise predictor is a mean predictor, of the conditional kind. Careful statements do exist in print — the tutorial of Nakkiran et al. presents \(\varepsilon\)-, \(x_0\)-, and \(v\)-prediction uniformly as estimation of conditional expectations5 — but they are the exception, not the phrasing that spread. The phrasing is harmless to authors who hold the algebra in their heads — and misleading to every reader who does not. (One terminological note: throughout, \(\varepsilon\) denotes the aggregate noise of the fixed-\(t\) representation \(x_t = \alpha_t x_0 + \sigma_t \varepsilon\) — the object the literature’s phrase refers to — not the one-step innovation between adjacent times.)

\[ \varepsilon = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t, t]}_{\displaystyle \varepsilon^\star(x_t, t)} + \underbrace{\big(\varepsilon - \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t, t]\big)}_{\text{zero conditional mean}} , \]

the first term a deterministic function of what the network sees, the second a residual with zero conditional mean (not independent of the observation — only its best point prediction is exhausted). For any predictor that is a function of \((x_t, t)\), the tower property splits the loss accordingly:

\[ \mathbb{E}\big\|\varepsilon_\theta - \varepsilon\big\|^2 = \mathbb{E}\big\|\varepsilon_\theta - \varepsilon^\star\big\|^2 + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon^\star\big\|^2}_{\text{irreducible floor}} \]

(a time-dependent weight \(\lambda(t)\) multiplies both terms alike). Only the first term is reducible in population risk. The floor equals \(\mathbb{E}\, \operatorname{tr} \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon \mid x_t, t)\) — the minimum mean-squared error — and for nondegenerate data at finite noise levels it is strictly positive: the training loss does not approach zero, and should not.

The reducible part is an object we have already met:

\[ \varepsilon^\star(x, t) = -\sigma_t\, s_t(x) \]

— the negative, \(\sigma_t\)-scaled score. The predictable component of the noise encodes exactly the score, and nothing more. Equivalently, in displacement form: \(\sigma_t\, \varepsilon^\star(x,t) = x - \alpha_t\, \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid x] = -\sigma_t^2 s_t(x)\) — the posterior-mean offset of the observation from the attenuated clean signal (a mean, not a most-likely value). It is a deterministic, smoothly varying field: for the two-point example below, \(\varepsilon^\star(x,t) = \big(x - \alpha_t \tanh(\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2)\big)/\sigma_t\), a smooth curve in \(x\).

The confusion has a simple source. In a regression there are two different objects: the target — the random quantity each training pair happens to contain (here, the sampled \(\varepsilon\)) — and the fitted function — what the trained model actually computes, namely the average of all targets compatible with a given input (here, \(\varepsilon^\star\)). The phrase “noise prediction” names the target; the network is the fitted function. In careful regression language the phrase is even defensible — predicting a random target means estimating its conditional mean — but the colloquial reading of “noise” hides that this is what is happening.

Two consequences sharpen the picture.

  • Structure in the data appears as the nonlinear part of the conditional-mean field — and that field, not any randomness, is what the network learns. For data with no data-specific structure (\(p_{\text{data}} = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), so \(p_t = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and \(s_t(x) = -x\)), the field reduces to the linear baseline \(\varepsilon^\star = \sigma_t x\); in the probability-flow ODE this score exactly cancels the VP drift — zero velocity, nothing to transport.
  • How much of the noise is predictable depends on the data and the noise level. For smooth, full-dimensional data, \(\mathbb{E}\|\varepsilon^\star\|^2 = \sigma_t^2\, J(p_t) \to 0\) as \(\sigma_t \to 0\) (with \(J\) the Fisher information): the standardized noise becomes mostly unpredictable even though denoising is easy. For atomic or well-separated data — the two-point example — the nearby atom becomes identifiable at small \(\sigma_t\) and the noise becomes nearly recoverable from the observation. At the other extreme (\(\sigma_t \to 1\), \(\alpha_t \to 0\)), \(x_t \to \varepsilon\) and prediction is trivial: the observation mostly is the noise.

The root cause, and the three misconceptions it breeds. Each falsehood below is a logical consequence of taking “the network predicts the noise” literally; each is followed by the correcting fact.

  • If the noise can truly be predicted, the training loss should reach zero. — In fact the loss is bounded below by \(\mathbb{E}\, \operatorname{tr} \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon \mid x_t, t) > 0\) for nondegenerate data at finite noise level; no function of \((x_t, t)\) can go beneath it.
  • If what is being predicted is “noise”, the prediction should look like static and jitter. — In fact the fitted function is a deterministic field: evaluated twice at the same \((x_t, t)\) it returns the same answer, and it varies smoothly with its input — the two-point formula above is a \(\tanh\) curve, not a jitter. (Its values can occasionally resemble a noise pattern — for Gaussian data \(\varepsilon^\star = \sigma_t x_t\) — but it is a fixed function of the input, never a fresh random draw.)
  • If the network predicts noise, it has the strange ability to predict randomness. — In fact it estimates a conditional mean: the structured component \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t, t]\), the part of the noise that the observation determines. The genuinely random residual is never predicted by anything.

From SDE to ODE: the derivation

This is the heart of the tutorial. The density of the forward SDE evolves by the Fokker–Planck equation (Module 5 derives it; here we use it):

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t} = -\nabla \cdot (f\, p_t) + \tfrac12 g(t)^2\, \Delta p_t \ } \]

— deterministic transport by the drift, plus spreading by the noise. Separately, purely deterministic particle motion \(\dot X_t = v(X_t, t)\) evolves its density by the continuity equation:

\[ \frac{\partial p_t}{\partial t} = -\nabla \cdot (p_t\, v). \]

The question writes itself: is there a velocity field \(v\) whose noiseless transport reproduces exactly the density evolution of the noisy SDE? Try

\[ v(x, t) = f(x, t) - \tfrac12 g(t)^2\, \nabla_x \log p_t(x). \]

Then, using \(p_t \nabla \log p_t = \nabla p_t\),

\[ p_t v = p_t f - \tfrac12 g^2\, p_t \nabla \log p_t = p_t f - \tfrac12 g^2\, \nabla p_t, \]

so

\[ -\nabla \cdot (p_t v) = -\nabla \cdot (p_t f) + \tfrac12 g^2\, \nabla \cdot (\nabla p_t) = -\nabla \cdot (p_t f) + \tfrac12 g^2\, \Delta p_t \]

— exactly the Fokker–Planck right-hand side. The diffusion term has been absorbed into an extra drift along the score (in forward time the correction points down the score, spreading mass; integrated backward it points up). Hence the deterministic dynamics

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{dX_t}{dt} = f(X_t, t) - \tfrac12 g(t)^2\, \nabla_x \log p_t(X_t) \ } \]

reproduces the marginals \(p_t\) on any interval \([\epsilon, T]\) with \(\epsilon > 0\), when initialized from the matching law at either end (from \(p_T\), integrated backward, for generation). If \(p_0\) itself is singular — the two-point example — the flow reaches it only in the \(\epsilon \downarrow 0\) limit: a smooth flow cannot terminate on atoms at any positive time, which is the VP face of the \(\sigma_{\min}\) discussion in the earlier tutorials. This is the probability-flow ODE. For VP diffusion it takes a compact form:

\[ \boxed{\ \frac{dX_t}{dt} = -\tfrac12 \beta(t)\, \big[ X_t + s_t(X_t) \big], \ } \]

and with the learned score, \(s_t \to s_\theta\). (In \(d > 1\) this velocity is the canonical choice, not the unique one: adding any field \(u\) with \(\nabla\!\cdot(p_t u) = 0\) preserves the same marginals. In one dimension the monotone flow is unique — the rigidity used below.)

One box on the stochastic alternative, for completeness. Introduce the increasing reverse clock \(\tau = T - t\), so that “later” means “less noisy”; then the same score defines the reverse-time SDE

\[ dX = \big[ -f(X, t) + g(t)^2\, s_t(X) \big]\, d\tau + g(t)\, d\bar W_\tau , \]

with \(\bar W\) a fresh Brownian motion in the \(\tau\) clock. Note the score coefficient \(g^2\) — twice the ODE’s \(\tfrac12 g^2\) — compensating the re-injected noise: the same doubling you saw in the two-point sampler of W01. With the exact score and exact initialization from \(p_T\), both samplers traverse the same family \(\{p_t\}\); the ODE is deterministic given \(X_T\), the SDE consumes fresh randomness at every instant. From here on we follow the ODE.

Two readings of the result:

  • Kinematic: the equation is first-order — it prescribes velocity, not force. Every point of space-time carries an arrow \(v(x,t)\); a particle follows the arrow under its feet, and the arrows change both because the particle moves and because the field is time-dependent.
  • Hydrodynamic: release many particles from \(p_T\) and let each follow the field; individually deterministic, collectively their density flows — the continuity equation is conservation of probability mass. Hence the name: probability flows through space under a deterministic velocity field.

And the answer to a question worth asking out loud: is the score the velocity? No. The velocity is drift plus score correction,

\[ v = \underbrace{f}_{\text{known from the schedule}} - \underbrace{\tfrac12 g^2\, s}_{\text{learned correction}}, \]

with the schedule deciding how strongly the learned geometry steers the motion.

Sampling is numerical integration

After training, everything on the right-hand side of the ODE is available: the schedule supplies \(\beta(t)\) — hence \(f\), \(g\), \(\alpha_t\), \(\sigma_t\) — and the network supplies \(s_\theta(x, t)\). Generation is: draw \(X_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) (the Gaussian stand-in for \(p_T\)), integrate backward to \(t = \epsilon\), return the endpoint. But the trajectory is defined implicitly — \(v\) depends on the state along the unknown path — so for a neural score there is generally no closed form. The trajectory is computed numerically.

Fix a descending grid \(T = t_N > t_{N-1} > \cdots > t_1 > t_0 = \epsilon\), with \(h_k = t_{k-1} - t_k < 0\) (backward steps), and \(\epsilon > 0\) a small final time. The Euler sampler:

\[ \boxed{\ \begin{aligned} &X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \\ &\text{for } k = N, N-1, \dots, 1: \\ &\qquad s \leftarrow s_\theta(X, t_k) \\ &\qquad v \leftarrow -\tfrac12 \beta(t_k)\, (X + s) \\ &\qquad X \leftarrow X + h_k\, v \\ &\text{return } X \end{aligned} \ } \]

Since \(h_k < 0\), the score increment is \(+\tfrac12 \beta(t_k)\, |h_k|\, s_\theta\) — the particle steps up the score, consistent with W01’s sampler. Four remarks that carry more weight than the pseudocode:

  • The score must be re-evaluated at every step. Both of its arguments change: the particle moved, and the noise level dropped. Each step is evaluate score → construct velocity → move → repeat — the sampler incrementally reconstructs a global transport from local information.
  • Better integrators exist. Heun’s method: take the provisional Euler step \(X^* = X_k + h_k\, v(X_k, t_k)\), re-evaluate the velocity at the provisional state and the new time, \(v^* = v(X^*, t_{k-1})\), then move by the average, \(X_{k-1} = X_k + \tfrac{h_k}{2}\big( v(X_k, t_k) + v^* \big)\) — second-order accuracy (for sufficiently smooth fields) at two network calls per step. Beyond it: Runge–Kutta, adaptive solvers, multistep and diffusion-specific schemes. The relevant cost metric is the number of neural function evaluations (NFE), since each velocity evaluation runs the network once.
  • The four errors, inventoried (mirroring the W01 tutorial’s honesty paragraph): (i) terminal-prior mismatch — sampling \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) instead of the true \(p_T\); (ii) score error — \(s_\theta \ne s_t\); (iii) integration error — finite steps; (iv) finite-\(\epsilon\) endpoint bias — stopping at \(t = \epsilon\). Under exact prior, exact score, and exact integration, the endpoint law is exactly \(p_\epsilon\); the practical sampler carries all four deviations.
  • The \(\epsilon\)-endpoint is not a technicality. Even the idealized endpoint \(p_\epsilon\) is the attenuated and smoothed data law (\(\alpha_\epsilon X_0 + \sigma_\epsilon Z\)), not the data law itself — the VP twin of the terminal width \(\sigma_{\min}\) quantified in the W01 tutorial and The Deterministic Map, Proved. Increasing the step count removes integration error only; lowering \(\epsilon\) sharpens the target, at the price of an increasingly stiff velocity field (for the two-point score below, the derivative at the separatrix scales like \(\sigma_t^{-4}\)).

The two-point example, in VP coordinates

Now run the entire pipeline on the course’s home dataset, \(p_0 = \tfrac12 \delta_{-1} + \tfrac12 \delta_{+1}\) — the case where every learned object has a formula, worked in the VE convention in the W01 tutorial and proved in The Deterministic Map, Proved. Conditioning on the component, \(X_t \mid X_0 = \pm 1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\pm\alpha_t, \sigma_t^2)\), so

\[ p_t(x) = \tfrac12\, \mathcal{N}(x; -\alpha_t, \sigma_t^2) + \tfrac12\, \mathcal{N}(x; +\alpha_t, \sigma_t^2) \]

(sanity check: \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \alpha_t^2 + \sigma_t^2 = 1\) — variance preserved exactly). The posterior is one line of Bayes — the likelihood ratio of the two components is \(\exp(-2\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2)\), so

\[ \mathbb{P}(X_0 = +1 \mid X_t = x) = \operatorname{sigmoid}\!\Big( \frac{2 \alpha_t x}{\sigma_t^2} \Big), \]

\[ \widehat X_0(x, t) := \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_t = x] = \tanh\!\Big( \frac{\alpha_t x}{\sigma_t^2} \Big), \]

and the score follows in one more line from VP Tweedie (\(s = (\alpha_t\, \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid x] - x)/\sigma_t^2\), derived in the next section):

\[ s_t(x) = \frac{ \alpha_t \tanh(\alpha_t x / \sigma_t^2) - x }{ \sigma_t^2 }, \]

\[ v_t(x) = -\tfrac12 \beta(t) \left[ x + \frac{ \alpha_t \tanh(\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2) - x }{ \sigma_t^2 } \right]. \]

Setting \(\alpha_t = 1\) recovers every corresponding W01 formula — the static dictionary at work. As \(t \to 0\): \(\alpha_t \to 1\), \(\sigma_t \to 0\), so \(\widehat X_0(x, t) \to \operatorname{sign}(x)\) pointwise for \(x \ne 0\) (not uniformly near \(0\)) — the smooth \(\tanh\) denoiser sharpening into the discontinuous sign map.

What does the integrated flow do? The 1-D argument of The Deterministic Map, Proved runs in two stages, and the order matters. Stage one, at positive noise: for each \(\epsilon > 0\), the flow map \(\Psi_{T \to \epsilon}\) is continuous and strictly increasing (trajectories of a smooth 1-D ODE cannot cross), and a monotone map pushing \(p_T\) to \(p_\epsilon\) is unique — therefore

\[ \Psi_{T \to \epsilon} = F_\epsilon^{-1} \circ F_T , \]

the quantile transport between the two mixture CDFs. (At finite \(T\), \(F_T\) is the CDF of the near-Gaussian mixture, not of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\); by symmetry both place their median at \(0\), which is all the limit needs.) Stage two, the limit: as \(\epsilon \downarrow 0\), the target collapses onto the atoms and

\[ \boxed{\ \Psi_{T \to \epsilon}(X_T) \;\longrightarrow\; \operatorname{sign}(X_T) \qquad p_T\text{-almost surely}. \ } \]

Every positive start ends at \(+1\), every negative start at \(-1\), and symmetry sends exactly half the mass to each atom. For the smoothed target \(\tfrac12\mathcal{N}(-1, \varrho^2) + \tfrac12\mathcal{N}(+1, \varrho^2)\) the finite-\(T\) endpoint map is the genuine quantile transport \(F_{0,\varrho}^{-1} \circ F_T\) (which becomes \(F_{0,\varrho}^{-1} \circ \Phi\) only in the idealized exactly-Gaussian-terminal limit), converging to the sign map as \(\varrho \to 0\).

One qualification keeps the example honest. In this special symmetric case the endpoint of the flow happens to be a formula we could have written down without any ODE. The solver is not “numerically approximating the sign function” in general: it solves a differential equation whose endpoint, for arbitrary data, is not known in advance — that is the entire point of learning the score.

Three distinctions that prevent three confusions

The score is not the velocity. \(v = f - \tfrac12 g^2 s\): the learned score is the correction; the schedule contributes the drift and decides the correction’s strength.

The denoiser is not the endpoint. VP Tweedie (set \(\alpha \equiv 1\) to recover W01’s version) gives the one-step posterior mean

\[ \mathbb{E}[X_0 \mid X_t = x] = \frac{x + \sigma_t^2\, s_t(x)}{\alpha_t} \qquad (\alpha_t > 0), \]

a single denoising estimate from noise level \(t\). The flow endpoint \(\Psi_{T \to \epsilon}(X_T)\) integrates the score field through every level. In the two-point limit both converge to the same sign decision — because a trajectory that never touches \(0\) keeps its sign, and the denoiser’s sign at any level agrees with it — but they are different objects, and conflating them is a common reading error in the literature.

The one-shot map and the incremental flow solve the same problem. Define the per-step Euler map \(E_k(x) = x + h_k\, v(x, t_k)\). Then the endpoint transport is represented implicitly as a time-ordered composition,

\[ \Psi_{T \to \epsilon} = \lim_{\max_k |h_k| \to 0}\; E_1 \circ E_2 \circ \cdots \circ E_N , \]

— the factorization view of The Deterministic Map, Proved, now with a learned field: each factor is near-identity and needs only local information. For fixed \(\epsilon > 0\) this limit is a continuous map; any discontinuity (the sign function) arises only in the separate limit \(\epsilon \downarrow 0\).

The pipeline, end to end

Design. Choose a schedule \(\beta(t)\). It determines the corruption kernel — \(f\), \(g\), \(\alpha_t\), \(\sigma_t\) — and, together with the data, the family \(p_0 \to p_t \to p_T\). The forward process learns nothing — it manufactures the training signal: a near-Gaussian endpoint, exact corruption pairs \((x_0, x_t, t, \varepsilon)\), and an analytic conditional score target.

Train.

\[ \boxed{\; \begin{gathered} x_0 \sim p_{\text{data}}, \quad t \sim p(t), \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \\[2pt] \min_\theta\; \mathbb{E} \left[ \lambda(t)\, \big\| s_\theta(\alpha_t x_0 + \sigma_t \varepsilon,\; t) + \varepsilon/\sigma_t \big\|^2 \right] \end{gathered} \;} \]

Sample.

\[ \boxed{\; \begin{gathered} X_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \\[2pt] \frac{dX_t}{dt} = f(X_t, t) - \tfrac12 g(t)^2 s_\theta(X_t, t) \quad \text{integrated } T \to \epsilon, \\[2pt] \text{return } X. \end{gathered} \;} \]

Takeaways

  1. The schedule \(\beta(t)\) determines the corruption kernel — drift, diffusion coefficient, \(\alpha_t\), \(\sigma_t\); together with the data it determines the family \(\{p_t\}\).
  2. The score \(\nabla \log p_t\) is the only learned time-dependent function the sampler needs, trained by denoising score matching on synthetically corrupted data; noise prediction is the same estimator in different units, with population optimum \(-\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t]/\sigma_t\).
  3. The probability-flow ODE replaces the SDE’s noise by an extra drift along the score, reproducing the same marginals deterministically on \([\epsilon, T]\): \(v = f - \tfrac12 g^2 s\).
  4. Sampling is numerical ODE integration; each step is score evaluation → velocity → move; accuracy is bought with NFE; and the practical sampler carries four distinct errors — prior mismatch, score error, integration error, and the finite-\(\epsilon\) endpoint bias.
  5. In the two-point limit the integrated flow converges to the sign map — locally learned gradients, integrated into a transport that is discontinuous only in the \(\epsilon \downarrow 0\) limit — and for real data the same principle holds with no formula available: the global generative transformation is obtained by integrating locally learned score information.

Exercises

  1. (One-dimensional.) Verify from the closed form \(X_t \overset{d}{=} \alpha_t X_0 + \sigma_t \varepsilon\) that \(\operatorname{Var}(X_t) = \alpha_t^2 \operatorname{Var}(X_0) + \sigma_t^2\), and confirm the variance-preserving property for unit-variance data. State the \(d\)-dimensional covariance version.
  2. Use the static dictionary (\(\alpha \equiv 1\)) to recover, from the VP two-point formulas: the mixture \(p_t\), the posterior, the denoiser, and the score — and match each against the W01 tutorial. Then use the dynamic dictionary (\(Y = X/\alpha\)) to transform the VP score into the VE score and check consistency.
  3. Complete the proof that the population minimizer of the denoising score-matching objective is the marginal score, stating explicitly where \(t > 0\), \(\lambda(t) > 0\), and differentiation under the integral are used.
  4. Show that the reverse-time SDE and the probability-flow ODE differ exactly by replacing \(\tfrac12 g^2 s\, dt\) with \(g^2 s\, dt - g\, d\bar W\), and argue via the Fokker–Planck equation that both preserve the family \(\{p_t\}\) under exact score and exact terminal initialization.
  5. A reproducible benchmark. Take \(\beta(t) = 0.1 + 19.9\,t\) on \([0, 1]\) (so \(\alpha_t = e^{-(0.05 t + 4.975 t^2)}\)), \(\epsilon = 10^{-3}\), a uniform grid in \(t\), exact two-point score, and \(X_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Measure the RMS distance of endpoints to the nearest atom. Compare Euler and Heun at equal NFE (Euler gets twice the steps). Which wins at NFE = 10? At NFE = 100?
  6. For the smoothed two-point target with component variance \(\varrho^2\), write the VP forward family \(p_t\) explicitly (hint: Gaussians convolve), and verify that the finite-\(T\) quantile transport is \(F_{0,\varrho}^{-1} \circ F_T\), reducing to \(F_{0,\varrho}^{-1} \circ \Phi\) only when \(p_T\) is replaced by its Gaussian idealization.
  7. For the two-point score, compute \(s_t'(0)\) and show it scales as \(\alpha_t^2/\sigma_t^4\) for small \(\sigma_t\) — the stiffness quoted in the sampling section. Where in \(x\) is \(|s_t'|\) largest, and why is that exactly where a numerical integrator needs small steps?
