Moving Distributions: The Transport Story

An informal tutorial, for readers with no particular background. No proofs, few formulas — just the one idea that organizes all of generative modeling, told from the beginning. Its rigorous twin, with the history and the theorems, is Generative Models as Transport: History and Validation.

The problem, without any symbols

You have a pile of examples — photographs, sentences, measurements. Behind them stands a distribution: the invisible shape that says which examples are common, which are rare, which are impossible. Generative modeling is one wish:

Make more examples with the same shape.

Here is the reframing that unlocks everything. A computer already owns one perfect source of randomness — dull, structureless randomness, like a uniform lottery number or television static. So the wish becomes a moving job:

Take the boring randomness we have, and transport it onto the interesting shape we want. Move the pile of sand into the sculpture.

Every generative model you have heard of — VAEs, GANs, normalizing flows, diffusion models — is a machine for doing exactly this. They differ only in how they decide which grain of sand goes where. That difference is the whole story.

In one dimension, the problem is completely solved

Suppose your data is just numbers — heights, incomes, temperatures. One dimension. Then there is a classical trick, and it settles everything.

The rank trick. To transform lottery numbers into incomes:

  1. Draw a uniform lottery number between 0 and 1 — say \(0.83\).
  2. Read it as a rank: “the 83rd percentile.”
  3. Output the income that sits at the 83rd percentile of the income distribution.

That’s the entire generator. Ask “what fraction of the data lies below this value?” — that question is the CDF, and it converts values into ranks. Answer it backwards — rank into value — and that is the inverse CDF. The generator is: uniform randomness in, read as a rank, matched to the value with the same rank.

Three things make this trick special, not just convenient:

  • It always works. Any distribution of numbers, however lumpy, has percentiles.
  • It is fair, and it never crosses. The 20th percentile of the lottery goes to the 20th percentile of incomes, the 90th to the 90th. Lower stays lower, higher stays higher — two grains of sand never swap places in flight.
  • It is the only such rule — if you insist on never crossing. Drop that insistence and even one dimension has many transports (flip every rank, \(u \leftrightarrow 1-u\), and the output distribution is identical). What is special about one dimension is not that transport is unique — it is that the line comes with a natural tie-breaking rule everyone accepts: lower stays lower. The rule crowns a unique king.
  • One small honesty note for the reverse direction: turning data back into ranks needs care when many data points tie (repeated values); ties must be broken with a coin flip. Generation — ranks into data — never has this problem.

You can watch this trick operating in the course demo Sampling as a Map, and it is the exact mechanism behind the two-point generator of W01. In one dimension, generative modeling is finished — a lookup in a percentile table.

And in two dimensions, the trick dies

Now let the data be pairs — a height and a weight; an image with many pixels. Try the rank trick: “what fraction of the data lies below this point?”

Below? Point A is up and to the left of point B. Which one is “lower”? In a plane there is no natural order. No order, no ranks; no ranks, no percentiles; no percentiles, no inverse CDF. The one canonical tool we had does not survive the second dimension.

Worse — the problem is not that transport becomes impossible. It becomes embarrassingly possible. Picture moving a round cloud of sand onto another round cloud: you can slide it straight over; or slide it over and rotate it by any angle; or swirl the middle while fixing the edge. Infinitely many transports do the job, and no one of them is the boss. In one dimension the never-cross rule crowned a unique king; in two dimensions there is no throne.

This is the honest statement of the difficulty: in higher dimensions, transport is not undefined — it is underdetermined. And the thing that is missing is precisely the tie-breaker: the line’s “lower stays lower” rule has no natural analogue in the plane. Something must be added to pick one map out of the infinite crowd.

Every model family is a different way of choosing

Here is the reveal: the famous generative model families are exactly the famous ways of adding that missing choice.

Sort by coordinates anyway — normalizing flows. Alphabetical order: sort by the first coordinate; break ties with the second; and so on. This resurrects ranks, one coordinate at a time — transform the first coordinate by its percentiles, then the second given the first, and so on. It works at every scale, and it is exactly what autoregressive normalizing flows do. The cost: the answer depends on which coordinate you called “first,” as alphabetical order depends on the alphabet.

Move at the cheapest total cost — optimal transport. Charge for distance: among all valid transports, take the one minimizing the total moving bill. A deep theorem says that (in the natural settings) this again produces a unique map. The cost: computing it at scale is hard, and — a genuine surprise below — it is not what the popular generators actually compute.

Melt, then re-freeze — diffusion models. Refuse the direct matching entirely. Instead, melt the data — add noise a little at a time until every shape becomes the same formless Gaussian blob — and learn to run the melting backwards. Freezing is guided by a purely local question: “from here, which direction looks denser?” That local question is learnable by a neural network even in a million dimensions. Two choices select the route: the melting schedule, and — a subtlety — a no-swirling rule: many wind patterns produce the same evolution of the sand’s density (add any swirl that circulates without piling anything up), and diffusion picks the swirl-free wind. This is the entire subject of this course, met from a new side.

Fool the inspector — GANs. Train a forger against a detective: any transport whose output the detective cannot distinguish from data is accepted. Note what is missing: any transport. Here is how loose that is, even in one dimension: take the fair rank-matcher, but first fold the lottery ticket — send every rank \(u\) to \(1-u\), or crease it in the middle like a tent. The folded generator produces exactly the same distribution, so no inspector, however perfect, can object — yet it is not the rank map. GANs add no selection principle at all, which is part of why they are powerful and part of why they are temperamental.

Compress and decompress — VAEs. Learn an encoder that squeezes data into tidy randomness and a decoder that reinflates it. Here is a lovely secret: in one dimension, the perfect encoder is precisely the rank trick — the CDF! — and the perfect decoder is the inverse CDF. But two honest footnotes. First, nothing in the VAE’s training forces that choice: many equally good codes exist (swap all ranks \(u \leftrightarrow 1-u\), for a start), and the VAE has no reason to prefer the tidy one. Second, a textbook VAE is not even allowed to be perfectly tidy — its rules require the code to stay a little blurry (a perfectly sharp code costs it an infinite penalty). The “encoder = CDF” story is the idealized, noise-free, never-crossing limit of a VAE — a beautiful limit, and one the actual training objective does not select. The VAE illustrates underdeterminacy from the inside.

The punchline: five roads, one destination — in 1-D only

Run all five families on one-dimensional data, each trained to perfection and each given the natural tie-breaking rule (never cross), and they collapse into the same map: the rank trick. But the fine print matters, and it splits three ways. For the smooth-flow families — diffusion’s deterministic sampler, normalizing flows, flow matching — the collapse is a theorem: a one-dimensional flow physically cannot cross itself, so the tie-breaker comes built in (this course proves the diffusion case in The Deterministic Map, Proved). For GANs and VAEs the collapse is merely available: their training rules accept the rank map but do not prefer it — the folded forger and the blurry code are equally welcome. In one dimension the five philosophies can be one philosophy; only some of them insist on it.

In two dimensions and up, they genuinely part ways — and that is not a defect but the point: each family is one answer to a question that no longer has a single answer. If you understand (a) the 1-D trick, (b) why it dies upstairs, and (c) what each family adds to revive it, you understand the map of the whole field.

A warning label: what not to carry upstairs

The 1-D picture is a superb ladder and a treacherous railing. Keep the ladder, do not lean on it:

  • “Lower stays lower” means nothing upstairs. There is no single order to preserve — and “monotone” does not become meaningless, it becomes many different things (several inequivalent notions replace it, and different fixes pick different ones).
  • “The transport” is not a phrase you may use without saying which selection rule you chose.
  • The diffusion route is not the cheapest route. In 1-D the melter and the cheapest mover coincide; in higher dimensions they provably differ — professionals conjectured otherwise in print and were corrected by a counterexample.
  • Even the melting does not determine the route by itself. Upstairs, many winds produce the same density evolution (swirls circulate without piling); the no-swirl rule is an extra choice, invisible in 1-D where there is no room to swirl.
  • Percentile bookkeeping is a 1-D luxury. The beautiful fact that a diffusing particle “rides its quantile” (proved in this course) has no higher-dimensional analogue.

Where to go from here

The rigorous twin of this tutorial — Generative Models as Transport: History and Validation — replays this story with theorems, dates, and citations: who proved the rank trick unique, who invented each fix and when, what exactly is true about the “VAE encoder = CDF” slogan, and where the counterexamples live. And the rest of the course is the deep dive into one of the five roads — the melt-and-refreeze road — chosen because its local, learnable steering question scales to the dimensions where images and language live.
