Diffusion, Neural ODEs, and Normalizing Flows: The Same Density, Different Journeys

ImportantTutorial 3 of 4 · Connections

Question: How does the probability-flow sampler connect to neural ODEs, normalizing flows, and flow matching?

Before this: Probability-Flow Sampling. See the series guide for the full route.

Core route: change of variables → continuous transport → density tracking → Gaussian couplings → likelihood → an introduction to flow matching. The infinitesimal determinant calculation and the flow-matching proof are optional derivation subsections.

Prerequisites: the first two tutorials, elementary calculus, and basic matrix algebra. This note introduces Jacobians through linear volume scaling. The published From Local Arrows is optional geometric background.

Suppose we can draw Gaussian noise, but want samples from a complicated data distribution. We need a mechanism that moves an entire random cloud into the right shape. An invertible neural network, a neural ODE, and a score-based diffusion model offer related ways to do this.

The connection is easiest to understand by keeping two questions separate:

  1. Particle question: where does this particular starting point go?
  2. Density question: how is the whole population distributed at each time?

An SDE and an ODE can give different answers to the first question while giving the same answer to the second. That is the central idea of this tutorial.

From discrete transformations to neural ODEs

Fix the notation and the direction of time

We retain the notation of the earlier tutorials.

Symbol Meaning
\(p_0=p_{\mathrm{data}}\) Clean-data density
\(p_t\) Density at forward noising time \(t\)
\(s_t(x)=\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\) Score, differentiated with respect to the sample coordinate
\(f(x,t),g(t)\) Forward-SDE drift and scalar noise amplitude
\(v(x,t)\) Deterministic velocity field
\(\Psi_{s\to t}\) ODE flow from time \(s\) to time \(t\)
\(\tau=T-t\) Increasing generation clock

Here \(\Phi\) remains reserved for the standard normal CDF. Forward time means data to noise; generation traverses that time interval backward. When discussing a generic flow, we write its density as \(q_t\) to distinguish it from the prescribed diffusion marginals \(p_t\).

Start with sampling as a transformation

Draw \(Z\sim p_Z\), then return \(X=F(Z)\). The output is random even when \(F\) is deterministic: the randomness came from \(Z\).

For a first example, let \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and

\[F(z)=\mu+az,\qquad a>0.\]

Then \(X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,a^2)\). Translation changes the mean; stretching changes the standard deviation. Because an interval has become \(a\) times as wide, its probability density must become \(1/a\) as large:

\[p_X(x)=\frac1a p_Z\!\left(\frac{x-\mu}{a}\right).\]

This is the intuition behind a normalizing flow. In \(d\) dimensions, take an invertible differentiable map with differentiable inverse—a diffeomorphism—and write

\[F=F_K\circ\cdots\circ F_1,\qquad x=F(z).\]

First consider a linear map \(X=AZ\) with invertible matrix \(A\). A unit box becomes a parallelepiped of volume \(|\det A|\). The same probability occupies that new volume, so

\[p_X(x)=\frac{p_Z(A^{-1}x)}{|\det A|}.\]

For example, \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,3)\) multiplies area by six and divides the corresponding density by six. Translation does not change area.

A differentiable nonlinear map is approximately linear near \(z\): \(F(z+\delta z)\approx F(z)+J_F(z)\delta z\). Here the Jacobian matrix has entries \((J_F)_{ij}=\partial F_i/\partial z_j\). Applying the linear volume argument locally gives the nonlinear change-of-variables formula:

\[\boxed{\log p_X(x)=\log p_Z(F^{-1}(x)) -\log|\det J_F(F^{-1}(x))|.}\]

The determinant measures local volume expansion. An expansion lowers density; a contraction raises it. Composing tractable invertible layers lets us build a complicated deformation while evaluating both its inverse and its density.

Replace layers by continuous motion

A small residual layer has the form

\[x_{k+1}=x_k+h\,v(x_k,t_k).\]

This is also explicit Euler’s approximation to

\[\boxed{\dot x_t=v(x_t,t).}\]

As the step size tends to zero under the usual convergence assumptions, the sequence of updates approaches an ODE trajectory. The resulting map is

\[x_t=\Psi_{s\to t}(x_s).\]

If \(v\) is continuously differentiable in space, suitably regular in time, and solutions exist uniquely throughout the interval in both directions, the flow is a diffeomorphism. Global spatial Lipschitz bounds on a finite interval are one useful sufficient condition for existence and uniqueness.

Why does uniqueness give invertibility? If two trajectories meet at the same point at the same time, solving backward from that state must recover the same initial point. Thus distinct initial states cannot merge. Spatial curves can pass through the same location at different times; that causes no problem.

For a time-dependent field, the correct composition law is

\[\boxed{\Psi_{r\to t}=\Psi_{s\to t}\circ\Psi_{r\to s}, \qquad \Psi_{s\to t}^{-1}=\Psi_{t\to s}.}\]

NoteThe exact flow and its numerical approximation are different objects

For \(\dot x=-x\), the exact map after duration \(h\) is \(x\mapsto e^{-h}x\), which is invertible for every finite \(h\). Euler gives \(x\mapsto(1-h)x\); at \(h=1\) it collapses every point to zero. An arbitrary residual layer or finite solver step does not automatically inherit the exact flow’s properties.

When a neural network parameterizes \(v_\theta\), this is a neural ODE. When we use that ODE to transport a base density and track its density change, it becomes a continuous normalizing flow (CNF). A neural ODE used only for prediction or hidden-state evolution need not be a generative density model. See Chen et al., Neural Ordinary Differential Equations.

Track density along the flow

From a Jacobian determinant to divergence

Let \(q_t\) be the density of particles moving with velocity \(v\). Recall Tutorial 2’s box argument: probability changes inside a small box only through its faces. The net outflow per unit volume in direction \(i\) is \(\partial_{x_i}(q_tv_i)\). Summing over coordinates gives

\[\boxed{\partial_tq_t=-\nabla\cdot(q_tv).}\]

This is the continuity equation. Assume densities are positive and smooth where we differentiate. Expanding the product gives

\[\partial_t q_t=-v\cdot\nabla q_t-q_t\nabla\cdot v.\]

Along a particle satisfying \(\dot x_t=v(x_t,t)\), the chain rule now yields

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\log q_t(x_t) &=\frac{\partial_t q_t}{q_t}(x_t) +v(x_t,t)\cdot\nabla\log q_t(x_t)\\ &=\boxed{-\nabla\cdot v(x_t,t)}. \end{aligned}\]

The two terms involving spatial density gradients cancel. Only the local expansion or compression of the velocity field remains.

Check with contraction. For \(v(x)=-x\) in \(d\) dimensions, \(\nabla\cdot v=-d\), so density along a trajectory grows as \(e^{d\,t}\). The map shrinks volume by \(e^{-d\,t}\). Their product stays constant, as conservation of probability requires.

Optional derivation: the infinitesimal Jacobian determinant

A tiny Euler layer has Jacobian \(I+hJ_v\). In two dimensions, for \(A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&e\end{pmatrix}\),

\[\det(I+hA)=(1+ha)(1+he)-h^2bc =1+h(a+e)+h^2(ae-bc).\]

The first-order term is \(h\operatorname{tr}A\), the sum of diagonal entries. The same expansion holds in any dimension. Since \(\log(1+z)=z+O(z^2)\),

\[\log\det(I+hJ_v)=h\operatorname{tr}J_v+O(h^2), \qquad\operatorname{tr}J_v=\nabla\cdot v.\]

The discrete log-volume change therefore becomes divergence per unit time. The previous continuity-equation argument already established the result; this calculation connects it directly to the discrete flow formula.

Recall the probability-flow construction

Tutorial 2 derives the density equation for \(dX_t=f(X_t,t)dt+g(t)dW_t\) and rewrites its flux using \(\nabla p_t=p_t s_t\). The resulting ODE velocity is

\[\boxed{v(x,t)=f(x,t)-\tfrac12g(t)^2s_t(x).}\]

With the exact score and the same initial law, it has the same one-time marginals as the forward SDE under the stated uniqueness and regularity conditions. The construction is due to Song et al.. We now ask what its trajectories and density tracking reveal about the generative model.

Solve and compare Gaussian couplings

Solved example: Brownian spreading without random trajectories

Take a one-dimensional Gaussian data distribution and constant noise amplitude:

\[X_0\sim\mathcal N(\mu,a^2),\qquad dX_t=g\,dW_t, \qquad a>0,\quad g>0.\]

The SDE has solution \(X_t=X_0+gW_t\), so

\[p_t=\mathcal N(\mu,V_t),\qquad V_t=a^2+g^2t.\]

Here \(V_t\) is the total marginal variance, not just the added noise variance. The score and probability-flow velocity are

\[s_t(x)=-\frac{x-\mu}{V_t},\qquad v(x,t)=\frac{g^2}{2V_t}(x-\mu).\]

The score points inward; the forward probability-flow velocity points outward. Set \(y_t=x_t-\mu\). Then

\[\frac{\dot y_t}{y_t}=\frac{g^2}{2(a^2+g^2t)}.\]

Integration, with the equilibrium \(y_t=0\) included separately, gives

\[\boxed{\Psi_{s\to t}(x) =\mu+\sqrt{\frac{V_t}{V_s}}(x-\mu).}\]

Every displacement from the mean is stretched by the same factor. Starting with variance \(V_s\), the output variance is \((V_t/V_s)V_s=V_t\), exactly as required. No Brownian increments were needed to obtain this spreading cloud.

For \(\mu=2\), \(a=1\), \(g=1\), and \(T=3\), the standard deviation doubles:

Starting point \(x_0\) ODE endpoint \(x_3=2+2(x_0-2)\)
0 -2
1 0
2 2
3 4
4 6

To generate the original data, sample \(X_T\sim\mathcal N(2,4)\) and use \(X_0=2+(X_T-2)/2\). This pure Brownian example does not make \(p_T\) standard normal; we sample its actual terminal law.

The density equation gives a second check:

\[\partial_xv=\frac{g^2}{2V_t},\qquad \log p_t(x_t)-\log p_s(x_s) =-\tfrac12\log\frac{V_t}{V_s}.\]

At a fixed standardized position, the Gaussian height falls in inverse proportion to its standard deviation, matching the change-of-variables rule.

Same marginals does not mean the same coupling

For the Brownian example, both constructions have marginal variance \(V_t\). Their relationships to the starting point differ:

Quantity, for \(t>0\) Forward SDE Probability-flow ODE
Conditional endpoint given \(X_0=x\) \(\mathcal N(x,g^2t)\) Point mass at \(\mu+\sqrt{V_t/a^2}(x-\mu)\)
Marginal endpoint \(\mathcal N(\mu,V_t)\) \(\mathcal N(\mu,V_t)\)
\(\operatorname{Corr}(X_0,X_t)\) \(a/\sqrt{V_t}\) \(1\)

The correlations follow from independence of \(X_0\) and \(W_t\) for the SDE, and the positive affine relationship for the ODE. They quantify the distinction: the histograms agree, but the pairings between starts and endpoints do not.

Consequently, inverting the probability-flow map for a noisy observation does not generally recover the clean point that generated that observation through the SDE. It inverts the ODE coupling. It is also generally different from Tweedie’s posterior mean denoiser for the stochastic observation model.

A sharper example. Consider stationary VP diffusion:

\[dX_t=-\tfrac12\beta(t)X_t\,dt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dW_t, \qquad X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1).\]

The density stays standard normal, so \(s_t(x)=-x\). Therefore

\[v(x,t)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)x-\tfrac12\beta(t)(-x)=0.\]

SDE particles wander while every probability-flow particle stays still. Both clouds are standard normal at every time. The bare score-ascent ODE \(\dot x=-x\), which contracts the cloud, is a third and different dynamics.

Use the flow for generation and likelihood

Generation: integrate the ODE backward

For VP diffusion more generally,

\[X_t\mid X_0=x_0\sim\mathcal N(\alpha_t x_0,\sigma_t^2I),\]

\[\alpha_t=\exp\!\left[-\tfrac12\int_0^t\beta(r)\,dr\right], \qquad\sigma_t^2=1-\alpha_t^2.\]

The terminal density approaches standard normal as the signal coefficient becomes small. Given the exact score, the exact generative map is

\[X_T\sim p_T,\qquad X_0=\Psi_{T\to0}(X_T).\]

With \(\tau=T-t\), the generation velocity is

\[\frac{dY_\tau}{d\tau} =-v(Y_\tau,T-\tau) =-f(Y_\tau,T-\tau)+\tfrac12g(T-\tau)^2s_{T-\tau}(Y_\tau).\]

On a regular interval down to \(t_0=t_{\min}\), take a descending grid \(t_N=T>\cdots>t_0\). With positive step magnitudes, Euler gives

\[\boxed{x_{k-1}=x_k-h_k v_\theta(x_k,t_k),\qquad h_k=t_k-t_{k-1}>0.}\]

The stochastic-sampler comparison in Tutorial 2 derives why adding fresh noise changes the generation drift from \(-f+g^2s/2\) to \(-f+g^2s\). These are two different samplers, not the same update with a noise switch.

Exact distributional recovery requires the exact score, the actual terminal law, and exact integration. Practical sampling introduces score error, terminal-prior approximation, and numerical error.

Diffusion’s deterministic sampler is a CNF

Under the smoothness and existence conditions stated above, we now have an invertible transport and a density-change equation. Those are the ingredients of a continuous normalizing flow.

For a learned score, define

\[v_\theta=f-\tfrac12g^2s_\theta.\]

Let \(\pi_T\) be the chosen terminal density, usually standard normal. The model density induced by backward integration is \(q_{\theta,0}\). For an input \(x_0\), solve forward to \(x_T\) and integrate divergence:

\[\boxed{\log q_{\theta,0}(x_0) =\log\pi_T(x_T) +\int_0^T\nabla\cdot v_\theta(x_t,t)\,dt.}\]

The plus sign follows by rearranging \(\log q_{\theta,T}(x_T)-\log q_{\theta,0}(x_0) =-\int_0^T\nabla\cdot v_\theta\,dt\).

This computes the learned ODE model’s likelihood, not automatically the true data likelihood. With an approximate score, its intermediate densities need not equal the prescribed \(p_t\), nor those of the approximate reverse SDE. Numerical integration and stochastic trace estimators can also introduce evaluation error. The change-of-variables identity itself is exact. If we stop at \(t_{\min}>0\), replace the lower integration limit and \(x_0\) by \(t_{\min}\) and \(x_{t_{\min}}\). That gives the model density at positive noise, not a likelihood for the unnoised clean law.

There is an endpoint qualification: a smooth diffeomorphism of a full-dimensional Gaussian cannot produce an exactly discrete distribution or one supported on a lower-dimensional surface. Such targets require a singular limit, smoothing, or stopping at positive noise. The finite-time invertibility claims apply on intervals where the stated regularity holds.

Connect score learning to velocity learning

Recall: score training is conditional averaging

Tutorial 1 proves that squared-error regression learns a conditional mean. Applied to corruption targets it gives

\[s_t(x)=\mathbb E[-\epsilon/\sigma_t\mid X_t=x],\qquad \mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x]=\frac{x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x)}{\alpha_t}.\]

This uses positive noise and \(\alpha_t>0\). We train away from the singular endpoint, or choose appropriate time weighting as explained in Tutorial 1. We can use the same conditional-averaging idea to learn velocities directly.

Where flow matching fits

The architecture and the training objective are separate choices. A CNF may be trained by likelihood, by score-derived objectives, or by direct velocity regression. Flow matching provides a way to construct tractable velocity targets along chosen conditional paths, including paths beyond diffusion. See Lipman et al., Flow Matching for Generative Modeling.

The idea is to prescribe simple paths for individual training pairs, then regress on their known velocities. If several paths pass through the same region, the learned velocity averages their directions conditional on the current position. For example, a path from a point \(a\) to a point \(b\) can be written \((1-r)a+rb\), with velocity \(b-a\). Fitting those velocities can train an ODE without first predicting a score. The fitted ODE need not reproduce each training pair’s path.

The optional calculation below shows that one particular family of Gaussian paths recovers the probability-flow velocity. The main connection is already visible: both methods use regression to learn local information and integration to generate a global transport. The paper uses noise-to-data time; here we retain data-to-noise time and generate backward.

Optional derivation: velocity regression on a diffusion path

Work on an interior interval where \(\alpha_t,\sigma_t>0\) are differentiable, with finite moments and sufficient regularity to differentiate expectations. This proof reuses conditional expectation from Tutorial 1 and the probe-function argument from Tutorial 2. Keep a single pair \((X_0,\epsilon)\) fixed and define the interpolant

\[\widetilde X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\epsilon.\]

Its one-time laws are \(p_t\), but this differentiable interpolant is not a Brownian sample path. Its conditional velocity is

\[U_t=\dot\alpha_tX_0+\dot\sigma_t\epsilon.\]

For a smooth test function \(\varphi\), differentiation gives

\[\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\varphi(\widetilde X_t)] =\mathbb E[\nabla\varphi(\widetilde X_t)\cdot U_t] =\mathbb E[\nabla\varphi(\widetilde X_t)\cdot \mathbb E[U_t\mid\widetilde X_t]].\]

The last equality is the tower property: once \(\widetilde X_t\) is fixed, \(\nabla\varphi(\widetilde X_t)\) is fixed, so we can average \(U_t\) conditionally. Define \(u_t(x)=\mathbb E[U_t\mid\widetilde X_t=x]\). For smooth probe functions that vanish outside a bounded region, integration by parts gives

\[\int\varphi\,\partial_t p_t\,dx =\int p_t\nabla\varphi\cdot u_t\,dx =-\int\varphi\,\nabla\cdot(p_tu_t)\,dx.\]

Equality for all probes says \(\partial_t p_t=-\nabla\cdot(p_tu_t)\). This integrated form is called the weak continuity equation. Under smoothness it gives the ordinary density equation, exactly as in Tutorial 2. The MSE lemma shows that regression onto \(U_t\) learns \(u_t\).

For a linear forward SDE with \(f(x,t)=a_t x\), the coefficients satisfy

\[\dot\alpha_t=a_t\alpha_t,\qquad 2\sigma_t\dot\sigma_t=2a_t\sigma_t^2+g(t)^2.\]

Using Tweedie and \(\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x]=-\sigma_t s_t(x)\),

\[\begin{aligned} u_t(x) &=\frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t} [x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x)]-\sigma_t\dot\sigma_t s_t(x)\\ &=a_t x-\tfrac12g(t)^2s_t(x)\\ &=v(x,t). \end{aligned}\]

For this choice of path, velocity regression and score regression recover the same population velocity. For VP with \(\beta(0)>0\), \(\dot\sigma_t\) grows like \(1/\sqrt t\) near zero, so the second moment of the velocity target can diverge like \(1/t\). As with score training, use a positive time cutoff or weighting that makes the expected loss finite. Smooth endpoint behavior must not be assumed from this interior calculation.

Other interpolation paths can give different transports. Straight conditional paths do not imply that the learned marginal ODE trajectories are themselves straight; the conditional averaging matters.

Review the model relationships

A map of the connections

Approach What is represented or learned? How are samples produced?
Discrete normalizing flow Invertible transformations with tractable Jacobians Apply the transformations to base samples
Likelihood-trained CNF A velocity and its induced density Integrate the learned ODE
Score-based diffusion Scores of prescribed noisy marginals Integrate a reverse SDE or probability-flow ODE
Flow-matching CNF A velocity from conditional-path regression Integrate the learned ODE

This is a map of relationships, not a claim that every discrete flow is the flow of an ODE. For example, an ODE flow starting at the identity has positive Jacobian determinant wherever regular, whereas an invertible discrete map may reverse orientation.

Check your understanding

  1. A standard Gaussian has score \(-x\). Why does this not imply its probability-flow ODE contracts? The probability-flow velocity also includes \(f\) and the coefficient \(-g^2/2\). For stationary VP diffusion, they cancel.
  2. Two simulators have identical histograms at every time. Must their start–endpoint scatter plots agree? No. In the Brownian example one is scattered, while the ODE pairs lie on a straight line.
  3. Why can deterministic generation produce random samples? Its input is random; the flow transports that randomness.
  4. Why is a conditional-score training target useful when it is not the marginal score? Squared-error regression averages over possible clean origins conditional on the observed noisy point, recovering the marginal score.
  5. What does a learned ODE likelihood describe? The density induced by its chosen terminal law and velocity, which need not equal the true data density.

The central distinction to retain is between a path of distributions and a coupling of particles across time. Diffusion specifies one stochastic coupling; its probability-flow ODE supplies a deterministic one with the same marginals in the ideal construction. That deterministic transport is where diffusion and continuous normalizing flows meet.
