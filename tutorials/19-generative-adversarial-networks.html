09 · Generative Adversarial Networks

The first deep generative model, and the one that changed what the field thought was possible. Its premise is a refusal: do not estimate a density, do not evaluate a likelihood, do not even define one. Learn a map from noise to data, and judge it by whether a second network can tell your samples from real ones.

This note derives the whole thing. The min-max objective and the counterfeiter analogy it comes from; the optimal discriminator in three lines; the theorem that the game is secretly minimising a Jensen–Shannon divergence; and then the part that occupied the field for five years afterwards — why an objective this elegant is so hard to optimise, and what each failure mode teaches about what a generative model actually needs.

1. The change of question

Every method so far has tried to write down \(p_{\text{data}}\) — as a Gaussian (note 05), a sparse graphical model, a mixture, a kernel sum, a posterior (note 06) — and each hit the curse of dimensionality from a different side. Note 08 then showed that even a known density is only the start of the difficulty, and that the machinery there cannot be pointed at images because there is no density to point it at.

So change what the model is. A GAN never represents \(p_g\) as a function. It represents a sampling procedure:

\[ z \sim p_z = \mathcal{N}(0, I_k), \qquad x = G_\theta(z) \in \mathbb{R}^d . \]

Push a low-dimensional Gaussian through a neural network. The distribution of the output — call it \(p_g\) — is a perfectly well-defined object, the pushforward \(G_\theta \# p_z\), and it is what the model claims \(p_{\text{data}}\) to be. You can draw from it in one forward pass. You cannot evaluate its density at a point, because that would require integrating over all \(z\) mapping there, and in general \(p_g\) has no density at all: its support is a \(k\)-dimensional set inside \(\mathbb{R}^d\), of Lebesgue measure zero.

This is called an implicit model — defined by how you sample it, not by a formula. Note 00’s “sampling as a map” is exactly this idea; the GAN is its first deep instantiation.

The problem this creates, immediately

Maximum likelihood is unavailable. Note 06 §11 showed that maximising likelihood is minimising \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)\) — but that requires evaluating \(p_\theta(x_i)\), which an implicit model cannot do. Worse, note 07 §8 showed that KL between two distributions on non-overlapping manifolds is identically \(+\infty\), which is the generic situation here.

So the loss must be built from samples only — a quantity computable from a batch of real \(x\)’s and a batch of fake \(G(z)\)’s, with no density anywhere. That constraint is what forces the entire GAN construction, adversary included.

2. Police and thief

Goodfellow’s framing, and the reason the idea is memorable: two players with opposed interests.

A counterfeiter (the generator) prints fake currency and wants it to pass. The police (the discriminator) inspect notes and want to catch every fake. Neither is competent at the start: the first forgeries are laughable, and the first inspections are guesswork. But each improvement forces the other to improve. Crude fakes teach the police to check the watermark; the police checking watermarks teach the counterfeiter to print one. The process ends only when the forgeries are indistinguishable from real currency — at which point the police can do no better than a coin flip, and that inability is the certificate of success.

The analogy is worth pressing on, because two of its details are the mathematics.

The counterfeiter never sees real money. \(G\) has no access to the training set. Not one gradient in the generator’s update touches a real image. Everything \(G\) learns about the data arrives through the discriminator — the police’s verdicts are the only channel. That is why a broken \(D\) is fatal: it is not a referee, it is the generator’s sole teacher.

The police hand over their reasoning, not just their verdict. A real counterfeiter learns only “caught” or “not caught”. Here, \(D\) is differentiable, so \(G\) receives \(\nabla_x D(x)\) — the direction in image space that would make the note more convincing. This is the trick that makes the whole thing work, and the point where the analogy stops being an analogy. Note 06’s comparison table put it exactly: rejection sampling throws away a rejected dart and learns nothing; a GAN turns every rejection into a direction.

3. The objective

The discriminator \(D_\phi: \mathbb{R}^d \to (0,1)\) outputs the probability that its input is real. Train it as a binary classifier on a balanced mixture of real and generated data — the cross-entropy loss, written out, is

\[ V(D, G) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(x)\big] \;+\; \mathbb{E}_{z \sim p_z}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big]. \]

\(D\) wants this large: \(\log D(x)\) near \(0\) on real data (so \(D \to 1\)) and \(\log(1-D)\) near \(0\) on fakes (so \(D \to 0\)). \(G\) wants the second term small, which means fooling \(D\). One quantity, two opposed interests — a zero-sum game, and the solution is its saddle point:

\[ \boxed{\;\min_G \max_D \; V(D,G) = \min_G \max_D \;\mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{p_g}[\log(1-D(x))].\;} \]

Note the second expectation is over \(p_g\), since \(x = G(z)\) with \(z \sim p_z\) is by definition a draw from \(p_g\). Written that way the objective involves the two distributions and no latent variable, which is what makes the next section possible.

The order matters: \(\max_D\) is inside. The generator is judged against the best available critic, not a fixed one — otherwise \(G\) would simply find the one blind spot of a mediocre \(D\) and park there.

4. Step one: the optimal discriminator

Proposition. For a fixed generator \(G\), the discriminator maximising \(V\) is

\[ \boxed{\;D^{*}_G(x) \;=\; \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)}.\;} \]

Proof. Write both expectations as integrals over the same variable:

\[ V(D,G) \;=\; \int \Big[\,p_{\text{data}}(x)\log D(x) \;+\; p_g(x)\log\big(1-D(x)\big)\Big]dx . \]

The value \(D(x)\) at one point appears in the integrand only at that point, so — allowing \(D\) to be any function, which is the assumption to remember — the integral is maximised by maximising the bracket pointwise. With \(a = p_{\text{data}}(x)\), \(b = p_g(x)\) and \(t = D(x) \in (0,1)\), maximise

\[ f(t) = a\log t + b\log(1-t), \qquad f'(t) = \frac{a}{t} - \frac{b}{1-t} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; t^{*} = \frac{a}{a+b}, \]

and \(f''(t) = -a/t^2 - b/(1-t)^2 < 0\), so this is the unique maximum. \(\square\)

Three consequences, each of which matters later.

The discriminator is a density-ratio estimator. Let \(r = p_{\text{data}}/p_g\). Then \(D^{*} = r/(1+r)\), strictly increasing in \(r\), and inverting,

\[ r(x) \;=\; \frac{D^{*}(x)}{1 - D^{*}(x)} . \]

If \(D = \sigma(h)\) for a logit \(h\), this says \(h^{*}(x) = \log \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_g(x)}\): the discriminator’s logit is the log density ratio. Two densities that cannot be written down individually, and a classifier recovers their ratio from samples alone. This is the density-ratio trick, and it is the single most reusable idea in the note.

At the solution \(D^{*} = 1/2\) everywhere. When \(p_g = p_{\text{data}}\) the ratio is 1. The police are reduced to a coin flip, as promised.

“Allowing \(D\) to be any function” is doing real work. A neural network of finite capacity, trained on finite data for finitely many steps, is not \(D^{*}\). Everything in §7 is downstream of that gap.

5. Step two: the game is a Jensen–Shannon divergence

Substitute \(D^{*}\) back and see what the generator is actually minimising. Define the mixture — the “average of the data distribution and the generated one”:

\[ m \;=\; \tfrac12\big(p_{\text{data}} + p_g\big). \]

Then \(D^{*} = p_{\text{data}}/(2m)\) and \(1 - D^{*} = p_g/(2m)\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} C(G) \;=\; \max_D V(D,G) &= \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[\log \frac{p_{\text{data}}}{2m}\right] + \mathbb{E}_{p_g}\!\left[\log \frac{p_g}{2m}\right] \\[4pt] &= \mathbb{E}_{p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[\log \frac{p_{\text{data}}}{m}\right] - \log 2 + \mathbb{E}_{p_g}\!\left[\log \frac{p_g}{m}\right] - \log 2 \\[4pt] &= -\log 4 \;+\; \mathrm{KL}\big(p_{\text{data}} \,\big\|\, m\big) + \mathrm{KL}\big(p_g \,\big\|\, m\big). \end{aligned} \]

The two KL terms are, by definition, twice the Jensen–Shannon divergence:

\[ \mathrm{JS}(p\|q) \;=\; \tfrac12 \mathrm{KL}\!\left(p \,\Big\|\, \tfrac{p+q}{2}\right) + \tfrac12 \mathrm{KL}\!\left(q \,\Big\|\, \tfrac{p+q}{2}\right). \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{\;C(G) \;=\; -\log 4 \;+\; 2\,\mathrm{JS}\big(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_g\big).\;} \]

Theorem (Goodfellow et al., 2014). The global minimum of \(C(G)\) is attained exactly when \(p_g = p_{\text{data}}\), where its value is \(-\log 4 \approx -1.386\).

Proof. \(\mathrm{JS} \ge 0\) always, with equality if and only if the two distributions coincide — it is an \(f\)-divergence, note 07 §4, and Jensen gives non-negativity with equality only at \(p = q\). So \(C(G) \ge -\log 4\) with equality precisely at \(p_g = p_{\text{data}}\). \(\square\)

This is the payoff of the derivation. A construction motivated entirely by a story about counterfeiters turns out to minimise a specific, principled, symmetric divergence. The adversary was never a heuristic: it is a variational estimator of a divergence, computing by optimisation what could not be computed in closed form. Note 07’s map of “what is each model family minimising?” lists the original GAN under Jensen–Shannon for exactly this reason, and the \(f\)-GAN generalisation — swap the \(\log\)s for the Fenchel conjugate of any convex \(f\) and get any \(f\)-divergence you like — follows the same template.

Read the small print

The theorem is about distributions, not parameters. Three gaps between it and anything you can run:

  1. \(D\) is not optimal. \(D^{*}\) assumes an unconstrained function and exact maximisation. In practice \(D\) is a network updated a few steps per generator step, so the quantity being descended is some estimate of JS, with error that is not controlled.
  2. \(G\) moves in parameter space, not function space. Goodfellow’s convergence argument (Prop. 2) treats \(p_g\) itself as the variable, where \(C\) is convex. Gradient descent moves \(\theta\), and \(C\) as a function of \(\theta\) is not convex. The proof does not transfer, and the paper says so.
  3. The expectations are estimated from minibatches. The gradient is noisy in a game where the two players’ noise is coupled.

Each gap is a real phenomenon in training, not a technicality. §7 is a catalogue of what comes through them.

6. Training, as actually implemented

Alternate stochastic gradient steps. For each iteration:

  1. \(k\) discriminator steps (\(k=1\) is standard). Draw a minibatch of \(m\) real samples and \(m\) latents, and ascend

    \[ \nabla_\phi \;\frac1m \sum_{i=1}^{m}\Big[\log D_\phi(x_i) + \log\big(1 - D_\phi(G_\theta(z_i))\big)\Big]. \]

  2. One generator step. Draw \(m\) fresh latents and descend the generator’s loss — with a modification introduced in the next paragraph and used universally since.

The \(k\) inner steps exist because the theory wants \(D\) near-optimal. But \(D\) is being asked to track a moving target: every generator step changes \(p_g\) and therefore changes \(D^{*}\). Chasing it too closely is as bad as not chasing it at all, for a reason that is the subject of §7.1.

The non-saturating loss

The min-max objective assigns \(G\) the loss \(\mathbb{E}_z[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]\). It does not work early in training, and the reason is a two-line computation. Write \(D = \sigma(h)\) with logit \(h\); then \(\log(1-D) = \log\sigma(-h)\) and

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial h}\,\log\big(1 - D\big) \;=\; -\,\sigma(h) \;=\; -D . \]

Early on, \(G\)’s samples are obvious fakes, so \(D(G(z)) \approx 0\) — and the gradient reaching the generator is proportional to \(D(G(z))\), hence also \(\approx 0\). The generator learns most slowly exactly when it is worst. The loss saturates.

The fix is to have \(G\) maximise \(\log D(G(z))\) instead of minimising \(\log(1 - D(G(z)))\) — same fixed point, since both are optimised by \(D(G(z)) \to 1\), but

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial h}\,\big(-\log D\big) \;=\; -\big(1 - \sigma(h)\big) \;=\; -(1-D) \;\xrightarrow[D \to 0]{}\; -1 . \]

Now the gradient is largest when the samples are worst. This is the non-saturating loss, and essentially every GAN ever trained uses it.

What you give up by switching

The heuristic fix breaks the theorem. Arjovsky and Bottou (2017) computed what the non-saturating generator gradient corresponds to at an optimal discriminator:

\[ \nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{p_g}\big[-\log D^{*}\big] \;=\; \nabla_\theta\Big[\mathrm{KL}\big(p_g \,\|\, p_{\text{data}}\big) - 2\,\mathrm{JS}\big(p_g\,\|\,p_{\text{data}}\big)\Big]. \]

Two things are wrong with the right-hand side. The first term is the reverse KL, which note 07 §3 identified as mode-seeking: it punishes putting mass where there is none, and is nearly indifferent to missing an entire mode. The second enters with a minus sign, actively pushing \(p_g\) away from \(p_{\text{data}}\).

So the loss people actually train is not a divergence, is mode-seeking, and contains a repulsive term. Mode collapse and instability are not surprising in retrospect — they are visible in the objective.

7. Why it is hard: four failure modes

7.1 Vanishing gradients, and the tension at the heart of the method

Note 07 §8 established the fact this rests on. If \(p_{\text{data}}\) and \(p_g\) have essentially disjoint supports — which is generic, since both live on low-dimensional manifolds in a high-dimensional space and two such manifolds generically miss each other — then

\[ \mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}} \| p_g) = \log 2 \quad\text{exactly, and}\quad \nabla_\theta \,\mathrm{JS} = 0 . \]

Not small. Zero. The divergence reports “maximally different” and stops responding, so it cannot distinguish a near miss from a wild one, and the generator receives no signal about which way to move.

The consequence is a genuine dilemma, and it is the deepest problem with the original formulation:

The theory wants \(D\) optimal. An optimal \(D\) gives the generator no gradient.

A near-perfect discriminator separates the two manifolds cleanly, is confident everywhere, and is therefore flat everywhere \(G\) can reach. A weak discriminator gives a usable gradient that points in the wrong direction. GAN training lives in a narrow band between the two, held there by hand-tuned learning rates, architectures and update ratios. This is what practitioners mean by “GANs are finicky”, and it is a statement about the objective, not about anyone’s code.

The non-saturating loss patches the symptom. WGAN removes the cause: replace JS with \(W_1\), which note 07 §8 showed reports the actual distance \(d\) between separated supports and keeps a gradient pointing home. That is the entire motivation for Wasserstein GAN, and it is why note 07 came before this one.

7.2 Mode collapse

The most visible failure: \(G\) maps every latent \(z\) to the same output, or to a handful of outputs. Train on ten digit classes, generate only sevens — convincing sevens, and nothing else.

Three complementary explanations, all correct.

The order of the min and max. The objective is \(\min_G \max_D\), but alternating optimisation drifts toward \(\max_D \min_G\), and these are not equal. In the swapped game the generator moves first against a fixed \(D\), and its optimal response is to put all mass on the single point \(\arg\max_x D(x)\) — the one input this discriminator most believes. A perfect delta function is the exact solution to the inner problem. Then \(D\) learns to reject that point, \(G\) hops to the next best, and the pair cycles through modes forever without ever covering them.

The divergence is mode-seeking. By §6’s callout the practical loss carries a reverse-KL term, and reverse KL is minimised by covering one mode well rather than all modes poorly. The generator is doing what its objective asks.

Nothing penalises missing mass. \(D\) judges samples one at a time. It can answer “is this a plausible digit?” but never “is this batch’s diversity right?” — that is a property of a set, invisible to a pointwise classifier. Diversity is simply not in the loss. This diagnosis suggests its own fix, and minibatch discrimination — letting \(D\) see cross-sample statistics — is precisely that.

The same pathology as note 08, from the other side

An MH chain trapped in one mode of a bimodal posterior looks perfectly healthy — good acceptance, stable trace — while sampling the wrong distribution. A mode-collapsed GAN looks perfectly healthy — sharp samples, low losses — while modelling the wrong distribution.

Both are cases of a procedure that is locally correct everywhere and globally wrong, and in both cases the diagnostic that would reveal it (does the sample cover all the mass?) is exactly the one the algorithm does not compute.

7.3 No convergence guarantee: it is a game, not a minimisation

Ordinary training minimises one function, and “the loss went down” means progress. Here there are two losses that move in opposite directions, and the target is a saddle point — a Nash equilibrium — not a minimum. Descending a loss is not a correct algorithm for finding a saddle, and it can orbit one forever.

The smallest possible instance makes it concrete. Take \(\min_x \max_y \, xy\), whose unique equilibrium is \((0,0)\). Simultaneous gradient steps with rate \(\eta\):

\[ x_{t+1} = x_t - \eta\,y_t, \qquad y_{t+1} = y_t + \eta\,x_t . \]

Then

\[ x_{t+1}^2 + y_{t+1}^2 = (x_t - \eta y_t)^2 + (y_t + \eta x_t)^2 = (1+\eta^2)\,(x_t^2 + y_t^2), \]

because the cross terms cancel exactly. The iterates spiral outward for any \(\eta > 0\), moving further from the equilibrium at every step. In continuous time (\(\eta \to 0\)) they orbit it forever on a circle and never converge. This is a convex-concave game — the friendliest case there is — and gradient descent-ascent still fails on it.

Practical consequences:

  • Losses are uninformative. \(D\)’s loss hovering near \(\log 2 \approx 0.69\) per term is what you want, and also what you see when everything has collapsed. Neither loss decreasing monotonically is a sign of health; there is no scalar whose descent means progress. Evaluation moves outside the training loop entirely — FID, Inception Score, human inspection — with all the arbitrariness that implies.
  • Oscillation. Sample quality visibly cycles: sharp, then degraded, then sharp again. There is no “the loss plateaued, we are done”; you checkpoint frequently and pick by eye.
  • Balance is a hyperparameter. If \(D\) outpaces \(G\), §7.1 kills the gradient. If \(G\) outpaces \(D\), it exploits a weak critic and collapses. The update ratio, the two learning rates and the two architectures must be tuned jointly, and a configuration that works on one dataset may not transfer.

7.4 Slow and expensive convergence

Beyond instability, the arithmetic of the game is unfavourable.

  • Two networks, coupled. Every generator step needs a discriminator that is approximately optimal for the current \(p_g\) — and \(p_g\) just changed. \(D\) is perpetually chasing a moving target, and much of the compute goes into tracking rather than into progress.
  • Indirect signal. \(G\) never touches a real sample. Its entire information about the data arrives second-hand through \(D\)’s gradient, filtered through whatever \(D\) currently happens to have learned. Contrast a diffusion model, whose training signal is a supervised regression target computed directly from a real image plus known noise.
  • No likelihood, so no model selection. You cannot compute held-out log-likelihood, so the ordinary machinery — early stopping, comparing architectures, detecting overfitting — is unavailable. A GAN that has memorised the training set and one that has generalised produce equally pretty samples.
  • Sensitivity. Results depend sharply on architecture, normalisation, and optimiser settings. That an entire paper (DCGAN, 2015) was needed to publish a set of architectural choices that trains reliably is itself the evidence.

8. What was done about it

For completeness, and because several of these ideas reappear later in more principled forms:

Problem Fix Idea
Vanishing gradient (7.1) non-saturating loss change \(G\)’s loss, keep the fixed point
Vanishing gradient (7.1) WGAN + weight clipping / gradient penalty replace JS with \(W_1\): an IPM, so it survives disjoint supports (note 07 §8)
Vanishing gradient (7.1) instance noise convolve both distributions with Gaussian noise so the supports overlap
Mode collapse (7.2) minibatch discrimination let \(D\) see batch statistics, so diversity enters the loss
Mode collapse (7.2) unrolled GANs let \(G\) see \(D\)’s future response, undoing the min-max swap
Instability (7.3) spectral normalisation constrain \(D\)’s Lipschitz constant, smoothing its gradient field
Instability (7.3) two time-scale updates (TTUR) different learning rates for \(D\) and \(G\)
Generality \(f\)-GAN the same variational template for any \(f\)-divergence
Instance noise is the diffusion idea, in embryo

Look at the third row. The fix for disjoint supports is: add Gaussian noise to both real and fake samples, so that both distributions acquire full support, the density ratio becomes finite, and JS regains a gradient. It works, and it was regarded as a trick.

A diffusion model does this at every noise level simultaneously and deliberately. The forward process is a whole continuum of noised versions of \(p_{\text{data}}\), and the model learns to reverse each level. The thing GANs had to smuggle in as a stabiliser is the diffusion model’s organising principle.

Note 07 makes the same observation from the divergence side: convolving with a Gaussian is what makes ratio-based quantities finite and informative again.

9. The connection back to Metropolis–Hastings

The two halves of this week look unrelated — a 1970 statistical algorithm and a 2014 deep learning paper. They are the same machine.

Note 08’s acceptance rule turns on the ratio \(\pi(y)/\pi(x)\), in which the normalising constant cancels. §4 above showed the discriminator’s logit is the log density ratio \(\log(p_{\text{data}}/p_g)\). Both methods run on a density ratio; they differ only in where it comes from.

Metropolis–Hastings GAN
Proposal chosen by hand for convenience learned
Density ratio computed exactly from \(\tilde\pi\) estimated by a classifier, from samples
Requires \(\tilde\pi(x)\) evaluable samples from \(p_{\text{data}}\) only
Rejected candidate chain stays put; nothing learned becomes a gradient: a direction to move
Output asymptotically exact, correlated draws approximate, independent draws, one forward pass each
Cost per sample thousands of steps, every time one forward pass, after training

The consequence is a fact worth stating plainly: a trained discriminator gives you a density ratio for two distributions you cannot write down. That is enough to run an MH accept/reject step on a GAN’s own output, correcting its samples toward \(p_{\text{data}}\) — which is exactly what MH-GAN (Turner et al., 2019) and discriminator rejection sampling (Azadi et al., 2019) do. The 1970 algorithm runs on the 2014 model’s output, using the learned classifier as its density ratio.

10. Key takeaway

Three sentences worth remembering.

The construction. Because an implicit model has no likelihood, its loss must be computable from samples alone; training a classifier to separate real from fake supplies exactly that, and its logit recovers the log density ratio that no formula could.

The theorem. At an optimal discriminator the game minimises \(-\log 4 + 2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_g)\), uniquely minimised when \(p_g = p_{\text{data}}\) — so the counterfeiter story was a principled divergence minimisation the whole time.

The trouble. Every difficulty traces to one of two roots: JS saturates between distributions on disjoint manifolds, which kills the gradient exactly when the discriminator is good; and a saddle point is not a minimum, so gradient descent has no reason to converge to it. The field’s response to the first was to change the divergence (WGAN), and to the second, to change the game.

Where this leaves the course

A GAN is a learned proposal with a learned judge. It fixed what was wrong with Metropolis–Hastings — the hand-picked proposal, the wasted rejections, the thousands of steps per sample — and inherited a new problem: the judge is an adversary, and training against an adversary is a game with no convergence guarantee.

The rest of this course removes the judge. A diffusion model’s forward process adds a small amount of Gaussian noise at each of many steps, which makes each reverse step nearly Gaussian and therefore matchable by direct regression against a target computed from real data. There is no adversary, no saddle point, no accept/reject, and the loss that goes down means progress.

You have now seen both classical sampling and adversarial sampling fail, and in each case the diagnosis pointed the same way. Module 3 builds the thing they were pointing at.

  2. Derive \(C(G) = -\log 4 + 2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_g)\) in full, stating where each \(\log 2\) comes from.
  3. Show that the discriminator’s logit at the optimum is \(\log(p_{\text{data}}/p_g)\), and explain why this is remarkable given that neither density is available.
  4. Confirm \(\mathrm{JS} \le \log 2\) always, and characterise exactly when equality holds.
