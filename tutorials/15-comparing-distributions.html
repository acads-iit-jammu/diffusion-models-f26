07 · Comparing Distributions: From CDFs to KL, and the Two Families

A standalone tutorial accompanying W03. One question runs through it: given two distributions, how far apart are they? The answer is not unique, and the choices form two families with genuinely different behaviour. We start where the answer is easiest — one dimension, where you can just compare CDFs — and find that three familiar quantities are three different norms of the same gap. Then KL, its relationship to maximum likelihood, and finally the map of which divergence each family of generative model is actually minimising.

1. In one dimension, compare the CDFs

Two distributions \(P\) and \(Q\) on \(\mathbb{R}\), with CDFs \(F\) and \(G\). The CDF determines the distribution completely, so any honest measure of “how different are \(P\) and \(Q\)” can be read off the gap

\[ \Delta(x) \;=\; F(x) - G(x). \]

That is a function. To turn a function into a single number you take a norm — and the three standard choices give three quantities you have already met or are about to.

\[ \underbrace{\sup_x |\Delta(x)|}_{\textbf{Kolmogorov–Smirnov}} \qquad \underbrace{\int |\Delta(x)|\,dx}_{\textbf{Wasserstein-1}} \qquad \underbrace{\int \Delta(x)^2\,w(x)\,dx}_{\textbf{Cramér–von Mises}} \]

The \(L^\infty\), \(L^1\) and \(L^2\) norms of one object. That is the whole taxonomy of one-dimensional distances-between-CDFs.

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic

\[ D(P,Q) \;=\; \sup_x \big| F(x) - G(x) \big| . \]

The largest vertical gap between the two CDF curves. It is always in \([0,1]\), it is a genuine metric, and it is distribution-free in a useful sense: if you replace \(P\) by any strictly increasing transformation of itself and do the same to \(Q\), \(D\) does not change — because relabelling the axis slides both curves identically. That invariance is why the KS test has a universal critical value table that does not depend on what you are testing.

Its empirical version is what you would actually compute: replace \(F\) by the empirical CDF \(\hat F_n(x) = \frac1n\#\{i : x_i \le x\}\), a staircase with a step of \(1/n\) at each sample, and take the sup — which is attained at one of the data points, so it is a finite maximum.

For \(P = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(Q = \mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\) the sup sits at \(x=\mu/2\) by symmetry, giving \(D = 2\Phi(\mu/2) - 1\): \(0.197\) at \(\mu = 0.5\), \(0.383\) at \(\mu=1\), \(0.954\) at \(\mu = 4\). Notice how it saturates — by \(\mu = 4\) it has nearly exhausted its range and can barely distinguish \(\mu = 4\) from \(\mu = 40\).

The \(L^1\) norm is a transport cost

Take the same gap and integrate its absolute value instead of maximising it:

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big| F(x) - G(x)\big|\,dx \;=\; W_1(P,Q), \]

the Wasserstein-1 distance — and this is exactly the quantity Transport You Can Compute derives as the cheapest way to move the mass of \(P\) onto \(Q\) when moving a unit of mass a distance \(|x-y|\) costs \(|x-y|\). The area between the CDF curves is the transport bill.

So the bridge to transport is not an analogy. In one dimension the optimal transport cost and an \(L^1\) norm of the CDF gap are the same number, and the monotone (quantile) coupling is what achieves it.

Unlike KS, \(W_1\) does not saturate: for \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) against \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\) it equals exactly \(|\mu|\), growing without bound. It answers “how far?” rather than “how distinguishable?”, and that difference will turn out to matter a great deal in §6.

And the \(L^2\) norm

\(\int (F-G)^2 w\,dx\) gives the Cramér–von Mises family; with the weight \(w = 1/(G(1-G))\) it is the Anderson–Darling statistic, which up-weights the tails and is correspondingly better at detecting differences out there. These are goodness-of-fit tools rather than modelling objectives, so we will not use them further — but they belong on the map.

Histograms: the same story, discretised

For distributions on a finite ordered set of bins, the integral becomes a sum and \(W_1\) becomes the Earth Mover’s Distance: \(W_1(p,q) = \sum_k |F_p(k) - F_q(k)|\), which is the discrete transport problem of tutorial 09 — solvable exactly as a min-cost flow, with the Transport Plan Lab to play with.

Where this whole approach stops working

Every construction above needs a CDF, and a CDF needs the real line to be ordered — \(F(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \le x)\) has no meaning without \(\le\). In \(\mathbb{R}^d\) there is no canonical order, no canonical CDF, and no canonical “area between the curves”.

Transport survives, because a cost \(c(x,y)\) makes sense in any dimension — but the closed form is gone and you are solving an optimisation. What we need instead is a way to compare distributions pointwise, using only the values of the two densities at the same point and never their order. That is KL.

2. KL divergence

For distributions with densities \(p\) and \(q\),

\[ \boxed{\;\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) \;=\; \int p(x)\,\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\!\left[\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right].\;} \]

No ordering, no CDF, no dimension limit: it compares the two densities at each point, through their ratio, and averages the log-ratio under \(p\).

It is non-negative, \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \ge 0\) with equality only when \(p = q\) almost everywhere. This is Jensen from the inequalities tutorial: \(-\log\) is convex, so

\[ \mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[-\log\frac{q}{p}\right] \;\ge\; -\log \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[\frac{q}{p}\right] = -\log\!\int q = 0 . \]

It is not a metric. It is asymmetric, \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \neq \mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\), and fails the triangle inequality. Hence divergence, not distance.

It is infinite when supports disagree. If \(p(x) > 0\) somewhere \(q(x) = 0\), the integrand is \(+\infty\). This is not a technicality — §6 is about the consequences.

Gaussian closed form, which is why this course can compute with it:

\[ \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\,\big\|\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\big) = \log\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1} + \frac{\sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} - \frac12 . \]

With equal variances this collapses to \((\mu_1-\mu_2)^2/2\sigma^2\) — a squared distance. Every KL term in a diffusion model’s training objective is an instance of this formula, which is the reason the objective reduces to a sum of squared errors.

3. KL is maximum likelihood in disguise

This is the connection that makes KL more than one option among many.

Let \(p_{\text{data}}\) be the true distribution and \(p_\theta\) your model. Expand:

\[ \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta) = \underbrace{\int p_{\text{data}}\log p_{\text{data}}}_{-H(p_{\text{data}}),\ \text{no } \theta} \;-\; \int p_{\text{data}}\log p_\theta . \]

The first term does not involve \(\theta\) at all. So

\[ \arg\min_\theta \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta) = \arg\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log p_\theta(x)\big], \]

and replacing the expectation by its sample average over \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) gives

\[ \arg\max_\theta \;\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(x_i), \]

which is exactly the maximum likelihood estimator of Distributions and Maximum Likelihood.

The sentence to remember

Maximum likelihood is minimising the forward KL from the data distribution to the model. Every time you fit by MLE — the Gaussian fit, logistic regression via BCE, a normalizing flow — you are minimising \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)\) whether or not anyone says so. The empirical average is just the Monte Carlo estimate of an integral you never write down.

Which way round? Forward and reverse KL behave differently

Because KL is asymmetric, the two directions optimise for different things.

\(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)\) — forward \(\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\|p_{\text{data}})\) — reverse
Penalises \(p_\theta\) small where data is \(p_\theta\) large where data is not
Behaviour mode-covering: spreads to cover all the data mode-seeking: locks onto one mode
Failure mode blurry averages over modes ignores modes entirely
Used by maximum likelihood, flows, diffusion bounds variational inference, the VAE’s \(q\)

The forward direction cannot afford \(p_\theta \approx 0\) anywhere the data lives — the log ratio explodes — so it hedges and covers. The reverse direction is integrated under \(p_\theta\), so it simply avoids putting mass in the wrong place and is content to ignore parts of the target. This is exactly the single-Gaussian-fitted-to-a-mixture trap of tutorial 02, now with a name.

The gap is not subtle. Fit a single Gaussian to an equal mixture of \(\mathcal{N}(-2, 0.7^2)\) and \(\mathcal{N}(2, 0.7^2)\) and the two criteria give completely different answers, each of which the other criterion scores as terrible:

Fit \(\mu\) \(\sigma\) \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) \(\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\)
forward optimum \(0.00\) \(2.12\) 0.42 0.81
reverse optimum \(\pm1.98\) \(0.73\) 6.84 0.69

The forward optimum is exactly moment matching — for a Gaussian family, minimising \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) means matching the mean and variance of \(p\), which is why it lands between the modes where no data lives. The reverse optimum sits on a mode and is the same distance from either, so which one it finds depends entirely on where the optimiser starts.

Interactive — The KL Lab draws both optima together, shades the two integrands so you can see where each direction pays its penalty, and plots both objectives against \(\mu\): forward KL has a single basin, reverse KL has one per mode with a barrier between them.

4. KL is one of a family: the \(f\)-divergences

Fix a convex \(f\) with \(f(1) = 0\) and define

\[ D_f(p \,\|\, q) \;=\; \int q(x)\, f\!\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) dx . \]

Convexity plus \(f(1)=0\) gives \(D_f \ge 0\) with equality at \(p=q\), by Jensen — the same one-line argument as for KL. Different \(f\), different divergence:

\(f(t)\) Divergence Note
\(t\log t\) \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) forward KL
\(-\log t\) \(\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\) reverse KL
\(\tfrac12\lvert t-1\rvert\) total variation the only one that is also a metric
\((t-1)^2\) \(\chi^2\) heavy penalty on ratio spikes
\((\sqrt t - 1)^2\) squared Hellinger bounded, symmetric
\(t\log\frac{2t}{t+1} + \log\frac{2}{t+1}\) Jensen–Shannon symmetric, bounded by \(\log 2\)

All of these share one structural feature, and it is the one that matters: they depend on \(p\) and \(q\) only through the pointwise ratio \(p/q\). They never look at where the points are. Move a lump of \(q\)’s mass from just beside \(p\) to a mile away and no \(f\)-divergence notices, because the ratio at every point is unchanged.

5. Wasserstein, and what changes in more than one dimension

So far \(W_1\) has appeared only through its one-dimensional formula \(\int |F - G|\). That formula is a happy accident of the line, and it is worth being precise about what the general object is before relying on it.

The Kantorovich definition. For \(p \ge 1\) and distributions \(P, Q\) on \(\mathbb{R}^d\),

\[ W_p(P,Q) \;=\; \left( \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(P,Q)} \int \lVert x - y \rVert^{p} \, d\gamma(x,y) \right)^{1/p}, \]

where \(\Pi(P,Q)\) is the set of couplings — joint distributions on \(\mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}^d\) whose marginals are \(P\) and \(Q\). A coupling is a plan for moving mass; the integral is what the plan costs; \(W_p\) is the cost of the cheapest one. Nothing here mentions densities, which is exactly why it survives when densities do not exist.

Why one dimension is special

On the line there is a total order, and that changes everything. The optimal plan is forced to be monotone — cheapest transport never lets paths cross — and the monotone coupling is unique and explicit. It is the one matching quantile to quantile, which gives, for every \(p \ge 1\),

\[ W_p(P,Q) = \left( \int_0^1 \big| F^{-1}(u) - G^{-1}(u) \big|^{p} du \right)^{1/p} . \]

Sort both samples, subtract, average. \(O(n \log n)\), and no optimisation at all. (Checked against an exact assignment solver on 3,000-point samples: the sorted matching reproduces the optimal cost to machine precision at \(p = 1, 2, 3\).)

The CDF formula is \(p = 1\) only

The quantile formula above holds for all \(p\). The other one-dimensional formula, the one in §1,

\[ W_1(P,Q) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big| F(x) - G(x) \big| \, dx , \]

does not generalise. At \(p=1\) the area between the two CDFs happens to equal the area between the two quantile functions — the same region, measured along different axes — so the two expressions agree. For \(p > 1\) that coincidence breaks, and \(\big(\int |F-G|^p dx\big)^{1/p}\) is not \(W_p\) and is not any Wasserstein distance.

Concretely, for \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) against \(\mathcal{N}(1.5, 2^2)\):

quantity value
\(\int_0^1 \lvert F^{-1}-G^{-1}\rvert\,du\) 1.5586
\(\int \lvert F-G\rvert\,dx\) 1.5586   (agree, as they must at \(p=1\))
\(W_2 = \big(\int_0^1 \lvert F^{-1}-G^{-1}\rvert^2 du\big)^{1/2}\) 1.8028
\(\big(\int \lvert F-G\rvert^2 dx\big)^{1/2}\) 0.6918   (not \(W_2\), not anything)

When in doubt, work with quantiles: that form is the one that generalises in \(p\). Neither form generalises in dimension, which is the subject of the rest of this section.

For one-dimensional Gaussians the quantile integral collapses to a formula worth remembering, and it is the \(d=1\) case of Bures–Wasserstein below:

\[ W_2\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2),\ \mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\big) = \sqrt{(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2 + (\sigma_1-\sigma_2)^2} . \]

(For the pair above: \(\sqrt{1.5^2 + 1^2} = 1.8028\), matching the numeric quantile integral.)

In \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with \(d > 1\) there is no total order, so this construction simply has nothing to stand on. There is no quantile function, and no reason a cheapest plan should be expressible in closed form. What survives is weaker but still substantial:

  • It is still a linear program. For empirical measures on \(n\) points each, the Kantorovich problem is an \(n \times n\) assignment problem. Exact solvers cost about \(O(n^3 \log n)\) — fine for thousands of points, hopeless for millions.
  • The dual still holds. Kantorovich–Rubinstein gives, for \(p = 1\), \(W_1 = \sup_{\lVert f \rVert_{\mathrm{Lip}} \le 1} \mathbb{E}_P[f] - \mathbb{E}_Q[f]\), which is what a WGAN critic estimates.
  • Brenier’s theorem replaces the quantile map. If \(P\) is absolutely continuous, the optimal \(p=2\) plan is deterministic: there is a map \(T\) with \(T_\# P = Q\) attaining the infimum, and it is the gradient of a convex function, \(T = \nabla \varphi\). In one dimension “\(\nabla\varphi\) with \(\varphi\) convex” means “increasing”, so this is precisely the monotone quantile map generalised. Convexity is what replaces the lost total order.

One case with a closed form: Gaussians

Between \(\mathcal{N}(m_1, C_1)\) and \(\mathcal{N}(m_2, C_2)\) in any dimension,

\[ \boxed{\;W_2^2 \;=\; \lVert m_1 - m_2 \rVert^2 \;+\; \operatorname{tr}\!\Big( C_1 + C_2 - 2\big(C_2^{1/2} C_1 C_2^{1/2}\big)^{1/2} \Big).\;} \]

This is the Bures–Wasserstein distance. When the covariances commute — in particular when both are diagonal — the trace term collapses to \(\lVert C_1^{1/2} - C_2^{1/2} \rVert_F^2\), so the means contribute Euclidean distance and the covariances contribute Frobenius distance between the square roots.

(Checked numerically: for a 2-D pair the formula gives \(2.35331\), and exact optimal transport between \(4{,}000\)-point empirical samples gives \(2.35356\).)

This is the formula behind the Fréchet Inception Distance, which is simply \(W_2\) between two Gaussians fitted to network features — accurate only to the extent that those features really are Gaussian.

The two costs of using it in high dimension

Computation is the visible cost, and it is the one people usually try to fix. Entropic regularisation adds \(\varepsilon H(\gamma)\) to the objective, which makes it strictly convex and solvable by Sinkhorn iterations costing \(O(n^2)\) each — fast and differentiable, at the price of a blurred plan and a biased distance.

Statistics is the cost that actually bites. The empirical Wasserstein distance converges to the truth at rate

\[ \mathbb{E}\,W_p(P_n, P) \;\asymp\; n^{-1/d} \qquad (d > 2p), \]

which is the curse of dimensionality again, in a new costume. Measured on independent samples from \(\mathcal{N}(0, I_d)\):

\(d\) fitted slope of \(\log W_2\) against \(\log n\) \(-1/d\)
5 \(-0.193\) \(-0.200\)
10 \(-0.106\) \(-0.100\)
20 \(-0.065\) \(-0.050\)

So a Wasserstein distance estimated from samples in high dimension is mostly noise, however exactly you solve the transport problem. Solving the wrong problem precisely does not help.

Sliced Wasserstein is the standard dodge: project both distributions onto a random direction \(\theta\), where the closed form applies, and average over directions,

\[ SW_p^p(P,Q) = \int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}} W_p^p\big(\theta_\# P,\ \theta_\# Q\big)\, d\theta . \]

Each slice is a sort. It is a genuine metric, it is cheap, and it inherits the better one-dimensional rate — at the cost of being a weaker comparison that can be fooled by structure invisible to every projection.

6. Integral probability metrics, in general

Wasserstein and MMD are both instances of one construction. Given a class \(\mathcal{F}\) of real-valued test functions,

\[ d_{\mathcal{F}}(P,Q) \;=\; \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \Big| \mathbb{E}_{P}[f] - \mathbb{E}_{Q}[f] \Big| . \]

Read it as a search for a witness: a single function on which the two distributions disagree as loudly as possible. If no test function can tell them apart, they are the same as far as \(\mathcal{F}\) can see.

Everything depends on how rich \(\mathcal{F}\) is, and the choice is a genuine trade-off in both directions:

  • \(\mathcal{F}\) too small and the metric is blind — it will report zero for distributions that genuinely differ.
  • \(\mathcal{F}\) too large and the supremum cannot be estimated from finitely many samples — the empirical witness overfits, and the estimate is biased upward no matter how much data you have.
Class \(\mathcal{F}\) Resulting IPM Witness is
\(\lVert f \rVert_{\mathrm{Lip}} \le 1\) Wasserstein-1 a Lipschitz function — the WGAN critic
unit ball of an RKHS \(\mathcal{H}\) MMD available in closed form (§7)
\(\lVert f \rVert_\infty \le \tfrac12\) total variation an indicator of a set
bounded, with bounded derivatives Dudley metric a smooth bump

Two structural remarks. First, IPMs are symmetric and satisfy the triangle inequality whenever \(\mathcal{F}\) is symmetric (\(f \in \mathcal{F} \Rightarrow -f \in \mathcal{F}\)), so they are honest metrics — unlike KL. Second, and this is the substantive contrast with §4, an IPM is defined through expectations of functions on the space, so it knows how far apart points are. An \(f\)-divergence is defined through a ratio of densities at the same point, so it knows only how much the two disagree there, never how far anything had to move. That single difference is what §8 is about.

Total variation is the intersection. It is an \(f\)-divergence (\(f(t) = \tfrac12|t-1|\)) and an IPM (bounded test functions) and a metric — essentially the only member of both families, which is a good way to remember that they are otherwise disjoint.

7. Maximum mean discrepancy: the IPM you can actually compute

Wasserstein’s definition contains an infimum over couplings; the IPM form contains a supremum over functions. Both are optimisation problems. MMD is the choice of \(\mathcal{F}\) for which the optimisation can be solved in closed form, and that is essentially the whole reason it exists.

Take \(\mathcal{F}\) to be the unit ball of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space \(\mathcal{H}\) with kernel \(k\). Define the kernel mean embedding of a distribution,

\[ \mu_P \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\big[\, k(X, \cdot)\,\big] \;\in\; \mathcal{H}, \]

a single point in \(\mathcal{H}\) that summarises the whole distribution. The reproducing property gives \(\mathbb{E}_P[f] = \langle f, \mu_P\rangle\) for every \(f \in \mathcal{H}\), so

\[ \mathrm{MMD}(P,Q) = \sup_{\lVert f \rVert_{\mathcal{H}} \le 1} \langle f, \mu_P - \mu_Q \rangle = \lVert \mu_P - \mu_Q \rVert_{\mathcal{H}} . \]

The supremum is attained by the obvious witness, \(f \propto \mu_P - \mu_Q\), and the value is just a norm. Expanding it,

\[ \boxed{\;\mathrm{MMD}^2(P,Q) = \mathbb{E}\,k(X,X') - 2\,\mathbb{E}\,k(X,Y) + \mathbb{E}\,k(Y,Y')\;} \]

with \(X, X' \sim P\) and \(Y, Y' \sim Q\) independent. Three expectations of a kernel — no densities, no couplings, no optimisation. Replacing them by sample averages (dropping diagonal terms) gives an unbiased estimator computable in \(O(n^2)\), or \(O(n)\) with a linear-time variant.

Does it separate distributions? Only if the kernel is characteristic, meaning \(P \mapsto \mu_P\) is injective. The Gaussian RBF and Laplace kernels are; a linear kernel is not — it embeds only the mean, so it cannot distinguish any two distributions with the same mean. With a characteristic kernel, \(\mathrm{MMD}(P,Q) = 0 \iff P = Q\).

A closed form to test against. For \(P = \mathcal{N}(a, I_d)\), \(Q = \mathcal{N}(b, I_d)\) and Gaussian kernel of bandwidth \(s\),

\[ \mathrm{MMD}^2 = \frac{2\,s^d}{(2+s^2)^{d/2}} \left(1 - \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\lVert a-b\rVert^2}{2(2+s^2)}\Big)\right). \]

(Checked: at \(\lVert a - b\rVert = 1\) and \(s = \sqrt{d}\), the formula gives \(0.1175\), \(0.0328\), \(0.0072\) for \(d = 2, 10, 50\), and the sample estimator returns \(0.1214\), \(0.0317\), \(0.0071\).)

The headline property, and its asterisk

The MMD estimator converges at \(n^{-1/2}\) independently of the dimension — there is no \(n^{-1/d}\) anywhere. Measured slopes of \(\log|\hat{}-\text{truth}|\) against \(\log n\): \(-0.55\) at \(d=2\), \(-0.71\) at \(d=10\), \(-0.48\) at \(d=50\). Against Wasserstein’s \(n^{-1/d}\) this looks like a free lunch.

It is not a free lunch

Look again at the closed form above. For a fixed separation \(\lVert a-b\rVert = 1\), the population MMD itself falls with dimension — \(0.1175\), \(0.0328\), \(0.0072\) at \(d = 2, 10, 50\). The error shrinks at \(n^{-1/2}\) regardless of \(d\), but the signal you are trying to detect shrinks too. What matters for a test is the ratio, and that still degrades.

So the honest statement is: MMD moves the curse from the estimation rate into the effect size. It does not abolish it. It also depends on a bandwidth \(s\) that you must choose — the median heuristic (set \(s\) to the median pairwise distance) is the usual default and is a heuristic, not a theorem.

MMD’s real strengths are that it needs only samples, costs \(O(n^2)\) with no inner optimisation, is differentiable in the samples — hence usable directly as a training loss, as in MMD-GAN — and comes with a clean two-sample test (Gretton et al.), which is how one asks “did my generative model actually match the data?” with a \(p\)-value attached.

Choosing between them

\(f\)-divergence (KL, JS) Wasserstein MMD
Needs densities yes no no
Sees geometry no yes partly — through the kernel
Disjoint supports saturates degrades gracefully degrades gracefully
Closed form in \(d\) dims Gaussians only Gaussians only (Bures) any kernel, from samples
Cost from \(n\) samples needs a density model \(O(n^3\log n)\), or \(O(n^2)\) Sinkhorn \(O(n^2)\), no optimisation

8. Why the distinction is not pedantry: disjoint supports

Take \(P\) uniform on \([0,1]\) and \(Q\) uniform on \([d, d+1]\) — the same shape, shifted by \(d\). Watch what each measure reports as \(d\) grows:

shift \(d\) overlap \(\mathrm{KL}\) JS TV \(W_1\)
0.5 0.5 \(\infty\) \(< \log 2\) 0.50 0.50
1.0 0 \(\infty\) \(\log 2\) 1 1.00
2.0 0 \(\infty\) \(\log 2\) 1 2.00
5.0 0 \(\infty\) \(\log 2\) 1 5.00

Once the supports separate, every \(f\)-divergence stops responding. KL is already infinite; JS is pinned at \(\log 2\); TV is pinned at 1. They report “as different as possible” and cannot distinguish a near miss from a wild one. Their gradients with respect to \(d\) are zero — so a model trained on them gets no signal telling it which way to move.

\(W_1\) reports \(d\). It keeps growing, and its gradient points home.

This is the WGAN argument

Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou (2017) built Wasserstein GAN on exactly this observation. A generator’s output distribution is supported on a low-dimensional manifold in a high-dimensional space — the manifold hypothesis of Measuring Things — and so is the data. Two such manifolds generically intersect in a set of measure zero, so the supports are essentially disjoint from the start, and JS (which the original GAN minimises at an optimal discriminator) is pinned at \(\log 2\) with vanishing gradient. Replacing it with \(W_1\), estimated through the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual with a Lipschitz-constrained critic, restores a usable gradient.

Notice that the same fact motivates diffusion models’ use of noise, for the same reason: convolving with a Gaussian makes the supports overlap, so the ratio-based quantities become finite and informative again.

9. The map: what is each model family minimising?

Model Objective, in divergence terms
Maximum likelihood / normalizing flows forward \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)\), exactly (§3)
VAE / autoencoders maximise the ELBO \(= \log p(x) - \mathrm{KL}(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z\mid x))\); the trainable form contains an explicit \(\mathrm{KL}(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z))\) against the prior
Original GAN at an optimal discriminator, Jensen–Shannon — an \(f\)-divergence
\(f\)-GAN any \(f\)-divergence, via its variational (Fenchel) representation
WGAN \(W_1\) — an IPM, deliberately not an \(f\)-divergence
MMD nets MMD — also an IPM
Diffusion models a variational bound that decomposes into a sum of KLs between Gaussians, each with the closed form of §2; equivalently, denoising score matching

Read down that column and the structure the whole field shares comes into view: every generative model is minimising some divergence between the data distribution and the model’s. What distinguishes the families is which divergence, and — because it is usually not computable directly — how they get at it: a bound (VAE), a learned dual (GAN, WGAN), a closed form after adding noise (diffusion), or exactly (flows).

Two rows of that table are now cashed in. Note 08 takes the case where you can write the target down up to a constant and builds a Markov chain that samples it. Note 09 takes the case where you cannot, and shows that training a classifier to separate real from generated samples estimates the density ratio \(p_{\text{data}}/p_g\) — and that the resulting game minimises exactly the Jensen–Shannon divergence in the table above, with §8’s saturation as its central difficulty.

Key takeaway

In one dimension everything is a norm of the CDF gap: sup gives Kolmogorov–Smirnov, \(L^1\) gives Wasserstein-1 which is a transport cost, \(L^2\) gives Cramér–von Mises. Beyond one dimension the CDF fails and two families take over. \(f\)-divergences — KL, JS, TV, \(\chi^2\) — compare densities through their pointwise ratio and are blind to distance, which makes them infinite or saturated when supports disagree. Integral probability metrics — Wasserstein, MMD — compare through test functions and do see distance, which is why they keep working when the supports come apart. Total variation is the one member of both.
