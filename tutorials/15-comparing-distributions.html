08 · Comparing Distributions: From CDFs to KL, and the Two Families

A standalone tutorial accompanying W03. One question runs through it: given two distributions, how far apart are they? The answer is not unique, and the choices form two families with genuinely different behaviour. We start where the answer is easiest — one dimension, where you can just compare CDFs — and find that three familiar quantities are three different norms of the same gap. Then KL, its relationship to maximum likelihood, and finally the map of which divergence each family of generative model is actually minimising.

1. In one dimension, compare the CDFs

Two distributions \(P\) and \(Q\) on \(\mathbb{R}\), with CDFs \(F\) and \(G\). The CDF determines the distribution completely, so any honest measure of “how different are \(P\) and \(Q\)” can be read off the gap

\[ \Delta(x) \;=\; F(x) - G(x). \]

That is a function. To turn a function into a single number you take a norm — and the three standard choices give three quantities you have already met or are about to.

\[ \underbrace{\sup_x |\Delta(x)|}_{\textbf{Kolmogorov–Smirnov}} \qquad \underbrace{\int |\Delta(x)|\,dx}_{\textbf{Wasserstein-1}} \qquad \underbrace{\int \Delta(x)^2\,w(x)\,dx}_{\textbf{Cramér–von Mises}} \]

The \(L^\infty\), \(L^1\) and \(L^2\) norms of one object. That is the whole taxonomy of one-dimensional distances-between-CDFs.

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic

\[ D(P,Q) \;=\; \sup_x \big| F(x) - G(x) \big| . \]

The largest vertical gap between the two CDF curves. It is always in \([0,1]\), it is a genuine metric, and it is distribution-free in a useful sense: if you replace \(P\) by any strictly increasing transformation of itself and do the same to \(Q\), \(D\) does not change — because relabelling the axis slides both curves identically. That invariance is why the KS test has a universal critical value table that does not depend on what you are testing.

Its empirical version is what you would actually compute: replace \(F\) by the empirical CDF \(\hat F_n(x) = \frac1n\#\{i : x_i \le x\}\), a staircase with a step of \(1/n\) at each sample, and take the sup — which is attained at one of the data points, so it is a finite maximum.

For \(P = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(Q = \mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\) the sup sits at \(x=\mu/2\) by symmetry, giving \(D = 2\Phi(\mu/2) - 1\): \(0.197\) at \(\mu = 0.5\), \(0.383\) at \(\mu=1\), \(0.954\) at \(\mu = 4\). Notice how it saturates — by \(\mu = 4\) it has nearly exhausted its range and can barely distinguish \(\mu = 4\) from \(\mu = 40\).

The \(L^1\) norm is a transport cost

Take the same gap and integrate its absolute value instead of maximising it:

\[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big| F(x) - G(x)\big|\,dx \;=\; W_1(P,Q), \]

the Wasserstein-1 distance — and this is exactly the quantity Transport You Can Compute derives as the cheapest way to move the mass of \(P\) onto \(Q\) when moving a unit of mass a distance \(|x-y|\) costs \(|x-y|\). The area between the CDF curves is the transport bill.

So the bridge to transport is not an analogy. In one dimension the optimal transport cost and an \(L^1\) norm of the CDF gap are the same number, and the monotone (quantile) coupling is what achieves it.

Unlike KS, \(W_1\) does not saturate: for \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) against \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\) it equals exactly \(|\mu|\), growing without bound. It answers “how far?” rather than “how distinguishable?”, and that difference will turn out to matter a great deal in §6.

And the \(L^2\) norm

\(\int (F-G)^2 w\,dx\) gives the Cramér–von Mises family; with the weight \(w = 1/(G(1-G))\) it is the Anderson–Darling statistic, which up-weights the tails and is correspondingly better at detecting differences out there. These are goodness-of-fit tools rather than modelling objectives, so we will not use them further — but they belong on the map.

Histograms: the same story, discretised

For distributions on a finite ordered set of bins, the integral becomes a sum and \(W_1\) becomes the Earth Mover’s Distance: \(W_1(p,q) = \sum_k |F_p(k) - F_q(k)|\), which is the discrete transport problem of tutorial 09 — solvable exactly as a min-cost flow, with the Transport Plan Lab to play with.

Where this whole approach stops working

Every construction above needs a CDF, and a CDF needs the real line to be ordered — \(F(x) = \mathbb{P}(X \le x)\) has no meaning without \(\le\). In \(\mathbb{R}^d\) there is no canonical order, no canonical CDF, and no canonical “area between the curves”.

Transport survives, because a cost \(c(x,y)\) makes sense in any dimension — but the closed form is gone and you are solving an optimisation. What we need instead is a way to compare distributions pointwise, using only the values of the two densities at the same point and never their order. That is KL.

2. KL divergence

For distributions with densities \(p\) and \(q\),

\[ \boxed{\;\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) \;=\; \int p(x)\,\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\!\left[\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right].\;} \]

No ordering, no CDF, no dimension limit: it compares the two densities at each point, through their ratio, and averages the log-ratio under \(p\).

It is non-negative, \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \ge 0\) with equality only when \(p = q\) almost everywhere. This is Jensen from the inequalities tutorial: \(-\log\) is convex, so

\[ \mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[-\log\frac{q}{p}\right] \;\ge\; -\log \mathbb{E}_p\!\left[\frac{q}{p}\right] = -\log\!\int q = 0 . \]

It is not a metric. It is asymmetric, \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \neq \mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\), and fails the triangle inequality. Hence divergence, not distance.

It is infinite when supports disagree. If \(p(x) > 0\) somewhere \(q(x) = 0\), the integrand is \(+\infty\). This is not a technicality — §6 is about the consequences.

Gaussian closed form, which is why this course can compute with it:

\[ \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\,\big\|\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\big) = \log\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1} + \frac{\sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} - \frac12 . \]

With equal variances this collapses to \((\mu_1-\mu_2)^2/2\sigma^2\) — a squared distance. Every KL term in a diffusion model’s training objective is an instance of this formula, which is the reason the objective reduces to a sum of squared errors.

3. KL is maximum likelihood in disguise

This is the connection that makes KL more than one option among many.

Let \(p_{\text{data}}\) be the true distribution and \(p_\theta\) your model. Expand:

\[ \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta) = \underbrace{\int p_{\text{data}}\log p_{\text{data}}}_{-H(p_{\text{data}}),\ \text{no } \theta} \;-\; \int p_{\text{data}}\log p_\theta . \]

The first term does not involve \(\theta\) at all. So

\[ \arg\min_\theta \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta) = \arg\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log p_\theta(x)\big], \]

and replacing the expectation by its sample average over \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) gives

\[ \arg\max_\theta \;\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \log p_\theta(x_i), \]

which is exactly the maximum likelihood estimator of Distributions and Maximum Likelihood.

The sentence to remember

Maximum likelihood is minimising the forward KL from the data distribution to the model. Every time you fit by MLE — the Gaussian fit, logistic regression via BCE, a normalizing flow — you are minimising \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)\) whether or not anyone says so. The empirical average is just the Monte Carlo estimate of an integral you never write down.

Which way round? Forward and reverse KL behave differently

Because KL is asymmetric, the two directions optimise for different things.

\(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)\) — forward \(\mathrm{KL}(p_\theta\|p_{\text{data}})\) — reverse
Penalises \(p_\theta\) small where data is \(p_\theta\) large where data is not
Behaviour mode-covering: spreads to cover all the data mode-seeking: locks onto one mode
Failure mode blurry averages over modes ignores modes entirely
Used by maximum likelihood, flows, diffusion bounds variational inference, the VAE’s \(q\)

The forward direction cannot afford \(p_\theta \approx 0\) anywhere the data lives — the log ratio explodes — so it hedges and covers. The reverse direction is integrated under \(p_\theta\), so it simply avoids putting mass in the wrong place and is content to ignore parts of the target. This is exactly the single-Gaussian-fitted-to-a-mixture trap of tutorial 02, now with a name.

4. KL is one of a family: the \(f\)-divergences

Fix a convex \(f\) with \(f(1) = 0\) and define

\[ D_f(p \,\|\, q) \;=\; \int q(x)\, f\!\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) dx . \]

Convexity plus \(f(1)=0\) gives \(D_f \ge 0\) with equality at \(p=q\), by Jensen — the same one-line argument as for KL. Different \(f\), different divergence:

\(f(t)\) Divergence Note
\(t\log t\) \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) forward KL
\(-\log t\) \(\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\) reverse KL
\(\tfrac12\lvert t-1\rvert\) total variation the only one that is also a metric
\((t-1)^2\) \(\chi^2\) heavy penalty on ratio spikes
\((\sqrt t - 1)^2\) squared Hellinger bounded, symmetric
\(t\log\frac{2t}{t+1} + \log\frac{2}{t+1}\) Jensen–Shannon symmetric, bounded by \(\log 2\)

All of these share one structural feature, and it is the one that matters: they depend on \(p\) and \(q\) only through the pointwise ratio \(p/q\). They never look at where the points are. Move a lump of \(q\)’s mass from just beside \(p\) to a mile away and no \(f\)-divergence notices, because the ratio at every point is unchanged.

5. Wasserstein is not an \(f\)-divergence

This is worth stating plainly, because it is a common and consequential slip.

\(W_1\) cannot be written as \(\int q\,f(p/q)\) for any \(f\). It is not in this family at all. It belongs to a second family, the integral probability metrics:

\[ d_{\mathcal{F}}(p,q) \;=\; \sup_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \left| \mathbb{E}_{p}[f] - \mathbb{E}_{q}[f] \right| , \]

defined by a class \(\mathcal{F}\) of test functions rather than by a convex \(f\) applied to a ratio.

Class \(\mathcal{F}\) Resulting IPM
\(\{f : \lVert f\rVert_{\mathrm{Lip}} \le 1\}\) Wasserstein-1 (Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality)
unit ball of an RKHS maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
\(\{f : \lVert f \rVert_\infty \le \tfrac12\}\) total variation

The two families are genuinely different, and the reason is the one flagged above: \(f\)-divergences see only ratios and are blind to geometry; IPMs test against functions defined on the space and therefore do see distance.

Total variation is the intersection. It is an \(f\)-divergence (\(f(t) = \tfrac12|t-1|\)) and an IPM (bounded test functions) and a metric. It is essentially the only member of both families — which is a good way to remember that the families are otherwise disjoint.

6. Why the distinction is not pedantry: disjoint supports

Take \(P\) uniform on \([0,1]\) and \(Q\) uniform on \([d, d+1]\) — the same shape, shifted by \(d\). Watch what each measure reports as \(d\) grows:

shift \(d\) overlap \(\mathrm{KL}\) JS TV \(W_1\)
0.5 0.5 \(\infty\) \(< \log 2\) 0.50 0.50
1.0 0 \(\infty\) \(\log 2\) 1 1.00
2.0 0 \(\infty\) \(\log 2\) 1 2.00
5.0 0 \(\infty\) \(\log 2\) 1 5.00

Once the supports separate, every \(f\)-divergence stops responding. KL is already infinite; JS is pinned at \(\log 2\); TV is pinned at 1. They report “as different as possible” and cannot distinguish a near miss from a wild one. Their gradients with respect to \(d\) are zero — so a model trained on them gets no signal telling it which way to move.

\(W_1\) reports \(d\). It keeps growing, and its gradient points home.

This is the WGAN argument

Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou (2017) built Wasserstein GAN on exactly this observation. A generator’s output distribution is supported on a low-dimensional manifold in a high-dimensional space — the manifold hypothesis of Measuring Things — and so is the data. Two such manifolds generically intersect in a set of measure zero, so the supports are essentially disjoint from the start, and JS (which the original GAN minimises at an optimal discriminator) is pinned at \(\log 2\) with vanishing gradient. Replacing it with \(W_1\), estimated through the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual with a Lipschitz-constrained critic, restores a usable gradient.

Notice that the same fact motivates diffusion models’ use of noise, for the same reason: convolving with a Gaussian makes the supports overlap, so the ratio-based quantities become finite and informative again.

7. The map: what is each model family minimising?

Model Objective, in divergence terms
Maximum likelihood / normalizing flows forward \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)\), exactly (§3)
VAE / autoencoders maximise the ELBO \(= \log p(x) - \mathrm{KL}(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z\mid x))\); the trainable form contains an explicit \(\mathrm{KL}(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z))\) against the prior
Original GAN at an optimal discriminator, Jensen–Shannon — an \(f\)-divergence
\(f\)-GAN any \(f\)-divergence, via its variational (Fenchel) representation
WGAN \(W_1\) — an IPM, deliberately not an \(f\)-divergence
MMD nets MMD — also an IPM
Diffusion models a variational bound that decomposes into a sum of KLs between Gaussians, each with the closed form of §2; equivalently, denoising score matching

Read down that column and the structure the whole field shares comes into view: every generative model is minimising some divergence between the data distribution and the model’s. What distinguishes the families is which divergence, and — because it is usually not computable directly — how they get at it: a bound (VAE), a learned dual (GAN, WGAN), a closed form after adding noise (diffusion), or exactly (flows).

Key takeaway

In one dimension everything is a norm of the CDF gap: sup gives Kolmogorov–Smirnov, \(L^1\) gives Wasserstein-1 which is a transport cost, \(L^2\) gives Cramér–von Mises. Beyond one dimension the CDF fails and two families take over. \(f\)-divergences — KL, JS, TV, \(\chi^2\) — compare densities through their pointwise ratio and are blind to distance, which makes them infinite or saturated when supports disagree. Integral probability metrics — Wasserstein, MMD — compare through test functions and do see distance, which is why they keep working when the supports come apart. Total variation is the one member of both.

Exercises

  1. For \(P = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(Q = \mathcal{N}(\mu,1)\), show the KS statistic is \(2\Phi(\mu/2)-1\) by finding where the CDF gap is maximised. Evaluate at \(\mu = 1\) and \(\mu = 4\) and comment on the saturation.
  2. Show \(\int|F-G|\,dx = W_1\) for these two Gaussians equals exactly \(|\mu|\), and explain why it does not saturate.
  3. Derive \(\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma^2)\|\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma^2)) = (\mu_1-\mu_2)^2/2\sigma^2\) from the general Gaussian formula, and say why this makes diffusion training objectives reduce to squared errors.
  4. Prove \(\mathrm{KL} \ge 0\) using Jensen, and identify exactly where equality requires \(p = q\) almost everywhere.
  5. Give two distributions with \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) finite and \(\mathrm{KL}(q\|p) = \infty\). (Hint: make one support strictly contain the other.)
  6. Verify that \(f(t) = t\log t\) gives KL and \(f(t) = \tfrac12|t-1|\) gives total variation in the definition \(D_f = \int q f(p/q)\).
  7. Explain in your own words why no \(f\)-divergence can distinguish “\(Q\) is just beside \(P\)” from “\(Q\) is a mile away”, once the supports are disjoint. Then say what an IPM does differently.
  8. For \(P\) uniform on \([0,1]\) and \(Q\) uniform on \([d,d+1]\) with \(d > 1\), compute \(W_1\), TV and JS. Which of the three would give a usable training gradient in \(d\), and why?
