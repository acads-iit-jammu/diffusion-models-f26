Probability-Flow Sampling: From Density Evolution to Samples

NoteTutorial 2 of 4 · Sampling

Question: Why does the learned score give a valid sampling velocity?

Before this: Learning the Score, including Gaussian increments and the expected Taylor step. No prior PDE course is needed.

Core route: particle motion → probability flux → density evolution → probability-flow ODE → backward sampling → examples. The multidimensional notation, integration-by-parts proof, and stochastic-sampler comparison are labelled subsections; the one-dimensional derivation gives the main argument.

Next: Diffusion, Neural ODEs, and Normalizing Flows.

Separate the particle question from the density question

A particle question asks: where does this starting point go? A density question asks: how is the whole population distributed at a given time? An ODE and an SDE can answer the particle question differently while giving the same density at every time. This tutorial constructs such a pair.

Write \(p_0=p_{\mathrm{data}}\) and let \(p_t\) be the forward-noised density from Tutorial 1. Our aim is to move random samples from \(p_T\) back to \(p_0\). For VP with enough signal attenuation, \(p_T\) is approximately standard Gaussian. Exact arguments use \(p_T\); practical samplers often substitute a Gaussian.

Recall: fields, trajectories, and flow maps

A vector field \(v(x,t)\) assigns a velocity vector to each location and time. The ODE \(\dot x_t=v(x_t,t)\) says that a particle follows its current local vector. For \(v(x)=-x\),

\[\dot x=-x,\qquad x_t=e^{-t}x_0,\qquad \Psi_{0\to t}(x_0)=e^{-t}x_0.\]

Separation of variables gives this solution when \(x_0\ne0\), and \(x\equiv0\) completes the equilibrium case. The flow map \(\Psi_{s\to t}\) collects the solutions for every starting state at time \(s\). Existence and uniqueness are needed for this map to be well defined; smooth fields with appropriate growth bounds suffice on the intervals we use. Tutorial 3 examines invertibility.

A deterministic map can generate random outputs: start it with a random input. But the contraction above takes \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) to \(\mathcal N(0,e^{-2t})\), so following the bare Gaussian score \(-x\) does not preserve Gaussian samples.

Recall: stochastic motion

The forward SDE is

\[dX_t=f(X_t,t)\,dt+g(t)\,dW_t.\]

Tutorial 1 explains its Euler–Maruyama approximation:

\[\widehat X_{k+1}=\widehat X_k+h f(\widehat X_k,t_k) +g(t_k)\sqrt h\,\xi_k,\qquad h>0.\]

The \(\xi_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) are independent. For VP, \(f=-\beta(t)x/2\), \(g^2=\beta(t)\), and the one-time conditional law is \(X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\epsilon\) with \(\sigma_t^2=1-\alpha_t^2\). A shared \(\epsilon\) across times is not a Brownian path.

Describe motion of an entire density

Derivation: probability flux in one dimension

Suppose particles move deterministically with velocity \(v(x,t)\). A small interval of width \(\Delta x\) contains probability approximately \(p_t(x)\Delta x\). Define probability flux

\[J(x,t)=p_t(x)v(x,t).\]

This is signed probability crossing a location per unit time. The interval gains mass from its left boundary and loses mass through its right boundary:

\[\frac{d}{dt}[p_t(x)\Delta x]=J(x,t)-J(x+\Delta x,t).\]

Expand \(J(x+\Delta x,t)=J(x,t)+\partial_xJ(x,t)\Delta x+o(\Delta x)\). After dividing by \(\Delta x\) and taking the limit,

\[\boxed{\partial_t p_t=-\partial_x(p_t v).}\]

This is the continuity equation, expressing conservation of probability. It is a partial differential equation (PDE) because \(p\) depends on both \(x\) and \(t\). The derivative \(\partial_t\) holds location fixed; the derivative \(\partial_x\) holds time fixed.

Multidimensional notation: gradient, divergence, Laplacian

For a scalar function \(p\), \(\nabla p\) collects its coordinate derivatives. For a vector field \(v=(v_1,\ldots,v_d)\), define

\[\nabla\cdot v=\sum_{i=1}^d\partial_{x_i}v_i, \qquad\Delta p=\sum_{i=1}^d\partial_{x_i}^2p=\nabla\cdot(\nabla p).\]

The first is divergence, measuring local expansion; the second is the Laplacian, adding the curvature in all coordinate directions. For \(v=x\), \(\nabla\cdot v=d>0\): a small cloud expands. For \(v=-x\), it contracts.

Apply the one-dimensional flux argument to opposite faces of a small box. The net outward flux in coordinate \(i\) is \(\partial_{x_i}(p_t v_i)\) times the box volume. Add all directions:

\[\boxed{\partial_t p_t=-\nabla\cdot(p_t v).}\]

No surface-integral theorem is needed for this box argument.

Derivation: how Gaussian spreading changes a density

Without drift, freeze \(g\) during a small positive step \(h\). Adding an independent \(\eta=g(t)\sqrt h\,\xi\) averages shifted copies of the old density:

\[p_{t+h}(x)\approx\mathbb E_\eta[p_t(x-\eta)].\]

This is the density-level version of the Euler–Maruyama step; for constant \(g\) and zero drift, that Gaussian increment is exact. Taylor expansion gives

\[p_t(x-\eta)=p_t(x)-\eta p_t'(x)+\tfrac12\eta^2p_t''(x)+\cdots.\]

The odd Gaussian moments vanish and \(\mathbb E\eta^2=g(t)^2h\). For sufficiently smooth densities, the frozen-step expansion is therefore

\[\mathbb E[p_t(x-\eta)] =p_t(x)+\tfrac12g(t)^2h\,p_t''(x)+O(h^2).\]

Dividing the change by \(h\) and taking the limit gives \(\partial_t p_t=\tfrac12g(t)^2\partial_x^2p_t\). The factor one half comes from Taylor’s quadratic term; it is not an arbitrary modeling constant.

Check at a Gaussian peak: \(p_t''(\mu)<0\), so the peak height decreases as noise spreads its mass. The same density gains mass farther out in its tails.

Drift contributes \(-\partial_x(fp_t)\) and random spreading contributes the second-derivative term. Together they give Fokker–Planck:

\[\boxed{\partial_t p_t=-\partial_x(fp_t)+\tfrac12g(t)^2\partial_x^2p_t.}\]

The next subsection justifies adding these effects. In \(d\) dimensions the independent noise coordinates contribute separate second derivatives, so

\[\boxed{\partial_t p_t=-\nabla\cdot(fp_t)+\tfrac12g(t)^2\Delta p_t.}\]

These formulas use scalar, spatially constant \(g(t)\). A state-dependent noise matrix requires a different diffusion term.

Optional derivation: combining drift and noise rigorously enough for calculus

Take a smooth function \(\varphi\) that vanishes outside a bounded interval. Think of it as a smooth probe that measures probability near a chosen region. Tutorial 1’s expected Taylor step says

\[\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E[\varphi(X_t)] =\mathbb E[f\varphi'+\tfrac12g^2\varphi''].\]

Write averages as integrals against \(p_t\). Integration by parts once on the drift term and twice on the noise term gives

\[\int\varphi\,\partial_t p_t\,dx =\int\varphi[-\partial_x(fp_t)+\tfrac12g^2\partial_x^2p_t],dx.\]

Boundary terms vanish because the probe is zero outside its bounded support. If two continuous expressions have equal integrals against every such probe, they agree pointwise: otherwise a probe around a region where they differ would distinguish them. This proves the displayed Fokker–Planck equation under the assumed differentiability. The same reasoning in each coordinate proves its multidimensional form.

Construct the probability-flow velocity

Derivation: turn diffusive flux into density times velocity

Where \(p_t>0\), the score satisfies \(\nabla p_t=p_t\nabla\log p_t=p_t s_t\). Consequently,

\[\begin{aligned} \partial_t p_t &=-\nabla\cdot(fp_t)+\tfrac12g^2\nabla\cdot(p_t s_t)\\ &=-\nabla\cdot\left[p_t\left(f-\tfrac12g^2s_t\right)\right]. \end{aligned}\]

Compare this with the continuity equation. A deterministic velocity producing the same density evolution is

\[\boxed{v(x,t)=f(x,t)-\tfrac12g(t)^2s_t(x).}\]

The score appears because diffusive probability flux involves \(\nabla p_t\), and writing flux as density times velocity divides that gradient by \(p_t\). This yields \(\nabla\log p_t\).

With the same initial law, and regularity and uniqueness of the density evolution, the SDE and this ODE have the same one-time marginals. This is the probability-flow construction of Song et al.. It does not identify their trajectories or their start–endpoint pairings.

The construction supplies a velocity, not necessarily the only one. Adding \(w\) with \(\nabla\cdot(p_t w)=0\) leaves the density equation unchanged.

Worked check: the stationary Gaussian does not collapse

For VP, \(v(x,t)=-\beta(t)[x+s_t(x)]/2\). If \(p_0=\mathcal N(0,I)\), Tutorial 1’s Gaussian kernel shows that \(p_t\) remains \(\mathcal N(0,I)\). Thus \(s_t(x)=-x\) and

\[v(x,t)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)x+\tfrac12\beta(t)x=0.\]

Every probability-flow particle stays still. SDE particles continue moving, but their population remains standard Gaussian. Following the score alone would contract this population; the complete probability-flow field does not.

Generate by integrating backward

What backward integration means

Forward diffusion time runs from data at \(t=0\) to noise at \(t=T\). We know how to sample a terminal state, so specify \(x(T)=z\) and solve the same ODE in decreasing \(t\). With a positive step magnitude \(h\),

\[x(t-h)\approx x(t)-h\,v(x(t),t).\]

The minus sign comes from the time increment \(-h\). Alternatively, use an increasing generation clock \(\tau=T-t\) and \(Y_\tau=X_{T-\tau}\):

\[\frac{dY_\tau}{d\tau}=-v(Y_\tau,T-\tau).\]

Neither description requires undoing individual forward Brownian increments.

A usable ODE sampling recipe

Take a descending grid \(T=t_N>\cdots>t_0=t_{\min}>0\).

  1. Draw \(x_N\) from \(p_T\), or use its Gaussian approximation in practice.
  2. For \(k=N,\ldots,1\), let \(h_k=t_k-t_{k-1}>0\).
  3. Evaluate \(v_\theta(x_k,t_k)=f(x_k,t_k)-g(t_k)^2s_\theta(x_k,t_k)/2\).
  4. Apply Euler: \(x_{k-1}=x_k-h_kv_\theta(x_k,t_k)\).

For VP the last update becomes \(x_{k-1}=x_k+\beta(t_k)h_k[x_k+s_\theta(x_k,t_k)]/2\). Exact integration with the exact score and terminal law returns \(p_{t_{\min}}\). Where the flow remains regular to zero, the exact map can reach \(p_0\). For discrete data the endpoint is singular; keep the positive cutoff and interpret the clean law as a limit. Applying a final denoiser is an additional approximation, not an identity equating a posterior mean with a transport endpoint.

Score estimation, a Gaussian substitute for \(p_T\), finite solver steps, and a positive cutoff introduce different approximation errors. Making the solver more accurate does not remove the other three.

Work through discrete and continuous targets

Two-point data: reuse the posterior, construct the motion

Let \(P(X_0=+1)=P(X_0=-1)=1/2\). This is a distribution with two atoms, not an ordinary density at \(t=0\). At positive noise,

\[p_t(x)=\tfrac12\mathcal N(x;-\alpha_t,\sigma_t^2) +\tfrac12\mathcal N(x;\alpha_t,\sigma_t^2).\]

Tutorial 1’s two-point calculation and marginal-score identity give

\[D_t(x)=\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x]=\tanh(\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2), \qquad s_t(x)=\frac{\alpha_tD_t(x)-x}{\sigma_t^2}.\]

Substitute into the VP velocity:

\[v(x,t)=-\frac{\beta(t)}2 \left[x+\frac{\alpha_t\tanh(\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2)-x}{\sigma_t^2}\right].\]

Positive-time trajectories preserve their order and cannot cross zero under uniqueness, since zero is itself an equilibrium trajectory. In the limit \(t\downarrow0\), the exact flow takes positive terminal points to \(+1\) and negative points to \(-1\). The endpoint is therefore \(\operatorname{sign}(X_T)\) almost surely under a continuous terminal law. The zero trajectory stays zero, a probability-zero exception. The quantile argument below explains this limit.

Starting with a symmetric Gaussian also gives equal endpoint masses in this special example. That symmetry does not make its intermediate marginals exact, and does not justify replacing \(p_T\) arbitrarily for other data distributions. The sign map is a singular limit, not a finite-time diffeomorphism.

Derivation: one-dimensional flow preserves probability rank

Let \(F_t(x)=P(X_t\le x)\) be the CDF. A one-dimensional ODE with unique solutions preserves order: trajectories cannot cross at the same time. Thus its map between two smooth positive-density marginals is increasing. For such a map,

\[F_t(\Psi_{T\to t}(x))=F_T(x),\qquad \boxed{\Psi_{T\to t}(x)=F_t^{-1}(F_T(x)).}\]

The first equality says that all particles below \(x\) remain below its image; their total probability is unchanged. This derives the map without solving the ODE explicitly. In the symmetric two-point limit, ranks below one half approach \(-1\) and ranks above one half approach \(+1\).

A continuous two-Gaussian target and the terminal-prior approximation

Let

\[p_0=\tfrac12\mathcal N(-1,\varrho^2)+\tfrac12\mathcal N(1,\varrho^2), \qquad\varrho>0.\]

Now the endpoint density is smooth and the exact probability-flow map is \(F_0^{-1}\circ F_T\). The actual finite-time terminal density is

\[p_T=\tfrac12\mathcal N(-\alpha_T,\alpha_T^2\varrho^2+\sigma_T^2) +\tfrac12\mathcal N(\alpha_T,\alpha_T^2\varrho^2+\sigma_T^2).\]

It is generally only close to standard normal. Let \(\Phi\) denote the standard normal CDF and \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\). The ideal direct Gaussian-to-data map is \(F_0^{-1}(\Phi(Z))\). Feeding \(Z\) into the finite-time probability-flow map instead gives \(F_0^{-1}(F_T(Z))\), which need not have density \(p_0\). This makes the terminal-prior approximation visible in a single formula.

A posterior denoiser is not a transport endpoint

Tweedie’s estimate \(D_t(x)=(x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x))/\alpha_t\) averages possible clean origins under the stochastic corruption model. The ODE map follows one particle through scores at successive times. They solve different problems. For two-point data, a small positive observation can have posterior mean close to zero, whereas its limiting ODE endpoint is \(+1\).

Reading Song’s forward and reverse SDEs

Song’s blog, “Reversing the SDE for sample generation” writes the reverse SDE in the original clock with negative \(dt\). Our increasing-clock notation describes the same reverse process. Keep the three equations distinct:

Dynamics Equation Time direction
Forward corruption SDE \(dX_t=f(X_t,t)dt+g(t)dW_t\) \(t:0\to T\)
Reverse SDE, original clock \(dX_t=[f(X_t,t)-g(t)^2s_t(X_t)]dt+g(t)d\overline W_t\) \(t:T\to0\)
Probability-flow ODE \(dx_t=[f(x_t,t)-\tfrac12g(t)^2s_t(x_t)]dt\) Either direction

Here \(\overline W\) denotes Brownian motion in reverse time, not the original forward noise replayed. For a descending step \(dt=-h\), its increment has covariance \(hI=|dt|I\). Never take \(\sqrt{dt}\) for negative \(dt\). With \(Y_\tau=X_{T-\tau}\), the reverse SDE becomes

\[dY_\tau=[-f(Y_\tau,t)+g(t)^2s_t(Y_\tau)]d\tau +g(t)d\overline W_\tau,\qquad t=T-\tau.\]

Thus Euler–Maruyama gives \(x_{\mathrm{next}}=x+h[-f(x,t)+g(t)^2s_\theta(x,t)]+g(t)\sqrt h\,\xi\), then \(t\leftarrow t-h\). This is the stochastic update used in the next subsection and in Tutorial 4. The ODE uses half the score coefficient and no fresh noise; equal marginals do not imply identical trajectories.

Optional comparison: the stochastic sampler

The generation-clock probability-flow velocity is \(-v\). Suppose we want to add fresh noise while keeping the same density evolution. A noise term with amplitude \(g\) adds \(g^2\Delta q/2\) to Fokker–Planck, where \(q_\tau=p_{T-\tau}\). Compensate with additional drift \(g^2s/2\):

\[b=-v+\tfrac12g^2s=-f+g^2s.\]

Derivation: check the marginals and the factor of two

All forward quantities below are evaluated at \(t=T-\tau\) and the current location. Since \(\nabla q=q s\),

\[\begin{aligned} -\nabla\cdot(qb)+\tfrac12g^2\Delta q &=\nabla\cdot(qv)-\tfrac12g^2\nabla\cdot(qs)+\tfrac12g^2\Delta q\\ &=\nabla\cdot(qv)=\partial_\tau q. \end{aligned}\]

Thus the SDE \(dY_\tau=b\,d\tau+g(T-\tau)d\overline W_\tau\) has the desired reverse-time marginals under the same regularity assumptions. Identifying it with the reversed stochastic path law requires a time-reversal theorem; matching marginals alone does not prove that theorem.

Euler–Maruyama in increasing \(\tau\) uses a positive step \(h\):

\[y_{j+1}=y_j+h[-f(y_j,t)+g(t)^2s_\theta(y_j,t)] +g(t)\sqrt h\,\xi_j.\]

The reverse SDE’s score coefficient is one, while the probability-flow ODE’s is one half. Deleting the noise from the reverse SDE retains the extra drift but removes the spreading it compensates for, generally changing the marginals.

Gaussian check: for stationary VP, \(s=-x\). Deleting reverse-SDE noise gives \(dY/d\tau=-\beta Y/2\), which incorrectly contracts the cloud. The correct probability-flow velocity is zero.

Check your understanding

  1. Why does a density obey a PDE even when every particle follows an ODE?
  2. Where does the one half in the probability-flow formula come from?
  3. If an exact sampler starts from a Gaussian approximation to \(p_T\), does an exact solver guarantee the correct data distribution?
  4. What distribution does an exact sampler return when stopped at \(t_{\min}>0\)?
TipAnswers
  1. Density depends on both location and time; flux determines its local change.
  2. The quadratic term of the Gaussian-step Taylor expansion.
  3. No. An exact solver still transports the substituted initial distribution.
  4. \(p_{t_{\min}}\), when both the score and terminal law are exact.
