07 · Neural ODEs and normalizing flows

NoteDraft — Connecting the model classes

Parts 01–06 constructed a sampling velocity from a learned score. Here we place that construction within Neural ODEs and continuous normalizing flows. We keep data at \(t=0\) and the prior at \(t=1\); generation integrates backward. Start in one dimension, then replace derivatives by Jacobians and divergences for vectors.

1. A network can parameterize the velocity directly

A Neural ODE uses a neural network to specify an ordinary differential equation:

\[\frac{dx}{dt}=v_\theta(x,t).\]

An initial state and an ODE solver determine its trajectory. For a forward Euler step,

\[x_{k+1}=x_k+h\,v_\theta(x_k,t_k).\]

This resembles a residual-network layer. With a suitable family of increasingly small steps, the discrete updates approximate continuous dynamics. An arbitrary residual layer need not be invertible; the exact ODE flow has stronger properties when its solutions exist and are unique.

A Neural ODE is a way to represent a transformation, not a particular training objective. It can model supervised predictions, physical trajectories, or generative transport. The original paper also introduced its use for continuous normalizing flows. Chen et al., Neural Ordinary Differential Equations

2. From trajectories to an invertible map

Write the flow as \(\Phi_{t\leftarrow s}(x_s)=x_t\). The two time indices matter because the field can depend on time. Under existence and uniqueness in both directions over the interval,

\[\Phi_{s\leftarrow t}=\Phi_{t\leftarrow s}^{-1},\qquad \Phi_{t\leftarrow r}\circ\Phi_{r\leftarrow s}=\Phi_{t\leftarrow s}.\]

For a continuously differentiable field with suitable bounds ensuring solutions remain defined, these maps are diffeomorphisms. Two trajectories at the same position at the same time coincide throughout their common interval of existence. Distinct trajectories cannot meet at a common time. Paths may visit the same location at different times.

Consider \(v(x,t)=-x\). Then

\[\Phi_{t\leftarrow s}(x)=e^{-(t-s)}x.\]

It contracts distances forward and expands them backward. It is invertible for every finite elapsed time; collapse to a point occurs only in the infinite-time limit. More generally, exact mapping from a full-dimensional smooth prior onto discrete data or a lower-dimensional support can require a singular limiting endpoint. The positive-noise cutoff used earlier avoids asserting regular invertibility at such an endpoint.

3. Normalizing flows track density as well as position

A discrete normalizing flow applies an invertible map \(x=F(z)\) to a tractable random variable. In one dimension,

\[\log p_X(F(z))=\log p_Z(z)-\log|F'(z)|.\]

The derivative measures local stretching: an interval that grows spreads its probability over a greater length. In \(d\) dimensions, the corresponding factor is the absolute Jacobian determinant:

\[\log p_X(F(z))=\log p_Z(z)-\log|\det J_F(z)|.\]

A continuous normalizing flow (CNF) uses an ODE flow for this map. To connect the discrete formula to continuous time, consider one small step \(F_h(x)=x+h v(x,t)\). In one dimension,

\[F_h'(x)=1+h\partial_xv(x,t),\qquad \log|F_h'(x)|=h\partial_xv(x,t)+O(h^2).\]

The change-of-variables correction per unit time therefore tends to \(-\partial_xv\). For vectors, the same expansion is

\[\log|\det(I+hJ_{\mathbf v})| =h\operatorname{tr}J_{\mathbf v}+O(h^2).\]

These expansions hold locally for sufficiently small steps and bounded derivatives. They explain how the discrete log determinant becomes a continuous divergence. Along the exact trajectory,

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(x_t)=-\partial_xv_\theta(x_t,t).}\]

In vector notation,

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(\mathbf x_t) =-\nabla\cdot\mathbf v_\theta(\mathbf x_t,t) =-\operatorname{tr}J_{\mathbf v_\theta}(\mathbf x_t,t).}\]

This is the instantaneous change-of-variables formula. Its short derivation from the continuity equation is in Appendix A. Divergence is a scalar measuring the rate of local volume expansion, not the magnitude of the velocity. Chen et al., Theorem 1

Check with a contraction

For \(v(x)=-x\), \(\partial_xv=-1\), so along each trajectory,

\[\log p_t(x_t)=\log p_0(x_0)+t.\]

Indeed, \(x_t=e^{-t}x_0\) gives \(p_t(x)=e^tp_0(e^tx)\). Density grows while occupied lengths shrink; total probability remains one.

4. Generation and likelihood use the same flow

Let the chosen terminal prior be \(p_1=\mathcal N(0,1)\). To generate, draw \(X_1\sim p_1\) and integrate \(v_\theta\) from 1 to 0. This defines a model distribution at time zero.

To evaluate that model’s density at an observed \(x_0\), integrate forward to \(x_1\) and accumulate divergence:

\[\boxed{\log p_0^{\mathrm{model}}(x_0) =\log p_1(x_1)+\int_0^1\partial_xv_\theta(x_t,t)\,dt.}\]

In higher dimensions, use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf v_\theta\). The sign follows by integrating the preceding log-density ODE. This formula is exact for the exact regular flow; numerical integration and approximate trace estimation introduce computational error.

For large vector states, one may estimate the divergence using a random probe \(\xi\) with \(\mathbb E[\xi\xi^\top]=I\):

\[\operatorname{tr}J_{\mathbf v}=\mathbb E_\xi[\xi^\top J_{\mathbf v}\xi].\]

A Jacobian-vector or vector-Jacobian product computes a probe value without forming the full Jacobian. A finite number of probes introduces estimation variability; in one dimension the derivative can usually be computed directly. This density calculation is not needed merely to draw ODE samples.

If the sampler stops at \(t_{\min}>0\), replace the lower integration limit by \(t_{\min}\): the resulting likelihood is for the positive-time model density, not the clean endpoint.

One way to train a CNF is maximum likelihood using this formula. It generally involves solving the ODE during training and differentiating the loss through its solution. Adjoint methods are one option; differentiating through solver steps is another. A Neural ODE does not require one specific gradient method. Flow matching, introduced in Part 08, trains the velocity by regression instead.

5. Our probability-flow ODE is a particular CNF

For the forward SDE

\[dX_t=f(X_t,t)\,dt+g(t)\,dW_t,\]

the exact marginal score defines the probability-flow velocity

\[v(x,t)=f(x,t)-\tfrac12g(t)^2s_t(x).\]

For VP diffusion this becomes

\[v_\theta(x,t)=-\tfrac12\beta(t)[x+s_\theta(x,t)].\]

Once a neural network estimates the score, this is a neural parameterization of an ODE field. Under the same regularity conditions it defines a CNF. The velocity is learned indirectly through the score and the known forward schedule. Song et al. use this connection for deterministic sampling and likelihood computation. Song et al., Section 4.3

With the exact score and matching initial law, the SDE and ODE have the same marginal densities. Their particle trajectories and joint couplings differ. With an approximate score, the learned ODE still defines its own transport model, but its marginals need not equal the prescribed forward densities. Its CNF likelihood refers to that induced model law.

Construction What is parameterized? How can it be trained?
Neural ODE A velocity determining continuous state evolution Depends on the task
Likelihood-trained CNF A velocity and its induced density transformation Change-of-variables likelihood
Score-based probability-flow ODE Score; velocity follows from the forward process Denoising/score regression
Flow-matching CNF Velocity directly Conditional-velocity regression

These are overlapping constructions, not mutually exclusive model families. A probability-flow ODE and a flow-matching model can both be Neural ODEs and CNFs.

Appendix A. Density change along a trajectory

The continuity equation is

\[\partial_tp_t=-\partial_x(p_tv) =-v\partial_xp_t-p_t\partial_xv.\]

Along \(\dot x_t=v(x_t,t)\), the chain rule gives

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\log p_t(x_t) &=\frac{\partial_tp_t(x_t)}{p_t(x_t)} +v(x_t,t)\frac{\partial_xp_t(x_t)}{p_t(x_t)}\\ &=\left.\frac{-v\partial_xp_t-p_t\partial_xv+v\partial_xp_t}{p_t}\right|_{x=x_t}\\ &=-\partial_xv(x_t,t). \end{aligned}\]

In \(d\) dimensions, expand \(\nabla\cdot(p_t\mathbf v)=\mathbf v\cdot\nabla p_t+p_t\nabla\cdot\mathbf v\). The same cancellation yields the divergence formula.
