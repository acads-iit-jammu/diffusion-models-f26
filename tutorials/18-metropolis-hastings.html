08 · Metropolis–Hastings: Sampling What You Cannot Normalise

Note 06 ended with a promise and half an algorithm: generation needs samples, not a density, and a Markov chain can produce them from a target known only up to its normalising constant. This note makes that precise. One condition — detailed balance — and one acceptance rule turn any proposal you can sample into a chain whose stationary distribution is exactly the one you wanted. Then the honest accounting: what it costs, where it breaks, and the single structural reason it cannot generate an image no matter how long you run it. That reason is the door into note 09.

1. The problem, stated exactly

Everything this course does is downstream of one request: produce a draw from a distribution. Note 00 argued that generation is sampling; note 06 showed that estimating the density first is hopeless in high dimension. So consider the situation where you are luckier than the image case — you can write down the target, but only up to a constant:

\[ \pi(x) \;=\; \frac{\tilde\pi(x)}{Z}, \qquad Z = \int \tilde\pi(x)\,dx , \]

with \(\tilde\pi\) cheap to evaluate at any point and \(Z\) an integral nobody can do.

This is not a contrived setup; it is the generic one.

Bayesian inference. The posterior over parameters given data is

\[ \pi(\theta) \;=\; p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) \;=\; \frac{p(\theta)\,L(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)}{p(\mathcal{D})}, \qquad p(\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\theta)L(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)\,d\theta . \]

The numerator is prior times likelihood — two things you chose and can evaluate in closed form. The denominator, the evidence, is an integral over the whole parameter space, and outside conjugate families it has no closed form and no usable quadrature past a handful of dimensions. So \(\tilde\pi = p(\theta)L\) is available and \(Z = p(\mathcal{D})\) is not.

Energy-based models. \(\pi(x) \propto e^{-E(x)}\) for an energy \(E\) you specify or learn. \(Z = \int e^{-E}\) is the partition function, and computing it is as hard as the original problem.

Conditioning of any kind. \(\pi(x) \propto p(x)\mathbf{1}\{x \in A\}\) — a known distribution restricted to a region. The restricted density is trivial to evaluate and its mass \(p(A)\) is not.

What note 06 already settled

Two facts carry over and are not re-derived here.

Rejection sampling is exact and unusable. It needs an envelope \(\pi \le M q\), and in \(d\) dimensions with a proposal only ten percent too wide the acceptance rate is \(1.1^{-d}\) — about \(7\times10^{-5}\) at \(d=100\). Every independently drawn candidate is a fresh gamble against the same odds.

The cure is to stop starting over. Move from where you already are, so that work already done is not thrown away. That is what a Markov chain buys, and what this note builds.

2. Chains, kernels, and what “converges” means

A Markov chain on a state space \(\mathcal{X}\) is a sequence \(x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots\) in which the next state depends on the past only through the present:

\[ \mathbb{P}(x_{t+1} \in A \mid x_t, x_{t-1}, \dots, x_0) \;=\; \mathbb{P}(x_{t+1} \in A \mid x_t) \;=\; \int_A K(x_t, y)\,dy . \]

The function \(K(x,y)\) is the transition kernel: the density of where you go next, given where you are. It is all the chain is.

Run the kernel forward from an initial law \(p_0\) and you get a sequence of distributions, not one distribution:

\[ p_{t+1}(y) \;=\; \int p_t(x)\,K(x,y)\,dx . \]

This is the same \(p_0, p_1, p_2, \dots\) that note 06 exhibited in closed form for Gibbs on a bivariate normal, where \(x_t \mid x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\rho^{2t}x_0,\, 1-\rho^{4t})\). Watching that sequence flow is the whole subject.

A distribution \(\pi\) is stationary (or invariant) for \(K\) when applying the kernel changes nothing:

\[ \boxed{\;\pi(y) \;=\; \int \pi(x)\,K(x,y)\,dx.\;} \]

Stationarity says: if you are already distributed as \(\pi\), one more step leaves you distributed as \(\pi\). It says nothing on its own about getting there. Two more conditions supply that.

  • Irreducibility: from any starting point the chain can reach any region of positive \(\pi\)-mass. No part of the space is walled off.
  • Aperiodicity: the chain does not cycle through regions on a fixed schedule.

With those, the fundamental theorem of MCMC applies: the chain is ergodic, \(p_t \to \pi\) regardless of \(p_0\), and time averages converge to space averages,

\[ \frac1T \sum_{t=1}^{T} f(x_t) \;\xrightarrow[T\to\infty]{}\; \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f(X)] . \]

That last line is the working promise: run one chain long enough and average along it, and you get expectations under \(\pi\) — even though no single \(x_t\) was drawn from \(\pi\) and no two of them are independent.

So the design problem is now sharp, and it runs backwards from the usual one. Ordinarily you are given a chain and asked for its stationary law. Here you are given the stationary law and asked to build a chain. Metropolis–Hastings is a universal answer.

3. Detailed balance: a sufficient condition you can actually check

Verifying \(\pi = \pi K\) directly means doing an integral over the whole space, which is the sort of thing we are trying to avoid. There is a stronger, purely local condition that implies it. A chain satisfies detailed balance with respect to \(\pi\) if for every pair of states,

\[ \boxed{\;\pi(x)\,K(x,y) \;=\; \pi(y)\,K(y,x).\;} \]

Read it as a flow statement: at equilibrium the probability mass moving from \(x\) to \(y\) exactly equals the mass moving from \(y\) to \(x\). Every pairwise exchange balances — hence reversible: a film of the chain in equilibrium looks the same run backwards.

Detailed balance implies stationarity. One line:

\[ \int \pi(x)K(x,y)\,dx \;=\; \int \pi(y)K(y,x)\,dx \;=\; \pi(y)\int K(y,x)\,dx \;=\; \pi(y), \]

using detailed balance in the first step and \(\int K(y,x)dx = 1\) in the last. \(\square\)

The converse is false — plenty of useful chains are stationary without being reversible, and some deliberately non-reversible chains mix faster. But detailed balance is a pointwise condition on two states at a time, which means it can be engineered, and that is exactly what the next section does.

4. The algorithm

Choose a proposal \(q(y \mid x)\): any conditional distribution you can sample from. It need not resemble \(\pi\); it need not be symmetric; it carries no theoretical burden at all. From the current state \(x_t\):

  1. Propose \(y \sim q(\cdot \mid x_t)\).
  2. Compute the acceptance probability \[ \boxed{\;\alpha(x_t, y) \;=\; \min\!\left\{1,\; \frac{\pi(y)\,q(x_t \mid y)}{\pi(x_t)\,q(y \mid x_t)}\right\}.\;} \]
  3. Accept or reject: draw \(u \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\); set \(x_{t+1} = y\) if \(u < \alpha\), and otherwise \(x_{t+1} = x_t\) — stay where you are.

Three observations, in order of importance.

The normalising constant cancels. \(\pi\) enters only as the ratio \(\pi(y)/\pi(x)\), and

\[ \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)} \;=\; \frac{\tilde\pi(y)/Z}{\tilde\pi(x)/Z} \;=\; \frac{\tilde\pi(y)}{\tilde\pi(x)} . \]

\(Z\) never appears. For a Bayesian posterior the rule is literally

\[ \alpha \;=\; \min\left\{1,\; \frac{p(\theta')\,L(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta')}{p(\theta)\,L(\mathcal{D}\mid\theta)} \cdot \frac{q(\theta \mid \theta')}{q(\theta' \mid \theta)}\right\}, \]

prior times likelihood at two points, and nothing else. The evidence integral — the reason the posterior was intractable — is gone.

Rejection is not a wasted draw. In rejection sampling a rejected candidate disappears and the sampler has nothing to show for it. Here a rejection emits the current state again, which is a genuine sample. Staying put is a legal move, and it is how the chain gives extra weight to high-probability regions. Omitting the repeat — recording only accepted states — is the single most common implementation bug, and it silently biases every estimate.

The correction term \(q(x\mid y)/q(y\mid x)\) is Hastings’ contribution. It compensates for a proposal that is easier to enter a region than to leave it. Metropolis’ original 1953 algorithm assumed a symmetric proposal; Hastings generalised it in 1970 to arbitrary \(q\).

Why it works

Take \(y \ne x\). The MH kernel is \(K(x,y) = q(y\mid x)\,\alpha(x,y)\), so

\[ \pi(x)K(x,y) = \pi(x)\,q(y\mid x)\,\min\!\left\{1, \frac{\pi(y)q(x\mid y)}{\pi(x)q(y\mid x)}\right\} = \min\big\{\pi(x)q(y\mid x),\; \pi(y)q(x\mid y)\big\}, \]

pushing the positive factor \(\pi(x)q(y\mid x)\) inside the \(\min\). The right-hand side is symmetric under \(x \leftrightarrow y\) — swapping the labels swaps the two arguments of a \(\min\) and changes nothing. Therefore \(\pi(x)K(x,y) = \pi(y)K(y,x)\): detailed balance holds, and by §3 so does stationarity. For \(x = y\) the condition is trivial, and the rejection atom is exactly what makes \(K\) integrate to one. \(\square\)

That is the entire theory. The acceptance rule was reverse-engineered to make a \(\min\) appear symmetrically — this is the rare algorithm whose correctness proof is shorter than its motivation.

Where the accept/reject step goes in this course

Metropolis–Hastings needs the accept/reject correction because the proposal is picked for convenience and is therefore wrong. The step is a patch applied to a mismatch.

A diffusion model has no such step. Its reverse process is built so that each small step is already almost exactly right — the forward process adds a tiny bit of Gaussian noise, so the reverse conditional is nearly Gaussian and can be matched directly. There is nothing left to accept or reject.

Keep the contrast in view: MH corrects a bad proposal; a diffusion model learns a good one.

5. The three special cases you will meet

Random-walk Metropolis. Take \(q(y\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(y; x, \tau^2 I)\) — a symmetric jump. Then \(q(x\mid y) = q(y\mid x)\), the Hastings ratio is 1, and

\[ \alpha \;=\; \min\left\{1, \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi(x)}\right\}. \]

Always move uphill; move downhill with probability equal to the density ratio. That downhill move is not a defect — it is precisely what separates sampling from optimisation. A hill-climber ends at the mode and reports one point; a sampler must visit the tails in proportion to their mass, so it has to be willing to go down.

The independence sampler. \(q(y \mid x) = q(y)\), ignoring the current state. Then \(\alpha = \min\{1, w(y)/w(x)\}\) with importance weight \(w = \pi/q\): accept whenever the candidate has a better weight than the incumbent. This is rejection sampling’s cousin and inherits its fragility — a proposal with lighter tails than \(\pi\) makes the chain stick for astronomically long stretches.

Gibbs sampling is MH with \(\alpha \equiv 1\). Propose by redrawing one coordinate from its exact conditional, \(q(y_i \mid x) = \pi(y_i \mid x_{-i})\), leaving the others fixed. Substituting into the acceptance ratio and using \(\pi(x) = \pi(x_i \mid x_{-i})\pi(x_{-i})\), everything cancels and \(\alpha = 1\). Gibbs never rejects — the price is that you must be able to sample each conditional exactly, which is a strong requirement.

MALA: the score walks in

Nothing forces the proposal to be blind. Metropolis-adjusted Langevin proposes

\[ y \;=\; x + \tfrac{\tau^2}{2}\,\nabla_x \log \pi(x) + \tau\,z, \qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I), \]

then applies the usual accept/reject step (the proposal is now asymmetric, so the Hastings ratio matters). The drift term pushes candidates toward high density before they are judged, so acceptance stays high with much larger steps.

And notice what it needs: only \(\nabla_x \log \pi\), the score. The normalising constant differentiates away, exactly as in note 06. Drop the accept/reject step and you have unadjusted Langevin dynamics — biased by \(O(\tau)\), but needing nothing except the score.

This is the closest classical ancestor of a score-based generative model. Module 4 will learn \(\nabla_x\log p\) from data instead of deriving it; the sampler on top is this one.

6. A worked example: two lines of arithmetic

Target: the posterior for a Gaussian mean with a Gaussian prior,

\[ \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 10^2), \qquad x_1,\dots,x_n \mid \theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta, 1), \]

so that \(\tilde\pi(\theta) = \exp\!\big(-\tfrac{\theta^2}{200}\big)\cdot \exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\sum_i (x_i-\theta)^2\big)\). Work on the log scale — always work on the log scale, or the products underflow to zero by \(n \approx 500\):

\[ \log\alpha \;=\; \min\Big\{0,\;\; \log\tilde\pi(\theta') - \log\tilde\pi(\theta)\Big\}, \]

with \(\log\tilde\pi(\theta) = -\tfrac{\theta^2}{200} - \tfrac12\sum_i(x_i-\theta)^2\). Propose \(\theta' = \theta + \tau z\), accept if \(\log u < \log\alpha\).

This example is conjugate, so the answer is known — \(\mathcal{N}\!\big(\frac{n\bar x}{n + 1/100},\, \frac{1}{n+1/100}\big)\) — which is the point: run the sampler, overlay the histogram on the exact posterior, and confirm that a procedure using only ratios of unnormalised densities reproduces a distribution whose normalising constant it never computed. Then change the prior to a Student-\(t\), destroy conjugacy, and observe that the code does not change at all. That robustness is why MH dominated applied Bayesian statistics for thirty years.

7. What it costs: burn-in, correlation, and tuning

The theorem says \(p_t \to \pi\). It does not say when, and everything difficult about MCMC lives in that gap.

Burn-in. The chain starts at \(p_0\), which is wherever you put it, and needs time before \(p_t\) resembles \(\pi\). Early samples are discarded. How many is a judgement call, informed by trace plots and by running several chains from dispersed starts and checking they agree (the Gelman–Rubin \(\hat R\) statistic, which compares between-chain to within-chain variance and should approach 1).

Autocorrelation. Consecutive states are dependent by construction. \(T\) MCMC draws are worth fewer than \(T\) independent ones — the effective sample size is

\[ \mathrm{ESS} \;=\; \frac{T}{1 + 2\sum_{k\ge1}\rho_k}, \]

with \(\rho_k\) the lag-\(k\) autocorrelation. Note 06’s Gibbs example makes this concrete: lag-1 autocorrelation \(\rho^2\), so a target with correlation \(0.99\) gives \(\rho^2 \approx 0.98\) and an ESS a fiftieth of the chain length. Strongly dependent targets are expensive.

Step size is a genuine dilemma. For random-walk Metropolis with scale \(\tau\):

\(\tau\) acceptance behaviour
too small \(\to 1\) almost every move accepted, but each is tiny — the chain diffuses, needing \(\sim(L/\tau)^2\) steps to cross a region of width \(L\)
too large \(\to 0\) proposals land in the tails and are rejected; the chain sticks at one point for hundreds of iterations
tuned \(\approx 0.234\) the asymptotically optimal rate for random-walk Metropolis on product targets (Roberts, Gelman & Gilks, 1997)

A first-time user’s instinct — “high acceptance means it is working” — is exactly backwards. An acceptance rate of \(0.95\) almost always means the chain is crawling.

Multimodality is the failure MCMC hides best. A chain in one mode of a well-separated bimodal target must cross a low-density valley to reach the other, and the probability of proposing across it can be \(e^{-100}\). The chain then looks perfectly healthy — good acceptance, stable trace, tight autocorrelation — while sampling from the wrong distribution. It has converged to a mode, not to \(\pi\). Tempering, parallel chains and mode-hopping proposals exist; none is a general solution.

And the curse, once more

Even with none of the above going wrong, the number of steps needed to produce one effectively independent sample grows with dimension. For product-form targets the standard scaling results are

sampler steps per independent sample
random-walk Metropolis \(O(d)\)
MALA (Langevin, adjusted) \(O(d^{1/3})\)
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo \(O(d^{1/4})\)

Each row is a real improvement, bought with more gradient information per step — and every row still grows. Compare rejection sampling’s \(\sigma^{-d}\) and this is a triumph: polynomial instead of exponential. Compare it with the \(d \approx 10^5\) of a modest image and it is still nowhere near enough.

8. The structural reason this is not a generative model

Suppose every difficulty in §7 were solved: a perfectly tuned sampler, no multimodality, dimension-free mixing. It would still not generate an image. The obstruction is not efficiency. It is the input.

Metropolis–Hastings requires \(\tilde\pi\): a function you can evaluate at any proposed point. It answers

“I can write down the target up to a constant. Give me samples.”

Generative modelling asks the opposite question:

“I cannot write down the target at all. I have 60,000 samples from it. Give me more.”

There is no \(\tilde\pi(x)\) for natural images. Nobody can supply a function that, given a \(256\times256\times3\) array, returns a number proportional to how likely that array is to be a photograph. That function is precisely the thing note 06 proved we cannot estimate. MH is a machine for converting a density into samples, and we have no density to feed it.

Notice too that MH never learns. Run it for a million steps, then ask for a sample from a fresh start and you pay the full burn-in again. There is no trained object, nothing amortised, no reuse. A generative model, by contrast, pays a large training cost once and then produces a sample in a single forward pass.

The pivot the lecture turns on

Note 06 already named the shape of the fix. In rejection sampling, in MH, in Gibbs, a human picks the proposal for convenience and hopes it resembles the target — and in high dimension it does not.

So stop picking it. Learn it. Learn a map \(G\) from easy noise to data, fit it using only samples, and dispense with the density entirely.

That immediately raises the question that defines the next note: if you never evaluate a density, how do you know your generator is any good? You cannot compute a likelihood, so you cannot maximise one. The answer — train a second network to tell your samples from real ones, and improve until it cannot — is the generative adversarial network, and it is the subject of note 09.

Exercises

  1. Show that detailed balance implies stationarity, and give an example of a chain that is stationary for \(\pi\) but not reversible.
  2. Verify the symmetry step \(\pi(x)q(y\mid x)\alpha(x,y) = \min\{\pi(x)q(y\mid x), \pi(y)q(x\mid y)\}\) explicitly in both cases of the \(\min\).
  3. Show that Gibbs sampling is MH with acceptance probability exactly 1.
  4. A student records only accepted proposals, discarding the repeats. Show that the resulting sample is biased, and identify which regions are over-represented.
  5. For target \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and proposal \(\mathcal{N}(x,\tau^2)\), compute the expected acceptance probability as a function of \(\tau\) and confirm it is monotonically decreasing.
  6. Implement random-walk Metropolis for the §6 posterior with \(n=50\). Plot the acceptance rate against \(\tau \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1, 10\}\) and the ESS against the same grid. Where do the two curves disagree about which \(\tau\) is best?
  7. Take \(\pi = \tfrac12\mathcal{N}(-5,1) + \tfrac12\mathcal{N}(5,1)\) with a random-walk proposal of scale \(\tau=1\). Estimate the expected number of iterations before the chain first crosses between modes. Now run for 10,000 steps from \(x_0 = -5\) and report the estimated mean of \(\pi\).
  8. Write the MALA proposal for \(\pi = \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\) explicitly and confirm the Hastings ratio is not 1. What goes wrong if you forget it?
  9. Show that the independence sampler’s acceptance ratio is a ratio of importance weights, and explain why \(q\) must have heavier tails than \(\pi\).
  10. For the Gibbs chain of note 06 with \(\rho = 0.9\) and \(\rho = 0.99\), compute the ESS of a chain of length \(10^4\). How much longer must the second chain run to match the first?
  11. An energy-based model has \(\pi(x) \propto e^{-E(x)}\). Write the Metropolis acceptance rule in terms of \(\Delta E\), and interpret it physically.
  12. Explain, in three sentences and without using the word “slow”, why a perfectly mixing MH sampler still cannot generate photographs.
