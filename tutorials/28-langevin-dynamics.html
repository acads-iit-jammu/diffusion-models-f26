Langevin Dynamics: Keeping a Density Still, Moving a Density Backward

NoteTutorial 4 of 4 · Langevin sampling

Question: How does the same score support both equilibrium sampling and reverse diffusion, and why are these different dynamics?

Before this: Tutorial 1 introduces Gaussian increments and Euler–Maruyama; Tutorial 2 derives Fokker–Planck and reverse-time sampling. No additional SDE background is assumed. Tutorial 3 provides the optional transport perspective.

Core route: a numerical sampler → Gaussian example → stationary density → comparison with reverse diffusion → annealing and predictor–corrector sampling. Longer calculations sit in derivation subsections; the final density-operator view is optional.

Start with a fixed target and a sampling update

Suppose we want samples from one fixed density \(p(x)\) and know its score \(s(x)=\nabla_x\log p(x)\). Use a positive step size \(\eta\) and independent \(\xi_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\):

\[\boxed{x_{k+1}=x_k+\eta s(x_k)+\sqrt{2\eta}\,\xi_k.}\]

The score term draws the particle toward higher density. The random term spreads the cloud and allows exploration. Their relative sizes matter: adding arbitrary noise to gradient ascent does not automatically sample \(p\). The initial point can come from an easy distribution; there is no promise that the first few iterates already have the target law.

This update is Euler–Maruyama for overdamped Langevin dynamics:

\[\boxed{dZ_u=s(Z_u)\,du+\sqrt2\,dB_u.}\]

“Overdamped” means we model position directly, without a separate momentum variable. \(B_u\) is standard Brownian motion, with independent increments of covariance \(hI\) over a time interval of length \(h\).

Keep three clocks distinct

Symbol Meaning
\(u\) Algorithmic Langevin time; the target \(p\) is fixed
\(t\) Forward diffusion time; the noisy density \(p_t\) changes
\(\tau=T-t\) Increasing reverse-diffusion clock
\(q_u\) Current Langevin sampler density, which need not yet equal \(p\)

We keep \(\tau\) reserved for reverse diffusion, as in the earlier tutorials. Langevin runs longer by increasing \(u\); this does not change the target’s noise level. At a fixed diffusion level \(t\), one can run a separate Langevin process in \(u\) targeting the frozen density \(p_t\).

Energy interpretation: the normalizing constant disappears

Suppose \(p(x)=e^{-E(x)}/C\), where \(C=\int e^{-E(x)}dx\) is finite. Then

\[s(x)=-\nabla E(x),\qquad x_{k+1}=x_k-\eta\nabla E(x_k)+\sqrt{2\eta}\,\xi_k.\]

The deterministic part resembles gradient descent on energy, but the aim is to sample a distribution of states, rather than return an energy minimizer. We do not need \(C\) because its logarithm has zero spatial derivative. For \(p(x)\propto e^{-E(x)/\mathcal T}\) at temperature \(\mathcal T>0\), \(dZ_u=-\nabla E(Z_u)du+\sqrt{2\mathcal T}\,dB_u\) has the corresponding balance. We use \(\mathcal T=1\) below.

First solved problem: a Gaussian target

Let \(p=\mathcal N(\mu,a^2)\) in one dimension, with \(a>0\). Its score is \(s(x)=-(x-\mu)/a^2\), so

\[dZ_u=-\frac{Z_u-\mu}{a^2}\,du+\sqrt2\,dB_u.\]

If we deleted the noise, the ODE would give \(z_u=\mu+e^{-u/a^2}(z_0-\mu)\), contracting every point toward the mean. Langevin keeps adding variance while the drift removes it.

Derivation: the mean and variance approach the target

The expected Taylor formula from Tutorial 1 is \(d\mathbb E[\varphi(Z_u)]/du=\mathbb E[s\varphi'+\varphi'']\). Setting \(\varphi(z)=z\) gives, with \(m_u=\mathbb E[Z_u]\),

\[\dot m_u=-\frac{m_u-\mu}{a^2},\qquad m_u=\mu+e^{-u/a^2}(m_0-\mu).\]

Apply the same formula to \((z-\mu)^2\), then subtract \((m_u-\mu)^2\) to obtain

\[\dot V_u=-\frac{2}{a^2}V_u+2,\qquad \boxed{V_u=a^2+e^{-2u/a^2}(V_0-a^2).}\]

This calculation assumes finite second moments. It shows how variance approaches \(a^2\); moment convergence alone would not prove convergence of the full law. Here the linear Gaussian structure gives more: conditional on \(Z_0=z_0\),

\[\boxed{Z_u\mid Z_0=z_0\sim \mathcal N\!\left(\mu+e^{-u/a^2}(z_0-\mu), a^2(1-e^{-2u/a^2})\right).}\]

As in Tutorial 1, repeated linear updates are weighted sums of independent Gaussians; their small-step limit explains this conditional Gaussian law. Equivalently, at one time we may write

\[Z_u=\mu+e^{-u/a^2}(Z_0-\mu) +a\sqrt{1-e^{-2u/a^2}}\,\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1),\quad\epsilon\perp Z_0.\]

The initial contribution vanishes and the noise contribution approaches \(\mathcal N(0,a^2)\). This establishes convergence in distribution to the Gaussian target from any initial probability law; this last distributional argument does not require the finite moments used in the earlier moment calculation. If \(Z_0\sim p\) already, the law stays \(p\), although individual particles move.

Example: take \(\mu=2\), \(a=1\), and \(Z_0=5\). At \(u=1\), the sampler has mean \(2+3e^{-1}\approx3.104\) and variance \(1-e^{-2}\approx0.865\). At long times it approaches \(\mathcal N(2,1)\), not a point mass at 2.

Why the target is stationary

Derivation: cancellation in Fokker–Planck

Tutorial 2 shows that drift \(b\) and scalar noise amplitude \(g\) give the density change \(-\nabla\cdot(bq)+g^2\Delta q/2\). For Langevin, \(b=s\) and \(g=\sqrt2\):

\[\partial_u q_u=-\nabla\cdot(q_u\nabla\log p)+\Delta q_u.\]

Substitute \(q_u=p\). Since \(p\nabla\log p=\nabla p\),

\[-\nabla\cdot(p\nabla\log p)+\Delta p =-\Delta p+\Delta p=0.\]

Thus \(p\) is stationary, or invariant: starting at \(p\) keeps the same density. We assume a smooth positive target, a well-defined nonexplosive process, and boundary behavior that conserves probability. The calculation is about the continuous-time SDE, not automatically about a finite-step algorithm.

Stationarity is not a convergence proof

Stationarity answers “does the process preserve \(p\)?” Convergence asks “does it approach \(p\) from another initial law?” Convergence needs further conditions ensuring exploration and return from the tails. For instance, a sufficiently regular strongly convex confining energy gives a useful favorable case. We proved convergence explicitly for the Gaussian rather than assume it from stationarity. With multiple separated modes, a sampler can take a long time to move between them: this is slow mixing. Imagine a cloud concentrated around one peak of a two-mode density: local steps may explore that peak well long before any particle crosses the low-density region to the other peak.

A chain’s successive outputs are also correlated. Many steps do not necessarily mean many nearly independent samples, especially when mixing is slow.

The numerical sampler introduces its own error

The Euler–Maruyama update is often called the unadjusted Langevin algorithm (ULA). “Unadjusted” means that no accept/reject correction is added. Even with the exact score, ULA’s stationary law, when it exists, generally differs from the target at a fixed positive step.

Derivation: finite-step bias for the Gaussian

For our Gaussian, write \(\gamma=1-\eta/a^2\). Then

\[x_{k+1}-\mu=\gamma(x_k-\mu)+\sqrt{2\eta}\,\xi_k.\]

Independence gives the variance recursion \(V_{k+1}=\gamma^2 V_k+2\eta\). A stationary variance exists when \(|\gamma|<1\), or \(0<\eta<2a^2\). Solving \(V_\eta=\gamma^2V_\eta+2\eta\) gives

\[\boxed{V_\eta=\frac{2\eta}{1-(1-\eta/a^2)^2} =\frac{a^2}{1-\eta/(2a^2)}.}\]

It is too large. For a standard normal target and \(\eta=0.2\), the stationary variance is \(1/0.9\approx1.111\), not 1. Running longer removes initialization error but not this discretization bias. At \(\eta=2a^2\) no finite stationary variance exists, and larger steps are unstable for this example.

An exact Gaussian step for comparison: for any \(\eta>0\),

\[x_{k+1}=\mu+e^{-\eta/a^2}(x_k-\mu) +a\sqrt{1-e^{-2\eta/a^2}}\,\xi_k\]

preserves the correct Gaussian. Its contraction and noise variance come from the exact conditional law above. Non-Gaussian targets usually do not supply such a closed-form transition. Step refinement reduces numerical error under appropriate conditions, but general ULA stability needs more than a valid score.

Compare Langevin with reverse diffusion

The forward/reverse SDE pair and the translation from Song’s negative-\(dt\) notation are displayed explicitly in Tutorial 2.

Use Tutorial 2’s reverse-SDE check for this comparison. If you skipped it, the density calculation below also verifies the marginal evolution. Write all reverse-diffusion quantities at \(t=T-\tau\):

\[\text{reverse SDE:}\quad dY_\tau=[-f(Y_\tau,t)+g(t)^2s_t(Y_\tau)]d\tau +g(t)d\overline W_\tau,\]

\[\text{probability-flow ODE:}\quad \frac{dY_\tau}{d\tau}=-f(Y_\tau,t)+\tfrac12g(t)^2s_t(Y_\tau) =-v(Y_\tau,t).\]

The reverse SDE evolves through a prescribed sequence \(p_{T-\tau}\) when initialized at the exact \(p_T\) and supplied the exact score. It does not wait for equilibrium at each time. Ordinary Langevin keeps one target fixed and approaches its equilibrium over algorithmic time \(u\).

Derivation: time scaling cannot repair a coefficient mismatch

Freeze a target \(p\) and consider constants \(A,D>0\):

\[dZ_u=A\,s(Z_u)du+\sqrt{2D}\,dB_u.\]

A density with zero probability flux satisfies \(Aq\nabla\log p-D\nabla q=0\), hence

\[\nabla\log q=\frac{A}{D}\nabla\log p, \qquad q(x)\propto p(x)^{A/D}.\]

When this expression is normalizable and the dynamics are well defined, it provides a stationary density. To target \(p\) itself, choose \(A=D\). Changing to \(w=cu\), where \(c>0\), divides both \(A\) and \(D\) by \(c\). To see why, over a \(w\)-interval of length \(h\), the old clock advances by \(h/c\). The drift increment is \(A s\,h/c\). The noise increment has covariance \(2D(h/c)I\), the same as noise amplitude \(\sqrt{2D/c}\) over an interval of length \(h\) in the new clock. Thus

\[A\longmapsto A/c,\qquad D\longmapsto D/c,\]

while the noise amplitude scales by \(1/\sqrt c\), not \(1/c\). The ratio \(A/D\) is unchanged. This explains the equivalent conventions

\[dZ=s(Z)du+\sqrt2\,dB_u, \qquad dZ=\tfrac12s(Z)dw+d\widetilde B_w,\quad w=2u,\]

where \(\widetilde B\) is a standard Brownian motion in the new clock.

A factor of two in the score alone cannot be absorbed by changing the clock.

Drift-free diffusion: similar ingredients, different balance

For \(dX_t=g(t)dW_t\), the reverse SDE is

\[dY_\tau=g(t)^2s_t(Y_\tau)d\tau+g(t)d\overline W_\tau.\]

At a frozen time with \(g(t)>0\), its constants are \(A=g^2\), \(D=g^2/2\). Their ratio is 2. If we artificially held that score and those coefficients fixed, the zero-flux stationary density would be proportional to \(p_t^2\), when normalizable, rather than \(p_t\).

For a Gaussian \(p_t=\mathcal N(\mu,V_t)\), this frozen process equilibrates to \(\mathcal N(\mu,V_t/2)\). That is a concrete way to see the mismatch. The genuine reverse process does not freeze \(t\): it changes the score and coefficients while transporting a changing density.

In particular, define a new reverse-sampling clock \(\lambda\) by \(d\lambda=g(t)^2d\tau/2\), on an interval where \(g>0\). Brownian variance scaling then gives

\[dY=2s_t(Y)\,d\lambda+\sqrt2\,d\widetilde B_\lambda,\]

with \(t\) still changing along \(\lambda\). This is a change of the reverse clock, not the fixed-target Langevin clock \(u\). The coefficient 2 remains; it is not ordinary Langevin targeting \(p_t\).

For this drift-free forward process, the added variance is \(\sigma_t^2=\int_0^t g(r)^2dr\) when initial added noise is zero. Increasing noise of this form gives the variance-exploding (VE) construction. It need not make \(p_T\) a standard normal: both its variance scale and residual data matter.

VP diffusion: the reversed drift also matters

For VP, \(f(x,t)=-\beta(t)x/2\) and \(g(t)^2=\beta(t)\), so

\[dY_\tau=\left[\tfrac12\beta(t)Y_\tau+\beta(t)s_t(Y_\tau)\right]d\tau +\sqrt{\beta(t)}d\overline W_\tau.\]

The \(+\beta Y/2\) reverses the forward contraction. Ignoring it changes the sampling process. There is a useful special case: if the data law itself is \(\mathcal N(0,I)\), VP leaves it unchanged, so \(p_t=\mathcal N(0,I)\) and \(s_t(x)=-x\) at every time. Its complete reverse drift is then \(-\beta x/2\). Together with noise \(\sqrt\beta\), this is correctly balanced Langevin for \(\mathcal N(0,I)\), with a time-dependent rate. Ordinary reverse VP for a changing, non-Gaussian \(p_t\) is not fixed-target Langevin.

The precise bridge: transport plus a Langevin contribution

Split the reverse drift algebraically:

\[\boxed{-f+g^2s_t =\underbrace{-f+\tfrac12g^2s_t}_{\text{probability-flow transport }-v} +\underbrace{\tfrac12g^2s_t}_{\text{Langevin drift}}.}\]

Pair the second term with the existing Brownian noise \(g\,d\overline W\). At a frozen noise level, drift \(g^2s_t/2\) and noise amplitude \(g\) have \(A=D=g^2/2\), the correct Langevin balance for \(p_t\).

So the reverse SDE combines transport that changes the target density with an instantaneous Langevin contribution that leaves that target unchanged. This is a decomposition of drift and density evolution, not a claim that two independent trajectories can be added together.

Optional derivation: check the decomposition on densities

At reverse time \(\tau\), let \(q\) be an arbitrary current density. Here \(s_t\) is the score of the prescribed target \(p_t\), not the score of \(q\). Its evolution under the reverse SDE can be written

\[\partial_\tau q=\nabla\cdot(qv) +\tfrac12g^2\left[\Delta q-\nabla\cdot(qs_t)\right].\]

The first term is the generation-clock ODE’s transport. The bracket is the Langevin contribution. Substituting \(q=p_t\) makes the bracket vanish because \(\nabla p_t=p_t s_t\). The remaining term produces the prescribed reverse marginal evolution. If \(q\ne p_t\), the bracket need not vanish.

This also explains the half-factor: fresh noise adds spreading, which requires an additional half-score drift beyond the deterministic transport. Removing noise while retaining that extra drift generally changes the density path.

Annealing and predictor–corrector sampling

Annealed Langevin: equilibrate approximately, then change the target

Construct smoothed data densities using additive noise, \(p_{\sigma_i}\) being the law of \(X_0+\sigma_i\epsilon\), with \(\sigma_1>\cdots>\sigma_L>0\). The subscript here is a noise standard deviation, not a clock. At level \(i\), hold \(\sigma_i\) fixed for \(K_i\) iterations:

\[x\leftarrow x+\eta_i s_\theta(x,\sigma_i) +\sqrt{2\eta_i}\,\xi.\]

Then move to \(\sigma_{i+1}\) using the current particles as starting states. Large noise can smooth sharp structure and help travel between separated modes; smaller noise gradually restores detail. This is the annealed Langevin approach used by Song and Ermon.

It is a sequence of fixed-target sampling problems. Finite iterations, finite steps, and approximate scores need not yield equilibrium at any level. Even perfect equilibration at the last level gives \(p_{\sigma_L}\), a smoothed law, not exactly the clean distribution. The starting distribution at the largest noise level is also a choice or approximation, not automatically exact.

Predictor–corrector: change the noise level, then improve sampling there

A predictor advances from \(t_k\) to \(t_{k-1}<t_k\) using a reverse-SDE or probability-flow step. A corrector then holds \(t_{k-1}\) fixed and runs a few Langevin updates targeting \(p_{t_{k-1}}\). This is the predictor–corrector framework of Song et al..

A schematic ODE-predictor version is

start with a terminal sample x
# v_theta(x,t) = f(x,t) - 0.5*g(t)^2 * s_theta(x,t)
for each descending pair t_k, t_{k-1}:
    h = t_k - t_{k-1}                  # h > 0
    x = x - h * v_theta(x, t_k)        # predictor: change level
    repeat K times:
        draw fresh xi ~ N(0, I)
        x = x + eta * s_theta(x, t_{k-1}) + sqrt(2*eta) * xi
                                        # corrector: hold level fixed
return x at a positive minimum noise level

Exact Langevin transitions preserve the target at the frozen level. Euler corrector steps generally have discretization bias, and a few such steps are not guaranteed to improve every error. The density-level decomposition motivates the method; arbitrary finite predictor/corrector steps are not an exact factorization of the reverse-SDE transition.

Compare the objectives

Method What is held fixed or changed? Ideal density statement
Ordinary Langevin One target \(p\), increasing \(u\) Preserves \(p\); convergence needs additional conditions
Annealed Langevin A sequence of frozen smoothed targets Approaches each target with exact dynamics and enough time; ULA adds step bias
Reverse diffusion SDE \(t=T-\tau\) changes continuously Follows \(p_{T-\tau}\) with exact initialization, score, and dynamics
Probability-flow ODE The same changing noise level Same prescribed marginals with exact initialization, score, and integration; different trajectories

Langevin uses the score to preserve a density while particles explore it. Reverse diffusion uses the score, the reversed drift, and a changing noise level to move through a prescribed family of densities. Their exact connection is the transport-plus-Langevin decomposition, not a time rescaling that removes the difference in coefficients.
