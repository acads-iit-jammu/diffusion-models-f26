From One-Dimensional Transport to Neural ODEs and Higher Dimensions

NoteRetired material

This earlier tutorial sequence is retained for reference. The current teaching sequence is Probability-Flow ODE, Parts 1–5 (Lectures 12–16). Follow those lectures for the current notation, derivations, and notebook exercises. These older notes contain additional background but are no longer part of the main reading sequence.

NoteTutorial 4 of 4 · Connections and generalization

Before this: the first three tutorials. We first finish the transport connections in one dimension. Only the final section introduces vectors, Jacobian matrices, divergence, and multidimensional diffusion.

Reading route: change of variables → continuous normalizing flows → diffusion likelihood → flow matching → extension to \(d\) dimensions. Shared notation.

Looking for Langevin dynamics? It is now in Tutorial 2.

Generation is a map applied to a random input

Draw \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and set \(X=\mu+aZ\), \(a>0\). This deterministic map produces \(X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,a^2)\). An interval expands by \(a\), so its density is divided by \(a\). More generally, for an invertible differentiable scalar map \(x=F(z)\) with differentiable inverse,

\[\boxed{\log p_X(F(z))=\log p_Z(z)-\log|F'(z)|.}\]

Such a smooth invertible map is a diffeomorphism. A discrete normalizing flow composes maps \(F=F_K\circ\cdots\circ F_1\) whose inverses and derivatives are tractable. The chain rule makes their log-density corrections add. The construction transports probability; it neither creates nor destroys it.

Continuous layers give an ODE

Small updates \(x_{k+1}=x_k+h v(x_k,t_k)\) are Euler steps. Under suitable regularity and convergence conditions, their small-step limit follows \(\dot x_t=v(x_t,t)\), with flow \(\Psi_{r\to t}\).

A continuously differentiable field, regular in time, with unique solutions existing forward and backward over the whole interval gives a diffeomorphic flow. A global spatial Lipschitz bound means \(|v(x,t)-v(y,t)|\le L|x-y|\) with one finite \(L\) across the time interval. Such bounds are one useful sufficient condition for existence and uniqueness. If two trajectories met at the same position and time, uniqueness backward would give the same initial point. Thus distinct initial states cannot merge on such an interval.

For an intermediate time \(r<t_*<t\), the composition and inverse rules are

\[\Psi_{r\to t}=\Psi_{t_*\to t}\circ\Psi_{r\to t_*},\qquad \Psi_{r\to t}^{-1}=\Psi_{t\to r}.\]

Finite numerical steps need not share the exact flow’s invertibility. For \(\dot x=-x\), the exact step is \(e^{-h}x\), always invertible at finite \(h\). Euler gives \((1-h)x\), which collapses everything at \(h=1\).

When a neural network supplies \(v_\theta\), we have a neural ODE. When we use that ODE to transport a base distribution and track its density, we have a continuous normalizing flow (CNF). A neural ODE used only for hidden-state prediction need not be a generative density model.

Track density continuously

Tutorial 1 derived

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\log q_t(x_t)=-\partial_xv(x_t,t)}\]

from conservation \(\partial_tq_t=-\partial_x(vq_t)\). Thus the scalar derivative of velocity replaces the discrete log-derivative correction per unit time.

Derivation: differentiate the flow with respect to its starting point

Write \(\Psi'_{0\to t}(x_0)=\partial x_t/\partial x_0\) for the flow’s derivative with respect to its initial position. Differentiating the ODE gives

\[\frac{d}{dt}\Psi'_{0\to t}(x_0) =(\partial_xv)(x_t,t)\Psi'_{0\to t}(x_0),\qquad \Psi'_{0\to0}=1.\]

Consequently, \(\Psi'_{0\to t}(x_0)=\exp(\int_0^t\partial_xv(x_r,r)dr)>0\). Scalar change of variables gives \(q_t(x_t)=q_0(x_0)/\Psi'_{0\to t}(x_0)\), reproducing the density identity. In one dimension the exact regular ODE flow is increasing: particle order is preserved. For contraction \(v=-x\), the flow derivative is \(e^{-t}\) and \(q_t(x_t)=e^tq_0(x_0)\).

Diffusion’s probability-flow ODE is a CNF

Tutorial 3 constructs \(v=f-g^2s_t/2\). It therefore provides a continuous normalizing flow whenever the needed regularity and endpoint conditions hold. Its forward SDE and deterministic ODE have the same one-time marginal densities, but different joint couplings of their particles across time.

For Brownian spreading from \(\mathcal N(\mu,a_0^2)\),

\[X_t^{\mathrm{SDE}}=X_0+gW_t,\qquad X_t^{\mathrm{ODE}}=\mu+\sqrt{\frac{a_0^2+g^2t}{a_0^2}}(X_0-\mu).\]

Both have law \(\mathcal N(\mu,a_0^2+g^2t)\). Yet conditional on \(X_0\), the SDE still has random outcomes while the ODE has one outcome. Their covariances with the starting point are \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_0,X_t^{\mathrm{SDE}})=a_0^2\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_0,X_t^{\mathrm{ODE}})=a_0\sqrt{a_0^2+g^2t}\). The ODE’s correlation with \(X_0\) is one; for \(g>0,t>0\), the SDE’s is \(a_0/\sqrt{a_0^2+g^2t}<1\). Equal marginals do not specify which particle arrived from which starting point. This is why noisy corruption and invertible deterministic transport can coexist.

Derivation: the likelihood identity and its sign

Use a learned score \(s_\theta\) to define \(v_\theta=f-g^2s_\theta/2\). Choose a terminal density \(\pi_T\). Backward integration induces a model density \(q_{\theta,0}\). To evaluate it at \(x_0\), solve forward to \(x_T\) and integrate:

\[\log q_{\theta,T}(x_T)-\log q_{\theta,0}(x_0) =-\int_0^T\partial_xv_\theta(x_t,t)dt.\]

Since \(q_{\theta,T}=\pi_T\),

\[\boxed{\log q_{\theta,0}(x_0)=\log\pi_T(x_T) +\int_0^T\partial_xv_\theta(x_t,t)dt.}\]

The plus sign comes from rearranging the density identity. For \(v=-x\) and \(\pi_T=\mathcal N(0,1)\), backward generation gives variance \(e^{2T}\); the formula is \(\log\phi_1(e^{-T}x_0)-T\), which is exactly that broader Gaussian’s log density.

This is the learned ODE model’s likelihood, not automatically the true data likelihood. With approximate scores, ODE and reverse-SDE marginals may differ. Numerical integration introduces evaluation error. If sampling stops at \(t_{\min}>0\), use that lower limit: the result is a density at positive noise. A smooth diffeomorphism cannot turn a smooth positive density into discrete atoms; endpoint singularities require a limit, smoothing, or a cutoff.

Learn velocities directly: flow matching

Score-based diffusion learns \(s_\theta\) and constructs a velocity from it. Flow matching instead regresses on tractable velocity targets along chosen conditional paths. Both may use the same CNF architecture; the learning objective and path construction are different choices.

For example, between a paired random source \(Z\) and data value \(X_{\mathrm{data}}\), define \(\widetilde X_t=(1-t)Z+tX_{\mathrm{data}}\), \(0\le t\le1\). Its velocity is \(U_t=\partial_t\widetilde X_t=X_{\mathrm{data}}-Z\), constant along this conditional path. Squared-error regression on \((\widetilde X_t,t)\) learns \(v(x,t)=\mathbb E[U_t\mid\widetilde X_t=x]\). This is a conditional average, not a promise that the fitted ODE follows each pair’s straight line.

Here \(t=0\) is the source and \(t=1\) is data, opposite to Tutorial 3’s corruption clock. The Gaussian-interpolant subsection below returns to that corruption convention: \(t=0\) is data and increasing \(t\) adds noise.

Derivation: why the averaged velocity transports the marginal

For a smooth test function \(\varphi\) of compact support, differentiating gives

\[\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb E\varphi(\widetilde X_t) =\mathbb E[\varphi'(\widetilde X_t)U_t] =\mathbb E[\varphi'(\widetilde X_t)v(\widetilde X_t,t)].\]

The second equality conditions on the observed position and uses the tower property. If \(q_t\) is the interpolant density, integration by parts gives

\[\int\varphi\partial_tq_tdx=\int\varphi'vq_tdx =-\int\varphi\partial_x(vq_t)dx.\]

Thus \(\partial_tq_t=-\partial_x(vq_t)\), in the integrated sense and pointwise when smooth. Appropriate regularity and uniqueness are needed to identify this path with the actual ODE marginals.

Optional derivation: recover probability flow from Gaussian interpolants

Keep a single pair \((X_0,\epsilon)\) fixed and set \(\widetilde X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\epsilon\). Its one-time laws are the corruption laws \(p_t\), but its paths are not Brownian paths. On an interior interval with differentiable positive \(\alpha_t,\sigma_t\), its velocity is

\[U_t=\dot\alpha_tX_0+\dot\sigma_t\epsilon.\]

For linear forward drift \(f(x,t)=b(t)x\), the corruption coefficients satisfy

\[\dot\alpha_t=b(t)\alpha_t,\qquad 2\sigma_t\dot\sigma_t=2b(t)\sigma_t^2+g(t)^2.\]

Using Tweedie and \(\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x]=-\sigma_ts_t(x)\),

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[U_t\mid\widetilde X_t=x] &=\frac{\dot\alpha_t}{\alpha_t}[x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x)] -\sigma_t\dot\sigma_t s_t(x)\\ &=b(t)x-\tfrac12g(t)^2s_t(x)=v(x,t). \end{aligned}\]

This particular velocity regression recovers probability flow. Other path choices may produce different transports. For VP with \(\beta(0)>0\), \(\dot\sigma_t\) grows like \(t^{-1/2}\) near zero. Use a positive cutoff or suitable weights to make training losses finite; interior formulas do not guarantee regular endpoints.

Only now: extend the picture to d dimensions

Replace scalar position by a vector \(x=(x_1,\ldots,x_d)\). A particle now carries one velocity component per coordinate. The principles—transport, spreading, and conservation—are unchanged; the notation records contributions from all coordinate directions. Bold type is unnecessary: this section explicitly switches \(x,X_t,v,f,s\) from scalars to vectors.

Gradient, divergence, and Laplacian

For a scalar function \(a(x)\), the gradient collects spatial derivatives: \(\nabla a=(\partial_{x_1}a,\ldots,\partial_{x_d}a)\). For a vector field \(v\), its divergence is \(\nabla\cdot v=\sum_i\partial_{x_i}v_i\). It measures local net expansion. The Laplacian of a scalar is \(\Delta a=\sum_i\partial_{x_i}^2a=\nabla\cdot\nabla a\).

Consider a small rectangular box. Opposite faces in direction \(i\) contribute net outflow \(\partial_{x_i}(q_tv_i)\) per unit volume. Summing over directions extends the interval argument to

\[\boxed{\partial_tq_t=-\nabla\cdot(q_tv).}\]

For \(v(x)=-x\), divergence is \(-d\). The flow is \(e^{-t}x_0\), volume shrinks by \(e^{-dt}\), and density along a trajectory grows by \(e^{dt}\).

Brownian motion and Itô’s lemma

Let \(W_t\) contain \(d\) independent one-dimensional Brownian motions. Its increment covariance over \(h\) is \(hI_d\). Keep scalar, spatially constant noise amplitude \(g(t)\):

\[dX_t=f(X_t,t)dt+g(t)dW_t.\]

For a scalar test function \(F(x,t)\), the Itô correction sums the coordinate second derivatives:

\[dF=\left(\partial_tF+f\cdot\nabla F+\tfrac12g^2\Delta F\right)dt +g\nabla F\cdot dW_t.\]

Independent Brownian coordinates have zero cross quadratic variation; this is why no mixed-derivative terms appear for isotropic noise.

The corresponding density equation and probability-flow velocity are

\[\boxed{\partial_tp_t=-\nabla\cdot(fp_t)+\tfrac12g^2\Delta p_t, \qquad v=f-\tfrac12g^2\nabla\log p_t.}\]

The same identity \(\nabla p_t=p_t\nabla\log p_t\) proves the correspondence. For state-dependent or non-isotropic diffusion, the density equation and velocity require the appropriate diffusion-matrix derivatives; these scalar \(g(t)\) formulas should not be reused unchanged.

Gaussian scores and Langevin

For \(p=\mathcal N(\mu,\Sigma)\) with positive-definite covariance,

\[s(x)=-\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu).\]

The score is an inward vector field. The score ODE contracts in covariance eigendirections; Langevin \(dX_t=s(X_t)dt+\sqrt2\,dW_t\) balances this with isotropic noise and has target \(p\) as its invariant density.

In several dimensions, stationarity requires zero divergence of flux, not necessarily zero flux itself. For a two-dimensional standard normal, one may add the circulating drift \(w(x)=(-x_2,x_1)\): direct differentiation gives \(\nabla\cdot(pw)=0\). The population stays stationary while particles also circulate. The score drift is the zero-flux choice; the one-dimensional uniqueness argument does not apply unchanged in higher dimensions.

For isotropic Gaussian corruption, the learning and denoising identities become

\[s_t(x)=\mathbb E[-\epsilon/\sigma_t\mid X_t=x],\qquad \mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x]=\frac{x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x)}{\alpha_t},\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d).\]

Replace scalar squared errors with squared Euclidean norms. The conditional mean proof is unchanged, coordinate by coordinate.

Jacobians replace scalar derivatives in normalizing flows

Write \(DF\) for the Jacobian matrix of \(F\), whose entries are \((DF)_{ij}=\partial F_i/\partial x_j\). For a linear map, \(|\det DF|\) is the factor by which a small box’s volume changes. A nonlinear differentiable map is locally linear, giving

\[\log p_X(F(z))=\log p_Z(z)-\log|\det DF(z)|.\]

For a tiny step \(F(x)=x+h v(x,t)\), its Jacobian is \(I+hDv\). To first order,

\[\log\det(I+hDv)=h\operatorname{tr}Dv+O(h^2) =h\nabla\cdot v+O(h^2).\]

Here trace is the sum of diagonal entries. In two dimensions, \(\det(I+hA)=1+h(A_{11}+A_{22})+h^2\det A\), which displays the first-order term explicitly. The continuous density and likelihood identities consequently use

\[\frac{d}{dt}\log q_t(x_t)=-\nabla\cdot v(x_t,t),\qquad \log q_{\theta,0}(x_0)=\log\pi_T(x_T)+\int_0^T\nabla\cdot v_\theta(x_t,t)dt.\]

In high dimensions, stochastic trace estimation can approximate divergence and adds numerical error. The mathematical change-of-variables identity is exact.

Compare the constructions

Construction What is specified or learned? How are samples produced?
Discrete normalizing flow Invertible maps Compose maps on a random base sample
Neural ODE / CNF Velocity and, for a CNF, density evolution Integrate deterministic transport
Score-based diffusion Corruption law and its learned marginal scores Reverse SDE or probability-flow ODE
Flow matching Conditional paths and velocity regression targets Integrate the learned averaged velocity
Langevin Score of a fixed target Run a balanced stochastic process toward equilibrium

A choice of architecture does not fix a training objective. A probability-flow ODE is a CNF derived from a diffusion density path; a generic CNF need not come from diffusion. Langevin’s equilibrium objective differs from traversing a prescribed path of marginals.

  4. What replaces velocity slope in more than one dimension?
TipAnswers
  1. It transports a random initial state.
  2. They share one-time marginals but define different joint couplings of endpoints.
  3. The density induced by the chosen terminal law and learned ODE, not automatically the data density.
  4. Divergence, the sum of coordinatewise velocity slopes.
