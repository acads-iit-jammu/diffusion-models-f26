Tweedie’s Formula: The Score as a Bayes-Optimal Denoiser

A standalone derivation. Prerequisites: Gaussian densities, Bayes’ rule, conditional expectation, and differentiation. No ODE or SDE theory is needed.

Place in the teaching sequence. This is the statistical derivation in the three-note sequence: geometric introduction → worked sampling tutorial → this proof. It can also be read independently. We reserve \(\varrho\) for a Gaussian prior’s standard deviation, keeping \(\tau\) available for the reverse clock in the companion notes.

We observe a corrupted signal

\[\boxed{Y=X+\sigma\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d),\quad\varepsilon\perp X,\quad\sigma>0.}\]

The clean signal \(X\) is random because it represents the population of possible data. Its law \(P_X\) is our prior. We observe \(Y=y\), but do not know either \(X\) or the particular noise realization. What should we return as a denoised estimate?

The answer depends on the loss. Under squared Euclidean error, the best answer is the posterior mean. Tweedie’s formula expresses that mean using only the score of the noisy marginal density:

\[\boxed{\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y]=y+\sigma^2\nabla_y\log p_Y(y).}\]

We will prove both statements: why this is Bayes-optimal, and why the score gives exactly that estimate. Open Lab 4: Tweedie’s denoiser to explore the two-point example as you read.

1. What does “Bayes-optimal denoiser” mean?

A denoiser is a function \(D\) that turns an observation into an estimate \(D(Y)\). Its mean squared error, or Bayes risk, is

\[R(D)=\mathbb E\|X-D(Y)\|^2.\]

Assume \(\mathbb E\|X\|^2<\infty\) and consider denoisers with finite risk. Then \(D(Y)\) is square-integrable too, so the cross term below is well defined. Let \(m(Y)=\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\). Add and subtract \(m(Y)\) inside the error:

\[X-D(Y)=[X-m(Y)]+[m(Y)-D(Y)].\]

Expanding the squared norm produces two squared terms and a cross term. Given \(Y\), the second bracket is known and \(\mathbb E[X-m(Y)\mid Y]=0\). Therefore the cross term has expectation zero:

\[ \mathbb E\big[(X-m(Y))^\top(m(Y)-D(Y))\big]=0. \]

It follows that

\[\boxed{ R(D)=\underbrace{\mathbb E\|X-m(Y)\|^2}_{\text{irreducible posterior uncertainty}} +\underbrace{\mathbb E\|m(Y)-D(Y)\|^2}_{\text{avoidable estimation error}}. }\]

The first term does not depend on \(D\). The second is nonnegative and is zero exactly when \(D(Y)=m(Y)\) almost surely. Thus

\[\boxed{D^*(y)=\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y].}\]

At a particular observation, the same argument reads

\[\mathbb E[\|X-a\|^2\mid Y=y] =\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(X\mid Y=y)+\|m(y)-a\|^2.\]

“Optimal” means optimal for this loss and this joint model, not guaranteed to recover the actual hidden signal. The remaining risk is the average posterior variance. With other losses, the optimal estimate can change: absolute error in one dimension gives a posterior median, for example.

2. The density we differentiate is the noisy marginal

Conditional on \(X=x\), the observation is Gaussian:

\[p(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{d/2}} \exp\left[-\frac{\|y-x\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right].\]

Averaging over possible clean signals gives

\[\boxed{p_Y(y)=\int p(y\mid x)\,P_X(dx).}\]

If \(X\) has a density, \(P_X(dx)=p_X(x)dx\). If \(X\) takes finitely many values, the integral is a sum. This notation lets the same proof apply to continuous data, discrete data, and data supported on a lower-dimensional set. Gaussian noise with \(\sigma>0\) makes \(p_Y\) smooth and strictly positive everywhere.

The score in this tutorial is

\[s_Y(y)=\nabla_y\log p_Y(y).\]

It is a gradient with respect to the observed data coordinates. It is not a parameter gradient, not the clean-data score, and not the score of one fixed conditional likelihood \(p(y\mid x)\).

3. Differentiate the Gaussian, then use Bayes’ rule

The Gaussian kernel has derivative

\[\nabla_y p(y\mid x)=\frac{x-y}{\sigma^2}p(y\mid x).\]

Differentiate under the integral:

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_y p_Y(y) &=\int\nabla_y p(y\mid x)\,P_X(dx)\\ &=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\int(x-y)p(y\mid x)\,P_X(dx). \end{aligned} \]

For fixed positive \(\sigma\), Gaussian kernels and their first derivatives are locally uniformly bounded as functions of \(x\) for \(y\) in a compact set. This provides a dominating integrable bound against the probability measure \(P_X\) and justifies the interchange. The finite-second-moment assumption above also makes the squared-error decision problem well defined.

Now divide by the positive marginal density:

\[\frac{\nabla_y p_Y(y)}{p_Y(y)} =\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\int(x-y) \frac{p(y\mid x)P_X(dx)}{p_Y(y)}.\]

Bayes’ rule identifies the fraction as the posterior measure:

\[P_{X\mid Y=y}(dx)=\frac{p(y\mid x)P_X(dx)}{p_Y(y)}.\]

Hence

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla_y\log p_Y(y) &=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\int(x-y)\,P_{X\mid Y=y}(dx)\\ &=\frac{\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y]-y}{\sigma^2}. \end{aligned} \]

Rearranging proves Tweedie’s formula:

\[\boxed{m(y)=y+\sigma^2s_Y(y).}\]

The whole proof is a Gaussian derivative followed by Bayes’ rule. Bayes optimality comes from the squared-error decomposition in Section 1, not from differentiating the density alone.

4. What the formula tells us geometrically

The vector

\[\boxed{\sigma^2s_Y(y)=m(y)-y}\]

is the displacement from the observed noisy point to its posterior-mean clean estimate. Thus a score is not merely an abstract derivative: it encodes a denoising displacement, measured in units of noise variance.

This is an exact identity for this Gaussian observation model. It is not an instruction to keep applying a fixed denoiser as a sampling algorithm. A posterior mean averages uncertainty and can lie between plausible clean signals. Likewise, repeated score ascent can concentrate particles at modes instead of reproducing a distribution.

For additive noise, \(s_Y(y)\) points toward \(m(y)\). For scaled corruption, the correct endpoint of that arrow is the scaled posterior mean; we will make that distinction explicit below.

5. Check it on a Gaussian prior

Let \(X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\varrho^2)\) in one dimension, with \(\varrho>0\). Then

\[Y\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\varrho^2+\sigma^2),\qquad s_Y(y)=-\frac{y-\mu}{\varrho^2+\sigma^2}.\]

Tweedie gives

\[ \begin{aligned} m(y)&=y-\frac{\sigma^2}{\varrho^2+\sigma^2}(y-\mu)\\ &=\boxed{\frac{\varrho^2}{\varrho^2+\sigma^2}y +\frac{\sigma^2}{\varrho^2+\sigma^2}\mu}. \end{aligned} \]

The denoiser balances observation and prior mean. If observation noise is small, it trusts \(y\). If noise is large, it trusts \(\mu\). The posterior variance is \(\varrho^2\sigma^2/(\varrho^2+\sigma^2)\), so even the optimal estimate usually has nonzero error.

Notice why the marginal matters. The clean score would be \(-(y-\mu)/\varrho^2\) and would give the wrong shrinkage. Tweedie requires the score of the noise-smoothed density \(p_Y\).

6. The VP diffusion form

At a fixed time, consider

\[\boxed{X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\perp X_0,\quad\sigma_t>0.}\]

In the usual VP convention, \(\alpha_t^2+\sigma_t^2=1\), but the derivation needs only the linear Gaussian observation model. Set \(Z=\alpha_tX_0\) and apply the additive formula to \(X_t=Z+\sigma_t\varepsilon\):

\[\mathbb E[Z\mid X_t=x]=x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x),\qquad s_t(x)=\nabla_x\log p_t(x).\]

Because \(\alpha_t\) is known at the fixed time,

\[\boxed{\alpha_t\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x] =x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x).}\]

When \(\alpha_t\ne0\) (usually \(\alpha_t>0\)), this gives

\[\boxed{m_t(x)=\mathbb E[X_0\mid X_t=x] =\frac{x+\sigma_t^2s_t(x)}{\alpha_t}.}\]

The companion tutorials write this same posterior mean as \(\widehat X_0(x,t)=m_t(x)\). The shorter \(m_t\) notation here emphasizes that it is a conditional mean, and is also used in the denoising lab.

Equivalently,

\[\boxed{s_t(x)=\frac{\alpha_t m_t(x)-x}{\sigma_t^2}.}\]

The score points from \(x\) toward \(\alpha_t m_t(x)\), not generally toward \(m_t(x)\) itself. The distinction matters when the clean signal has been attenuated. At \(\alpha_t=0\), the observation contains no information about \(X_0\), so its posterior mean is the prior mean. Dividing by \(\alpha_t\) is then invalid. At \(\sigma_t=0\), the smoothed-score formula cannot be used as written.

7. Noise prediction is another expression of the same conditional mean

Rearrange the corruption equation and take conditional expectations:

\[ \begin{aligned} \varepsilon&=\frac{X_t-\alpha_tX_0}{\sigma_t},\\ \mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_t=x] &=\frac{x-\alpha_t m_t(x)}{\sigma_t} =-\sigma_t s_t(x). \end{aligned} \]

Write \(e_t(x)=\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_t=x]\). This is \(\varepsilon^\star(x,t)\) in the companion tutorials; a fitted predictor \(e_\theta(x,t)\) here corresponds to their \(\varepsilon_\theta(x,t)\). Then

\[\boxed{ s_t=-e_t/\sigma_t,\qquad m_t=(x-\sigma_te_t)/\alpha_t,\qquad e_t=(x-\alpha_tm_t)/\sigma_t. }\]

These are identities between optimal fitted functions, not claims that the particular random noise realization can be recovered. Under squared loss, a noise predictor estimates \(e_t\), a clean-data predictor estimates \(m_t\), and denoising score matching estimates \(s_t\).

Indeed, the conditional-score target is \(-\varepsilon/\sigma_t\), and

\[\mathbb E[-\varepsilon/\sigma_t\mid X_t=x]=s_t(x).\]

That is why regression against a known corruption target can recover the unknown marginal score.

The parameterizations encode the same information at the population optimum when \(\alpha_t,\sigma_t\ne0\). Their training losses are not automatically equivalent under arbitrary time weighting. For example, with \(s_\theta=-e_\theta/\sigma_t\),

\[\lambda(t)\|s_\theta+\varepsilon/\sigma_t\|^2 =\frac{\lambda(t)}{\sigma_t^2}\|e_\theta-\varepsilon\|^2.\]

With \(s_\theta=(\alpha_tD_\theta-x_t)/\sigma_t^2\), the same score-target loss equals \(\lambda(t)\alpha_t^2\|D_\theta-X_0\|^2/\sigma_t^4\). Finite networks, optimization, and time weights can therefore make the practical training problems behave differently.

8. Two possible clean values: derive the tanh denoiser

Suppose \(X_0\) takes values \(-1\) and \(+1\) with equal probabilities. Observe

\[X_t=\alpha_tX_0+\sigma_t\varepsilon,\qquad \alpha_t>0,\ \sigma_t>0.\]

The two likelihoods are Gaussians centered at \(-\alpha_t\) and \(+\alpha_t\). By Bayes’ rule, the posterior odds are

\[\frac{P(X_0=+1\mid x)}{P(X_0=-1\mid x)} =\exp\left[\frac{(x+\alpha_t)^2-(x-\alpha_t)^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\right] =\exp\left(\frac{2\alpha_t x}{\sigma_t^2}\right).\]

Writing \(\kappa=\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2\) gives

\[w_+(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-2\kappa}},\qquad w_-(x)=1-w_+(x).\]

The posterior mean is their signed difference:

\[\boxed{m_t(x)=w_+(x)-w_-(x)=\tanh\left(\frac{\alpha_t x}{\sigma_t^2}\right).}\]

Substitution into Tweedie yields

\[\boxed{s_t(x)=\frac{ \alpha_t\tanh(\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2)-x}{\sigma_t^2}.}\]

The tanh denoiser and the mixture score are two expressions of the same posterior calculation. An unequal prior would add half the log prior odds inside the tanh; the symmetric formula relies on the equal weights.

Posterior mean, posterior mode, and posterior sample differ

At \(x=0\), the posterior gives probability \(1/2\) to each clean value. Consequently:

Decision or draw Result at \(x=0\)
Posterior mean, optimal under squared error \(0\)
Posterior mode (MAP) A tie between \(-1\) and \(+1\)
Posterior sample Randomly \(-1\) or \(+1\), each with probability \(1/2\)

Zero is not an allowed clean value, but it is the best squared-error estimate. Its conditional risk is \(1\). Always choosing either endpoint has risk \(2\). An independent draw from the posterior also has conditional risk \(2\) here. Plausibility as a clean sample and optimality as a point estimate are different criteria.

For a general observation in this two-point example,

\[\operatorname{Var}(X_0\mid x)=1-m_t(x)^2,\]

and an estimate \(a\) has conditional risk

\[\boxed{R_x(a)=1-m_t(x)^2+(a-m_t(x))^2.}\]

Lab 4. Move the observed value, signal scale, and noise level. Read the posterior weights, posterior mean, and the score displacement. Move the candidate estimate away from the mean and watch its squared-error risk increase. At \(x=0\), compare the posterior mean with both possible clean values.

The small-noise limit needs a qualification

If \(\alpha_t\to1\) and \(\sigma_t\to0\), then for each fixed \(x\ne0\),

\[m_t(x)=\tanh(\alpha_t x/\sigma_t^2)\longrightarrow\operatorname{sign}(x).\]

At \(x=0\), the posterior mean remains zero by symmetry. This is a pointwise statement away from the decision boundary; it is not uniform near zero. At exactly zero noise the data law is atomic, with no ordinary density score on \(\mathbb R\). The positive-noise score has a different limiting behavior from the posterior mean. For every fixed \(x\notin\{-1,0,1\}\),

\[\sigma_t^2s_t(x)\longrightarrow\operatorname{sign}(x)-x\ne0,\]

so its magnitude diverges as the noise vanishes. At \(x=0\), the score remains exactly zero by symmetry. At the clean points \(x=\pm1\), the behavior depends on how the signal scale approaches one: with additive corruption (\(\alpha_t\equiv1\)) the score tends to zero, whereas with VP corruption (\(\alpha_t=\sqrt{1-\sigma_t^2}\)) it tends to \(\mp\tfrac12\). These are limits of smoothed-density scores, not scores of the atomic law itself.

9. A useful extension: correlated Gaussian noise

If \(Y=X+\eta\), independently, with \(\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma)\) for known positive-definite \(\Sigma\), then

\[\nabla_y p(y\mid x)=\Sigma^{-1}(x-y)p(y\mid x).\]

Repeating the proof gives

\[\boxed{\mathbb E[X\mid Y=y]=y+\Sigma\nabla_y\log p_Y(y).}\]

For non-isotropic noise, the covariance transforms the score into a denoising displacement; they need not point in the same Euclidean direction. The simple \(\sigma^2\) multiplier is specific to isotropic Gaussian corruption.

10. How this fits into diffusion sampling

At each positive noise level, the model estimates a local field that encodes a posterior mean. A diffusion sampler uses that field together with its known drift, noise schedule, and current time to take the next step along a path of distributions. Returning a posterior mean once, or repeatedly applying a fixed-noise denoiser, is not generally equivalent to drawing a clean-data sample.

The vector-fields tutorial explains the reverse-SDE and probability-flow ODE updates. This note supplies their statistical bridge:

\[ \text{squared-error optimality} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{posterior mean} \ \overset{\text{Gaussian corruption}}{\longleftrightarrow}\ \text{score}. \]

Exercises with checks

  1. Let \(X\sim\mathcal N(0,4)\) and noise variance be \(1\). Find \(s_Y(y)\) and \(m(y)\). Check: \(s_Y=-y/5\), \(m=4y/5\).
  2. For equal two-point data, take \(\alpha=1\), \(\sigma=1\), \(y=0.5\). Compute the posterior mean, score, and minimum conditional risk. Check: approximately \(0.4621\), \(-0.0379\), and \(0.7864\).
  3. If \(P(X_0=+1)=\pi\in(0,1)\), derive the mean. Check: \(\tanh(\alpha x/\sigma^2+\tfrac12\log[\pi/(1-\pi)])\).
  4. Why is the sampled target \(-\varepsilon/\sigma_t\) usually different from the true score at that input? Check: the score is its conditional average over all compatible clean-signal and noise pairs.
