11 · Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM)

NoteDraft

These lecture notes are in development and may be revised. This lecture uses finite Gaussian transitions; no knowledge of Brownian motion or SDEs is required. The aim is to understand what is trained and how the trained model generates samples.

1. Generation by reversing a corruption sequence

Start with a data observation \(X_0\). Repeatedly contract its current value and add independent Gaussian noise. With enough corruption, its distribution is close to a standard normal. We then train a model to generate observations by traversing a learned reverse sequence, starting from a normal sample.

Use \(N\) steps and normalized times \(t_n=n/N\). Data are at \(t_0=0\); the terminal prior is at \(t_N=1\). For now every observation is scalar. The same formulas later apply coordinatewise to vectors and images with independent Gaussian corruption noise.

2. One step and its cumulative form

Choose a discrete noise variance \(0<\beta_n<1\) and set \(a_n=1-\beta_n\). One step is

\[X_n=\sqrt{a_n}X_{n-1}+\sqrt{\beta_n}\varepsilon_n, \qquad \varepsilon_n\overset{\mathrm{iid}}\sim\mathcal N(0,1).\]

Here \(X_n\) means \(X_{t_n}\). With \(\bar a_n=\prod_{j=1}^n a_j\), the conditional law after \(n\) steps is

\[\boxed{X_n\mid X_0=x_0\sim \mathcal N\left(\sqrt{\bar a_n}x_0,1-\bar a_n\right).}\]

Consequently, a training example at step \(n\) can be drawn directly:

\[X_n=\sqrt{\bar a_n}X_0+\sqrt{1-\bar a_n}\,\varepsilon.\]

This equality constructs the conditional distribution at one chosen time. Using a single shared \(\varepsilon\) at every time does not simulate the forward Markov chain’s joint trajectories.

Many DDPM sources call our \(a_n\) “\(\alpha_n\).” We use \(a_n\) here to reserve \(\alpha_t\) for the cumulative amplitude used throughout Parts 1–5. A discrete \(\beta_n\) is a variance, whereas a continuous \(\beta(t)\) is a rate.

If \(\bar a_N\) is small, the terminal distribution is approximately normal. It is not exactly normal for arbitrary data at finite nonzero \(\bar a_N\). For example, its mean is \(\sqrt{\bar a_N}\mathbb E[X_0]\) and its variance is \(\bar a_N\operatorname{Var}(X_0)+1-\bar a_N\).

3. What is known about a reverse step?

If the clean observation is also known, the forward process gives an exactly computable conditional distribution:

\[p(x_{n-1}\mid x_n,x_0)=\mathcal N(\widetilde m_n,\widetilde\beta_n),\qquad n\ge2,\]

where

\[\widetilde\beta_n=\beta_n\frac{1-\bar a_{n-1}}{1-\bar a_n},\]

\[\widetilde m_n= \frac{\sqrt{\bar a_{n-1}}\beta_n}{1-\bar a_n}x_0 +\frac{\sqrt{a_n}(1-\bar a_{n-1})}{1-\bar a_n}x_n.\]

At \(n=1\), conditioning on \(x_0\) gives a point mass at \(x_0\), and the variance formula is zero.

During generation we do not know \(x_0\). The actual conditional \(p(x_{n-1}\mid x_n)\) averages over the posterior possibilities for \(x_0\) and generally is not Gaussian. A DDPM models it with a learned Gaussian transition. Inserting an estimate of \(x_0\) into the displayed Gaussian is a model choice, not an exact finite-step inversion of arbitrary data corruption.

4. Learn a noise predictor

Train a network \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_n,t_n)\) using pairs constructed from data and newly sampled noise:

\[\boxed{\mathcal J(\theta)= \mathbb E_{X_0,n,\varepsilon}\left[ \left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar a_n}X_0+ \sqrt{1-\bar a_n}\varepsilon,t_n)\right)^2\right].}\]

Here \(n\) is commonly sampled uniformly from \(1,\ldots,N\). For vectors, use squared Euclidean error, with an explicitly chosen sum or coordinate average.

Squared-error regression estimates

\[\varepsilon_\theta(x,t_n)\approx\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_n=x].\]

An observed \(x_n\) can arise from several clean observations and noise draws. The optimal network predicts their conditional mean noise, not the particular realized noise with certainty.

Convert the output into a clean-data estimate:

\[\widehat x_0(x_n,n)= \frac{x_n-\sqrt{1-\bar a_n}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_n,t_n)}{\sqrt{\bar a_n}}.\]

The simple noise loss is a standard DDPM training choice. It is related to, but is not the full weighted variational likelihood objective. See Ho et al., Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models.

5. The ancestral sampler

Substituting the estimate into the posterior-mean expression yields the learned reverse mean

\[m_\theta(x_n,n)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{a_n}} \left(x_n-\frac{\beta_n}{\sqrt{1-\bar a_n}} \varepsilon_\theta(x_n,t_n)\right).\]

One common sampler uses the fixed posterior variance \(\widetilde\beta_n\):

\[\boxed{x_{n-1}=m_\theta(x_n,n)+\sqrt{\widetilde\beta_n}\,\varepsilon_n, \qquad \varepsilon_n\sim\mathcal N(0,1).}\]

The \(\varepsilon_n\) in this reverse update are fresh draws, unrelated to the forward-step noises. Start with \(x_N\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\) and repeat for \(n=N,\ldots,1\). At the final step, \(\widetilde\beta_1=0\), so no fresh noise is added. Other DDPM variants use different variance choices; training and sampling must specify which one is used.

This finite Gaussian chain is an ancestral DDPM sampler. It is not automatically the same algorithm as Euler–Maruyama for a continuous reverse SDE. Their connection requires an appropriate small-step limit.

6. A normal-data check

Suppose \(X_0\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\). Every forward marginal is then \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) and

\[\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_n=x]=\sqrt{1-\bar a_n}\,x.\]

The learned reverse mean at the population optimum becomes \(\sqrt{a_n}x_n\). The exact reverse conditional for this stationary normal chain has variance \(\beta_n\). The fixed posterior variance \(\widetilde\beta_n\) above is smaller: it conditions additionally on \(x_0\).

This provides a useful diagnostic. Even a perfect noise predictor does not make a finite-step Gaussian sampler with an arbitrary variance choice exact. Increasing resolution, changing the variance convention, and improving score estimation are different changes.

7. From discrete denoising to a vector field

At a positive noise level, the marginal score satisfies

\[s_{t_n}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\log p_{t_n}(x) =-\frac{\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X_n=x]}{\sqrt{1-\bar a_n}}.\]

The network therefore provides a score estimate. Part 3 derives this identity and its relationship to the posterior mean denoiser.

With a continuous corruption schedule, that score determines a probability-flow velocity. For a VP process,

\[v(x,t)=-\frac{\beta(t)}2\bigl[x+s_t(x)\bigr].\]

Part 1 develops why this velocity transports the required densities. Generation follows its ODE from \(t=1\) toward \(t=0\). This deterministic sampler and the stochastic reverse sampler share marginal densities under exact continuous-time conditions; their individual trajectories differ.

Appendix A. The cumulative corruption law

Suppose the accumulated signal coefficient after \(n-1\) steps is \(\sqrt{\bar a_{n-1}}\) and the conditional noise variance is \(1-\bar a_{n-1}\). The next step multiplies that variance by \(a_n\) and adds independent variance \(\beta_n\):

\[a_n(1-\bar a_{n-1})+\beta_n=1-\bar a_n.\]

The mean becomes \(\sqrt{a_n\bar a_{n-1}}x_0\). Induction establishes the formula without simulating intermediate states.

Appendix B. The conditional reverse Gaussian

For \(n\ge2\), Bayes’ rule gives

\[p(x_{n-1}\mid x_n,x_0)\propto p(x_n\mid x_{n-1})p(x_{n-1}\mid x_0).\]

As a function of \(u=x_{n-1}\), the negative log density, up to constants, is

\[\frac{(x_n-\sqrt{a_n}u)^2}{2\beta_n} +\frac{(u-\sqrt{\bar a_{n-1}}x_0)^2}{2(1-\bar a_{n-1})}.\]

The coefficient of \(u^2/2\) is

\[\frac{a_n}{\beta_n}+\frac{1}{1-\bar a_{n-1}} =\frac{1-\bar a_n}{\beta_n(1-\bar a_{n-1})}.\]

Its reciprocal is \(\widetilde\beta_n\). Multiplying that reciprocal by the linear coefficient \(\sqrt{a_n}x_n/\beta_n+\sqrt{\bar a_{n-1}}x_0/(1-\bar a_{n-1})\) gives \(\widetilde m_n\).
