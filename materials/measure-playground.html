The Measure Playground

Two ideas, made concrete: a σ-algebra is what a report tells you, and expectation is an area under the quantile curve.

Act I · InformationA σ-algebra is what a report tells you

Forget abstract shapes. Here is a concrete sample space: eight students, one of whom is picked at random, so each outcome ω has probability ⅛. The random variable X is the marks column — a number attached to every outcome.

Now suppose you do not get to see which student was picked. You are handed only a report. Choose what the report says, and read off the three things that follow: which students it cannot tell apart, exactly which events it settles (that list is the σ-algebra), and the best estimate of X it supports.

Act II · IntegrationExpectation is the area under the quantile curve

Take Ω = [0,1] with uniform mass and let X = Q(u) be the quantile (inverse-CDF) map of a two-bump mixture. Then E[X] = ∫₀¹ Q(u) du: the signed area under the curve. Lebesgue's construction is the staircase: approximate Q by a simple function (finitely many values), add value × width, refine.

The two acts are one story. A random variable is a number attached to each outcome, and expectation is its measure-weighted total. Act I computes it where the outcomes live — value × probability, box by box — and shows that a σ-algebra is just a record of which outcomes an observer can tell apart. Act II computes the same number after transporting the mass to [0,1], where it becomes an area under the quantile curve. Same number, two homes.
