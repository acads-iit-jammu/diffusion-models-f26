HW 1 — Random Variables, Joints, Conditionals, Bayes

Practice homework · 5 problems · 50 points total · no calculators needed beyond arithmetic.

Scope. Everything here is covered by Probability & Random Variables and the W02 primer deck. Distributions, likelihood and maximum likelihood are not examined here — they are HW 2.

Who this is for. No background beyond first-year calculus and algebra is assumed, and no branch-specific knowledge of any kind. Problem 4 needs only single-variable integration.

How to present your work. Full marks require the reasoning, not just the number. Where a probability is requested, leave it as an exact fraction and give a decimal to three places. State clearly which variable you are conditioning on every time you condition.

Difficulty, so you can plan

Problems 1 and 2 are straightforward; 3 and 4 are moderate; 5 is the most demanding and is best attempted last. Every problem is self-contained.

Problem 1 — The two maps (10 points)

Straightforward.

Roll one fair six-sided die once. Let

\[ X(\omega) = \omega, \qquad Y(\omega) = \begin{cases} 1 & \omega \text{ is prime},\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases} \]

  1. (2) Write down the sample space \(\Omega\). How many distinct events does this experiment have? Justify the count in one line.

  2. (2) List the two preimages \(\{\omega : Y(\omega) = 1\}\) and \(\{\omega : Y(\omega) = 0\}\).

  3. (3) A classmate writes “\(Y\) maps the event \(\{2,3,5\}\) to \(1\).” Explain in two or three sentences why this is the wrong description of what \(Y\) does, and write down the correct chain of steps that takes a random variable to a probability.

  4. (3) Compute \(\mathbb{P}(Y = 1)\) and \(\mathbb{P}(X \le 3,\ Y = 1)\). For the second, name the event explicitly before computing its probability.

Problem 2 — A table of counts (10 points)

Straightforward.

A plant inspects 200 finished components. Each is recorded by which machine made it (\(X \in \{A, B, C\}\)) and its quality grade (\(Y \in \{1, 2, 3\}\)):

\(Y=1\) \(Y=2\) \(Y=3\)
\(X=A\) 40 20 10
\(X=B\) 15 35 20
\(X=C\) 10 15 35

  1. (2) Compute the row totals, the column totals and the net, and from them the two marginal distributions \(\mathbb{P}(X = \cdot)\) and \(\mathbb{P}(Y = \cdot)\). Say in one line what you divided by, and why.

  2. (3) Compute the conditional distributions \(\mathbb{P}(Y = \cdot \mid X = A)\) and \(\mathbb{P}(Y = \cdot \mid X = C)\). Verify each sums to \(1\). What does the comparison between the two tell you about \(X\) and \(Y\)?

  3. (2) Compute \(\mathbb{P}(X = B \mid Y = 2)\). State which total you divided by and why it is that one and not the net.

  4. (3) If \(X\) and \(Y\) were independent, what count would you expect in the cell \((A, 1)\)? Compare with the observed count and state your conclusion. Would matching in this one cell have been enough to conclude independence?

Problem 3 — A screening test (10 points)

Moderate.

A condition affects \(1\%\) of a population. A screening test is applied to a randomly chosen person. The test is positive for \(95\%\) of those who have the condition, and — wrongly — for \(10\%\) of those who do not.

Let \(D\) be the event “has the condition” and \(+\) the event “tests positive”.

  1. (2) Write the three given numbers in conditional-probability notation. Which of them is a conditional, and on what?

  2. (3) Compute \(\mathbb{P}(+)\), stating the rule you are using and why the two cases you sum over are exhaustive and mutually exclusive.

  3. (3) Compute \(\mathbb{P}(D \mid +)\).

  4. (2) The test is “\(95\%\) accurate” on the diseased, yet your answer to (c) is far below \(95\%\). Explain why in one or two sentences. Then state which single number you would change, and in which direction, to raise \(\mathbb{P}(D \mid +)\) the most.

A useful check

Redo (b) and (c) by imagining 10,000 people and filling in a \(2 \times 2\) table of counts, as in Problem 2. You should get the same answer, and the arithmetic is easier. Doing it both ways is worth doing even though only one is required.

Problem 4 — A joint density (10 points)

Moderate.

Let \((X, Y)\) have joint density

\[ f(x,y) = c\,(x + y), \qquad 0 \le x \le 1,\ \ 0 \le y \le 2, \]

and \(f = 0\) elsewhere.

  1. (2) Find the constant \(c\).

  2. (3) Find both marginal densities \(f_X(x)\) and \(f_Y(y)\), stating their ranges. Verify that each integrates to \(1\). Which variable did you integrate out in each case?

  3. (3) Find the conditional density \(f(y \mid x)\) for a fixed \(x \in [0,1]\), and verify that it integrates to \(1\) over \(y\). Explain in one sentence what you divided by, and why that is the continuous analogue of “divide the row by its row total”.

  4. (2) Are \(X\) and \(Y\) independent? Justify by an explicit comparison, not by inspection alone.

Problem 5 — A hidden cause (10 points)

The most demanding problem. Attempt it last.

Items come from one of two machines. Machine \(1\) is used for a fraction \(0.6\) of production and machine \(2\) for \(0.4\). The number of surface defects \(X\) on an item depends on which machine made it:

\(X = 0\) \(X = 1\) \(X = 2\)
\(\mathbb{P}(X = \cdot \mid Z = 1)\) 0.7 0.2 0.1
\(\mathbb{P}(X = \cdot \mid Z = 2)\) 0.2 0.3 0.5

You observe only \(X\). The machine label \(Z \in \{1, 2\}\) is not observed.

  1. (2) Using the factorisation \(p(z, x) = p(z)\,p(x \mid z)\), write out the full \(2 \times 3\) table of joint probabilities. Check that it sums to \(1\).

  2. (2) Compute the marginal \(\mathbb{P}(X = x)\) for each \(x\). Which variable did you sum out? Why is it reasonable to call it a nuisance variable here, and what has been irretrievably lost by summing it out?

  3. (3) Compute the posterior \(\mathbb{P}(Z = 1 \mid X = x)\) for each of \(x = 0, 1, 2\). Write down Bayes’ rule in the form you are using it, and identify each factor as the prior, the likelihood, or the evidence.

  4. (2) Compute the posterior mean \(\mathbb{E}[Z \mid X = x]\) for each \(x\). Notice that it is not always one of the two possible values of \(Z\) — explain what such a value means and why it is not a contradiction.

  5. (1) Verify numerically that

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\,\mathbb{E}[Z \mid X]\,\big] \;=\; \mathbb{E}[Z]. \]

State in words what this identity says about averaging.

Why this problem is here

Parts (c) and (d) are, in miniature, exactly what a diffusion model’s denoiser computes: a posterior over a hidden quantity given a noisy observation, and then its mean. You will meet the continuous version as \(\mathbb{E}[x_0 \mid x_\sigma]\) in The World’s Smallest Diffusion Model, where the same two lines of algebra produce a \(\tanh\).
